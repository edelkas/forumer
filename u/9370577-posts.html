I personally think it would be neat to have more specific categories at NUMA. I'd like to see:

1) Collection. I still think this was a good genre identifier. It's a map in with many switches which you can 'collect' in any order, which can make a good theme for a map.

2) Stealth. Usually involves hiding from chainguns and lazer drones, who move in cleverly enforced paths.

3) Utility. A rare class of maps such as my 'Solid' and TDL's 'Checkpoints'

4) Jumper. Nothing to do with ogmo, just maps where you must do clever jumps, usually with just mines, such as JUMP!, or just tiles and less harmful objects, such as Lucidium's trademark style of jump puzzles.

5) Climber. We all know what these are.

Obviously nothing is clear cut. Most jumpers have puzzles running through them (you must work out just how to jump which is a puzzle). Even a standard action level is a puzzle to some extent, since you need the right strategy usually to avoid being blown to pieces, but I think opening the genres up will make it easier to find something you're looking for.

IN ADDITION to this, I also think having a secondary rating system, for LEVEL DIFFICULTY, would be an excellent addition to NUMA. People could rate maps on a scale of difficulty, and sort maps by difficulty, or find maps of a specific difficulty level. The ratings could work as follows (I think it's important that people have a guideline such as this, as the numbers become arbitary).

0) No difficulty. A beginner is 99% guaranteed to beat it first time. Think episode 0.
1) Easy. Think 'bamboo forest' and that ballpark
2) Easy-Intermediate. Average player will take a few goes to beat it.
3) Intermediate. Middle ground difficulty, like the bulk of Metanet levels.
4) Hard. Difficult but still possible for the average player.
5) Expert. Only veterans will stand a chance of finishing.

By letting people find the difficulty levels they want, there might be less bitter feelings and map markdowns of maps which are too hard/easy etc... Just a thought

Yeah I was going to say, always go through your E blocks and look for ways to add other tiles into them. You can chip bits away (to make it look more rugged, or to make symmetrical dips here and there), smooth edges (with either a rounded tile, or a curve made of triangle tiles), add notches with 5 tiles, and plenty more.

My creative process is just... Well I just stare at the screen and ideas come to me. Or I get ideas when I'm doing something else. I've been doing it for years and as I'm also a composer/artist, I've kinda trained my mind to be creative and think obliquely.

But the best way to think about level design, is to think of finding the 'sweet spots' in the game engine, and working on those. For example a sweet spot in N is the way the ninja interacts with the tiles, so good tile placement is one of the things that makes a great N level. Also, for some reason, most shapes that look interesting, are also fun to play on (or at least novel).

As far as objects goes they are usually placed after the tiles, but not always. Some levels that depend heavily on objects (eg, thwumps which may need to be used in a certain way), I build the objects alongside the tiles and modify both in a back-and-fourth manner.

Lastly, if you're stuck for ideas, copy. It's the oldest trick in the book. Just make a level 'based on' a level you really like on NUMA or N, and you should get some new ideas on the way. If you don't, you've got yourself a decent, but very unoriginal, map.

One thing I am kinda learning from you guys is that there and backs aren't that popular.

A 'there and back' as it's name implies, is when you have to traverse a level in some way and then return or backtrack to get to the exit - usually playing the level in reverse if it's a linear (or mostly linear) kind of level.

The plus side of there and back is that it can create tension, and it can be truly interesting to see how a linear style course plays in both directions. A good example is my map 'fetch', that was recieved well and was a fairly long but easy there and back.

Anyway, the point is, I really don't think I'm going to be making any more tough there and backs... I think it only really works if one of two things is true: 1) The level is linear but easy and works really well on the return journey (adding interesting twists), or 2) the level is totally nonlinear, so that the 'back' journey can be taken via a number of routes.

What do you guys think?

Sendy's Level Structure and Concept Manifesto (first draft)

In this guide I hope to give some general ideas and pointers to the factors which come into play in the overall structure of a level. Being aware of the spacetime topology of your level as you build it, and how that influences it's gameplay, is an essential design skill. If this guide gives just one person a small bit of inspiration, I will consider my job done.

Linearity vs. Nonlinearity[/u]

This is an age-old debate which has been running since the early 80's. As soon as the idea of coded levels was invented (as opposed to repeating stages that got faster or added more aliens, a la Space Invaders, Asteroids, etc), the obvious question was "do you force a fairly limited path for the player to follow, or do you give them multiple routes to explore?".

The generalized answer, is, of course, that either way can work, and there have been classic games and levels in both of these modes. Each mode has it's pros and cons, and I believe a good level designer should master both.

Level design in N is unlike any other platform game I've ever designed for, in that the movement in N is, by it's very nature, very open-ended anyway. All but the most strictly linear (and we're talking enclosed tunnels forcing the route, here) level will have a gamut of ways it can be approached. Some people may walljump and leap over a certain part. Others may use the bounceblock, and others still will do a triple-reverse corner kick (if indeed there is such a thing) off of a moving thwump and catapult themselves right to the end of your level. So, controlling the linearity factor involves being aware of the diverse range of moves available to the player. Try and watch as many demos as possible - experts and beginners and everyone inbetween.

The crux of this message is - look out for shortcuts. If you've designed a puzzle or a timing challenge which is the centerpiece of your level, the chances are you don't want people skipping right over it. But the process doesn't stop there. Sometimes these alternative routes are desirable and add depth to the map. An experienced mapmaker will have a trained eye to spot the different potential routes, how easy they are to take, and hence will position gold and impassible barriers (mine barricades, walls, one-ways) to shape the level into a dynamic affair with little to no trivial design features. A well-designed shortcut may allow a player to skip some of the level, but at the cost of being very tricky to do and/or not being able to get some gold. You get the idea.

Now you know the special-case rules for N (or have a vague idea of them), you can make a decision on the level of linearity you'd like in your level.

Linear levels:

Linear levels have a sense of progression and composition to them which is satisfying in it's-self. Usually, the player is forced to pass all of the challeneges in sequence and therefore you may get more use out of your object placement. Furthermore, in a decent linear course, there is a microcosm of sub-routes the ninja can take, and so we see that a good linear level is not linear at all, apart from in appearance and basic concept.

Making linear levels is generally (much!) easier than making nonlinear ones, because there are less possibilities for player routing and hence outcome. Effectively it's easier to manage the time dimension of a linear level. However, when using linearity you should be very aware of the difficulty contour you are creating. This is the hardest part of making a good linear map - getting the balance between thrilling and frustrating the player. If you make a very hard part, or a part that requires a skill that the player doesn't have, they have no choice but to pass through it, which could lead to problems and the player quitting.

A good strategy is to put more difficult challenges at the beginning, and have challenge peter out towards the end as excitement at beating the level mounts. Obviously, though, sticking to this plan would become predictable and samey, so I use it only when I'm more unsure of my design intentions.

Nonlinear levels:

The possibility for these kinds of levels in N is immense, due to the complexity and intricacy of the ninja's movements and interactions with terrain and objects. Anyone who's made a DDA knows how the tiniest detail in timing and motion can affect the ninja's trajectory. Nonlinear levels are less frustrating and generally more fun. You feel free, master of your own destiny as you hop around the level accepting and avoiding challenges as your skill permits.

Designing a good, fluid nonlinear level in N takes some skill and familiarity, since you need to consider each area of the map and what direction the player may enter it from, not to mention which objects may be in which state of play at that point in time - for example : a thwump might have thwumped and be slinking back into place, a rocket may have fired and followed the player into this part of a map, the player may or may not have triggered switch or trapdoor x, y, or z, a stray seeker drone may be complicating matters. All of these need to be considered when balancing the difficulty of a nonlinear map.

But don't let that put you off.

One tool you have at your disposal is forcing a certain amount of linearity. Your map may consist of four seperate challenges in isolation which can be completed in any order. There may be several sub-sections to your otherwise nonlinear map which are linear and even are one-way systems (enforced by one-ways, doors, or even mines). This will give your level structure as well as making the time dimension of your map easier to manage. Breaking your map down into hermetic sections sealed with normal doors or easy 'safe zones' which allow the ninja to shake off any following dangers such as rockets or drones, before moving onto the next section, may also help.

Things which can provide extra player routing are:

- Sloped floors (especially the gently sloped '3' tiles) allow the ninja to jump much higher - providing access to any routes above. Other types of slopes may be used for double-jumping off of and reaching much further than a basic jump. Be aware how tiles guide the ninja around the map in various types of flight.
- Thwumps can act as lifts and moving platforms to distant parts of the map.
- Doors can be opened (or not) if a switch has been triggered. How difficult is the switch to trigger? What are the rewards for the player? How difficult/rewarding is the route opened by the switch? Is this route essential to completion of the level?
- Bounceblocks can provide the player with exceptionally long and high jumps. They can also be jumped off of from the side (walljumped).
- Launchpads are kindof obvious
- There are others, and many combinations.

Nonlinearity is by it's very nature highly complicated and is a more advanced subject of map-making. In writing I can only scratch the surface and perhaps give you some ideas. The true mastery can only come through nonverbal understanding of how it works, and lots of experience in building maps and playing yours and other people's maps (good and bad). In the past I have made many attempts at making good, open-ended maps, and failed. Only after about two years mapping experience do I feel comfortable with the challenge.

There-and-Backs[/u]

Touted by some (and often correctly) as the bain of N, 'there-and-backs' are situations where the ninja starts at or near the exit, and the exit switch is at the end of an (often quite long-ish) course of any type.

The there-and-back's cheif benefit is psychological. There's nothing quite like the sense of venturing out into the danger and then returning 'home'. Many or all of the obstacles will need to be crossed twice, once in each direction. This also opens an interesting design challenge - making your level dynamic and right in both directions.

Done right, a there-and-back is an adrenaline pumping rush. Done wrong, or too often, and it'll have you reaching for your NES emulator faster than you can say "It's-a-me, Mario!". And as I see it, the line between good and bad is quite distinct.

First there is the difficulty: Because there-and-backs are generally longer than most levels, you need to remember to lower the difficulty to compensate. Going 'there and back' through a hail of bullets, rockets and lazer fire is more soul-destroying than anything else. Typically I find going 'back' should be easier than going 'there', though by no means is this always the case (consider the infamous but lovable Trench Run from episode 5 of N). That way the hard work is got out of the way first, and as you return the difficulty is provided by the players nerves, knowing she must now get 'home' and with one mistake she'll be right back to the start.

You can make the return trip easier with clever positioning of level elements, but sometimes that's not enough. To further aid this cause, you can try adding advantages on the way back such as short-cuts past hard bits, jump-pads to aid vertical climbs which were drops on the first leg, or platforms made of trap-doors which appear over difficult jumps, neutralizing them for the return journey. As a last resort, consider closing some turrets off with trap-doors. Sometimes this looks very tacky but occasionally it works for the good of the level. Note that when you do this, it's neat-looking to put the turret in a 'notch' in the wall which you can seal off with one or two trapdoors, as using multiple trapdoors can often look even tackier.

The second thing to consider is what the there and back gives a level. Since almost any level can be a there-and-back with minimal editing, there needs to be a good reason. It might enhance the mood of the level. For example, you may start above ground and need to descend into some caves, get the switch, and then spelunk your way back to the tunnel mouth to the exit on the surface. Another example is 'infiltrating' a building and escaping. Providing an open choice of routes for both legs of the journey can reduce the linearity and severity of the there-and-back. A player can take one route there, and the other coming back, and try different routes to find the one that suits his style and abilities the most. Bear in mind, though, that as the nonlinearity increases, it loses it's identity as a there-and-back. An open-styled map with the exit and start near the bottom and a switch up top is hardly what I would term as a there-and-back.

The level might be very easy and thus need this extra challenge. The jumps might work very differently in one direction to the other, providing a technical showcase. Make sure there is a good reason for the there-and-back because I for one can vouch for the reduncancy of adding it into levels willy-nilly.

Also, there is the 'round trip' there-and-back, where the 'back' leg of the journey uses a completely different route to the 'there'. The order may be enforced with switches or one-ways, or left open to the player to decide. This variant doesn't have quite the same psychological connotations as the straight there-and-back, but can sometimes be more suitable to the level you want to create.

Finally there are there-and-back-and-theres, or there-and-back-and-there-and-backs, ad nauseam, which are made by nesting doors and switches in a certain way. The golden rule is that these should be avoided AT ALL COSTS unless the level is trivially short and will evolve (such as drones changing phase or a slowly drifting lazer drone).

Have a go at making maps with some of these strategies. Done right, they are one of my favourite kinds of map.

To be continued...

If anyone knows of any other archetypal level structures (like there-and-backs) which could be added to this document, please let me know.

#465993 54 2008-08-31T17:10:43Z Sendy
I actually suggested having the enemy speeds/reaction times etc as variables a long long time ago, and the idea was shot down. So it's interesting to see how people have embraced these ideas.

Personally, I like most of what's in Nreality. The new drones are great, IMO, but should be used sparingly. Player mods would look out of place in a column, unless you used them for the whole column (like a low gravity column or whatever), or for novelty maps.

I despise image maps with a passion. Every one I've played, the image has just gotten in the way of the gameplay. Graphics before gameplay is a big no-no.

Object-mods are great, IMO. Once you've seen the modded enemy and how it reacts, you know what you're in for in that level. It takes away the feeling of 'perfect information' about the level that you get in N, but in games where you get infinite lives, a certain amount of tasteful trial-and-error is often part and parcel of the game. Some of the weirder behaviours may seem out of place but this is all a matter of designer discretion.

Personally I'd only use mods and objects in a way that would seem logical in a professional level design, unless I was larking about or making an experimental/novelty map. But then I'm perfect like that.
