#121836 3 2005-12-10T18:06:58Z Raigan
hm.. i can see what you mean.

off the top of my head, all i can think of is to figure out a way to precalculate the center of each polygon.

one method is the "centroid" of each polygon (just sum the vertex positions then divide the x and y components by the number of vertices) and use that as the "center" of the polygon.. another method would be to fit a bounding circle or box on the polygon, then use the center of the circle/box as the center point. since you're using quads you could also intersect the two diagonals of each quad and call the intersection point "the center".. i'm sure there are many more ways, since (as you noted) the "center" of a polygon isn't a really well-defined term.


i recommend the book "geometric tools for computer graphics", which has a lot of useful info, including stuff on SAT. the approach they recommend for two 2D polys is based on the following idea: if you're testing normal n from poly A (n is perp to the edge formed by verts a0 and a1), you only need to project poly B onto the axis and see if it goes "past" a0 or a1 ("past" meaning "futher in the direction -n").

this may not be clear, but try doing it on paper and hopefully it will become clear: since the polygons are convex, in the above case you know (due to the definition of convexity) that no vertices of A will be "in front of" v0 or v1 ("in front of" meaning "further in the direction n").

..now that i think about it, you would then just need to find the vertex of B which is furthest in the direction opposite the normal: this is the vertex which is most penetrating n. but it seems like you're now no longer using SAT since you're not really projecting 2D shapes onto 1D intervals..

the concept of finding the point on a polygon which is "furthest" in a given direction is (i think) called the support mapping, and it used in a different collision detection approach which gino van den bergen has written a book about.

#130541 22 2006-01-07T19:20:24Z Raigan
hi,
Over the past year, 2 or 3 other teams have written us to propose something similar (an N sequel/remake).

Our position on this is: we can't give you permission to use the name, graphics, characters, story, etc. in your game. We intend to develop N in the future and need to remain in control of the IP -- and for instance, a condition of most Canadian grants is that the "chain of title documentation" be 100% controlled by whoever's applying for the grant.

Everyone is free to make a game "inspired by" N -- in the credits to N we've listed several games which influenced us quite a bit when developing N; however, N remains distinct from each of them.

Since we can't give permission to use our IP, we encourage developers to similarly find a way to make their project their own original creation, even when it borrows from or is influenced by other games (such as N). It's not much harder to do, and it means that the resulting ideas will fit much better with the game itself, since you'll have total control over them and will be able to change things to maximize the fun -- you're not locked into anything.


One distinction we'd also like to make is the use of our IP in other fan projects such as comics, animations, etc.. this is fine, since it's work that exists in a different sphere than the game itself. A useful analogy in terms of music: there's a huge difference between recording a cover version of "Yesterday", and painting a picture inspired by the song "Yesterday".

Also, we love knowing about N-related projects, so please email us to show us your N-related stuff; when we update our site we'll be updating the art section!

thanks,
Mare and Raigan

we're not trying to discourage people from being creative, our main point was that we reserve the right to make "N" games. some other fans have made games based on N, but they weren't the same sort of game as N.. you could tell they stood on their own. but once you use the graphics AND the gameplay, it just infringes a bit -- what if we wanted to release a 3D version of N in the future? hypothetical, but we'd just prefer to keep our options open!

#187303 4 2006-06-10T17:52:40Z Raigan
this is a hard problem to solve, since you really want to find the global solution to many-things-colliding-at-once, but realistically you can only solve them pairwise.

in N we never really fixed this problem, however for the new game we're trying to handle it. i'd recommend running several iterations of collision -- things will be slower, but that's really the only way to move towards the global solution using only pairwise operations.

another trick which might help is to build a "contact graph" each frame, which describes how close to the ground/unmoveable world each object is. if you google "shock propagation" you'll find some papers. BUT: this is quite complicated, probably more than necessary.

we've found that a simpler solution (a dumbed-down version of the real solution) is to reorder collision:
-collide all objects vs. the world (i.e vs any objects that can't be moved/have infinite mass); for each collision, keep track of the collision normal (i.e the direction you pushed the object out of collision)
-collide all objects vs. each other as usual, BUT: if either of the objects collided with the world, don't let it move at all in the world-collision-normal direction -- it should behave as if it's infinitely massive if pushed in that direction (i.e into the world). so, the other object should move instead.

of course, since this is sort of a hacky solution, there are still problems sometimes, for instance when two objects touch the world but push each other into the world.. still, it helps a lot.. it should certainly help the standing-on-boxes case.


finally, i'm not sure that our projection method is the best way to respond to collisions; we may actually end up rewriting all of our physics code in the new game with stuff based on erin catto's awesomely simple impulse-based method. you can use the same collision detection code as in our tutorials, you just need to change the response: http://www.gphysics.com/?page_id=16

unfortunately triangles aren't as nice as boxes, since you have to test vs. each edge individually (and thus you're really just testing linesegs). boxes are nice since you can test two edges with one test due to the parallel edges.

anyway, if you have a circle c and a linesegment p0->p1, one approach is:

1) find the point on the linesegment that's closest to the circle
2a) if the point is an endpoint, perform a circle-vs-circle test between the circle and that endpoint (the endpoint can be considered a 0-radius circle, there should be lots of resources online for circle-vs-circle if you don't have that already)
2b) otherwise, perform a circle-vs-line test


briefly:

1)
d = unit direction vector that points from p0 to p1 (you can normalize the vector p1-p0 to get this, or use atan, cos and sin)

v = c - p0;//v is the vector from the segment's start to the circle center
dp = dotproduct of v and d

if(dp < 0): c is "behind" p0, the starting point of the seg, so p0 is the closest point (circle-vs-circle test)
else if(dp > segment's length): c is "past" p1, the ending point of the seg, so p1 is the closest point (circle-vs-circle test)
else: the closest point on the linesegment isn't an endpoint. perform a circle-vs-line test (the position of the actual closest point is p0 + dp*(p1-p0))

there are almost certainly better or different ways to do this, hopefully the above will help.

the seperating axis theorem really isn't great for triangles, and it can't handle circles at all really, so you're better off with a closest-point/minimum-distance approach like this.

also, you should really read about voronoi regions because they're AMAZINGLY useful. for instance, the above solution detemines if the circle is in the voronoi region of an endpoint, or the segment. VRs are just regions of space in which all points in that region are closest to some point or edge of a shape.. so if you want to find the closest point/edge, you just need to figure out which region you're in. it's like magic!

this is indeed a problem, which was sort of glossed-over by the turorials. in fact, the situation on the right occurs in the game, and it my most-hated bug.

to start with, you absolutely should always be updating the object position after collision with each tile. this is how this sort of method works.. you're finding the global solution (move object out of all tiles) by interatively finding local solutions (move object out of tile X).. the assumption being that finding the global solution all at once is hard/impossible, and that repeatedly applying local solutions will converge to the global solution.

however, this won't totally fix things since the result depends on the order in which you test tiles (testing vs tile2 first will work; testing vs tile1 will result in hitting the invisible wall). still, it works well enough in general, aside from the problem you're having. if you look in tutorial B, you'll see that we first find the 4 tiles an object overlaps with, and then process the collisions in a specific order.. this was a "rule-based method" (i.e hack) to fix some of the problems with naively processing collisions one-by-one.


the _real_ solution is to switch from a convex-chunk-based method (i.e a set of tiles that describe the area of the world) to a surface-based method (i.e a set of linesegs that describe the surface of the world).. with tiles you don't have an explicit representation of the world's surface, which sucks when what you want to do is push something onto the surface of the world.

in fact, we retro-fitted a surface-based engine into the tile-based code as part of r&d for our new game.. however, that's a big and rather painful change, and probably not necessary to get results that are "good enough".


in N, we fixed most of these problems by using edge-flags to approximate the world's surface.. tutorial B explains this, looking through the source code will hopefully make things more clear. it's a bit hacky.. this really only fixes things when the surface is axis-aligned, which is why we still have problems with slopes.

i'd love to know how other people handle this, because all the literature says to handle concave shapes by splitting them into several convex chunks, and colliding vs each chunk individually (which is what tile-based collision does).. but noone ever mentions your problem. but they MUST be solving it somehow..


the problem pretty much boils down to: you need to add extra information to the algorithm, because otherwise the algorithm will only know about tiles individually.


one idea that's just occurred to me is this: if you look at both your cases, it is the case that the solution you desire is also the single projection which moves the shape out of collision with BOTH shapes at once. for A that's vertical projection out of tile1, and for B it's projection out of either of the tiles' diagonal faces.

so, a simple algorithm would be something like:
(assuming we already know obj is colliding with tiles t0 and t1)
-for(each axis in t0)
-remember original position of obj
-push obj out of the tile along that axis
-test if obj is colliding with t1
-if so, we didn't find a solution; restore obj's position and continue
-if not, you found the solution
-repeat the above for each axis of t1
-if we found a solution, good (respond by altering object velocity/etc); if not, fall back to colliding vs each tile individually.

anyway, i don't know if this will work, but i suspect that there should be some rule-based game-specific solution that will patch up the problems "well enough", and will be much easier than totally changing your collision system.

one thing specific to your game would be to try considering _two_ segments at once, instead of one-by-one.. so, you'll have to write code that projects a point out of a "V" shape. we actually did something like this as a test a couple years ago..


for convex Vs, just find the penetration with each side and push out of the smallest one (or, push out of whatever side the rest of the box is on, or whatever). if the point isn't penetrating one of the segs, it's not penetrating either.


for concave Vs, it's slightly trickier. first, test if the point is "behind" the lines that contain the linesegs (it's behind if the dotprod of the line normal and the point-on-line->test-point vector is -ve). if it's not behind either, no hit. if it's behind one but not the other, just project it out of that one. if it's behind both, things get slightly more complicated: you'll either have to push it out of one of the segs, OR push it to the point where the segs intersect.

if you understand about voronoi regions you can usee them to decide what to do in this case: if you're in the VR of the point where the segs meet, then project out to the point, otherwise you're in the VR of one of the segs -- project out of that seg.

hopefully this makes sense or at least gives you some ideas. mainly, i don't think you can't solve this problem if you only consider each segment in isolation. i really wish i knew better resources or more theory about this sort of thing, because it's something that happens all the time (i.e you have to add extra information in order to represent something you want).

you can even extend this idea to handle points with size (i.e circles).

Or, another way of looking at it is that if you placed a solid lineseg on the edge, you'd still need to define which side of the lineseg was "outside" and which was "inside".

The convention we established was for a cell's edges to have their surface normals pointing towards the center of the cell. For each pair of identical edges, the one that belongs to the cell whose tile is solid along the edge is turned off (because its normal points in towards the full/solid region), while the one that belongs to the cell that's empty along the edge is turned on (because its normal points out towards the empty space).

Doors are "double-sided" edges, both edges are solid.

This is one of the many reasons why we encourage people to take our ideas and implement them themselves -- our methods aren't the best.

A much better solution would be to store edges in a seperate grid, or to find a different way to represent the surface of the world. Unfortunately it'll take a while, but when we're done robotology we're going to write about our new method, which is much, much, much simpler, faster, and each to implement. Basically, the best way to represent the surface of the world is to use arbitrary linesegs and arcs, and only use "tiles" in the editor.

hi,
This is a fundamental problem with any sort of tile-based collision, which is: you're only solving the problem on a tile-by-tile basis, not for all tiles at once. the reason is that solving for all tiles at once is _much_ harder.

To answer your follow-up question, there is no simple way to add (or otherwise "munge") individual penetration info from different tiles to get a single vector that solves them all together. We spent quite a while about a year ago trying to figure out if this was possible, and concluded that it just isn't -- you have to solve the all-at-once problem for real if you want a good solution.

One approach that might be very useful is to look at the problem from a different point of view: instead of circle-vs-world, think of this as point-vs-inflated-world: shrink your circle from radius = r to radius = 0 (i.e a point), and expand/inflate the world by r. This is an equivalent problem (i.e a vector that moves the point to the surface of the inflated world will also move the circle to the surface of the non-inflated world), but it's a bit easier to see what you're actually trying to do.

I'll attach a picture of what i mean -- in the picture, the actual surface of the world is grey, and the inflated surface is black.

If you sketch out these sort of things you'll find a pattern:
1) if your circle is penetrating only one edge of the world (a line or a circular arc), it should be pushed out of that edge along the edge's normal, by the amount of penetration (i.e this is what the tutorial code does)
2) if your circle is penetrating _two_ edges, then it should be pushed out to the point where the two edge's _inflated_ versions intersect.

the problem with (2) is that it's still only a local solution, i.e it will fail in cases where the circle penetrates more than 2 edges at once (in such cases there really 0 solutions, because the 3 or more inflated edges may not all intersect in a single point), or where the two edges' inflated surfaces never intersect (i.e if they're parallel).

We spent some time trying to explicitly generate the inflated-intersection points, etc, but in the end it was much faster to calculate the inflated surface approximately, by using the "repeatedly push the circle out of a single edge" method.

I think there may be a bug in your code -- it's true that using the tutorial method the projection direction may "jump" from one face to another, but it should never push the object down into the tile -- the object should be pushed _up_ by m.

The overlap/penetration on a given axis doesn't have a sign/direction, so for a given overlap there are two potential directions to project: +n or -n, where n is the axis vector. There are several ways to choose which direction to push, one being to project the objcenter->objcenter vector onto n and see which way that points.

Another option is to rearrange your axis tests so that the direction info isn't lost; the only reason you have an ambiguous +n/-n case is that you're calculating the unsigned overlap along the n axis -- instead you could calculate the _signed_ overlap along +n and -n seperately. This will result in more axis tests (i.e objects with parallel faces can't "share" axis tests) but may help simplify things for you.

It's funny, everyone seems to act like good collision detection is simple, but in our experience it's much harder to do than physics!

For your last problem: I suggest moving away from tile-based collision and using geometry (lists of edges). You can still use tiles in the editor, but when you load the level you convert them into the corresponding surface edges -- so that in this case the dark grey tile wouldn't contribute any edges at all. Correctly generating the edges can be a bit tricky, but in the long run it pays off.

For your first problem: we also came across this. The basic problem is that (as usual) your code isn't looking at the "big picture", it's finding a local solution which is no good. You can actually detect when this is happening, see the attached picture -- in the lower case, the intersection point lies _outside_ of the edges that generated it. However, once you've detected this it's hard to say how to fix it -- you could use this as a hint to look at vertex-edge intersection instead of edge-edge perhaps.


Finally, our experience is that you're never going to get a system that is perfect -- you'll always be able to dream up a case which breaks it, and that can be frustrating. But, a better solution is to realize when you're doing this, and attack the problem from a different direction: try to figure out a way to prevent that case from ever happening.. such as clamping the max speed of objects, running collision detection several times per frame (so that things don't move very far in a single step), etc. For instance, in Robotology we were having problems getting very thin/acute corners to behave well; after about a week, we simply said: screw it, we just won't allow very thin/acute corners. Problem solved

We actually gave up on these analytical methods and used a different approach: we spawn a "proxy" circle at the circle's position, then iteratively push the proxy out of collision with all nearby edges, one-by-one. Then, the penetration vector is the vector from the circle's position to the proxy's position. This isn't an exact solution, and can fail, but it's simple.

You're correct that the cross product in 3D becomes the "perp" operator in 2D. I think your problem is the calculation of the partial derivatives -- you have to take the derivative of the constraint function w.r.t _each_ particle involved.

So, if you have the particles p1,p2 which form the "stick" you're colliding against, and the particle p that's colliding against the stick, your constraint function is:

C(p,p1,p2) = Dot((p - p1), Perp(p2 - p1)/||p2-p1||)

I suck at vector calculus so I just expand everything to scalars:
C(px,py, p1x,p1y, p2x,p2y) = ( (px - p1x)*-(p2y-p1y) + (py - p1y)*(p2x - p1x) ) / √((p2x-p1x)**** + (p2y-p1y)****)

to make things easier to read, let len = √((p2x-p1x)**** + (p2y-p1y)****)

dC/dpx = -(p2y - p1y) / len
dC/dpy = (p2x - p1y) / len

#352382 2 2007-08-07T16:05:55Z Raigan
The trick with VRs is that they're almost always "implicit" in the code, i.e you should never have to actually build the lines that define them.

For convex polygon vs. circle, you just need lots of lineseg-vs-point tests, with a bit of added logic (WARNING: this might not be the fastest method, it's just the one we're familiar with).

Let's start with circle-vs-lineseg: p is circle's pos, lineseg is a->b
The lineseg's VR is a rectangular strip, defined by 2 lines (one passing through each of a,b, both perpendicular to the lineseg).

v = b - a //v is the vector from the lineseg 's start vertex to its end vertex
d = p - a //d is the vector from the lineseg's first vertex to the circle center

len2 = Dot(v,v) //len2 is the squared-length of the lineseg
dp = Dot(d,v)

if(dp < 0) CASE 1
else if(dp > len2) CASE 2
the circle is outside of the lineseg's VR on the side of a (case 1) or b (case 2); this means the closest point on the lineseg is a (case 1) or b (case 2), if you were doing lineseg-vs-circle you'd test p vs a (case 1) or b (case 2), using a circle-vs-point test, aka simple distance test.

else:
the circle is inside the lineseg's VR; if you were doing lineseg-vs-circle, you'd want to find the perpendicular distance from p to the lineseg, i.e by looking at np = Dot(d, Normalize(Perp(v))) to measure the position of p along the lineseg's normal.


The only difference moving to polygon-vs-circle is that the linesegs are connected, and "sided" (i.e one side of the lineseg is "inside" the polygon, the other is "outside"). Let's say your polygon is defined by vertices v0 to vN, which are in counter-clockwise order.. you need to test your circle (call its position p) vs the edges v0->v1, v1->v2, etc.

Whenever you hit case 1 or 2, you'd want to test vs the neighboring edge. Basically, the voronoi region of vertex v[i] is a wedge-shaped region located between the rectangular regions of the CW/prev (v[i-1]->v[i]) and CCW/next (v[i]->v[i+1]) linesegs. So, if you test p vs v[i-1]->v[i] and find case 2, and then test p vs v[i]->v[i+1] and find case 1, that means p is in v[i]'s VR, and you should test p vs v[i] using a simple distance/circle-vs-point test.

How to best implement this so that you don't do the same tests more than once is tricky.. but it probably doesn't matter, don't get fancy until you know for a fact that this is the bottleneck of your program. Another thing to note is that if p is "outside" (np > 0) _any_ of the edges, it's definitely outside the polygon, while if it's "inside" (np < 0) _all_ of the edges, it's definitely inside the polygon.

hope this helps,

if you're using boxes there are shortcuts; also, if your polygons are quite complex (many vertices) you could consider NOT testing against all of them, and instead using some sort of binary search to test in O(logN) time.. you're looking for the closest point on the polygon, and you can exploit the fact that the polygon is convex (and the ordering of the vertex list).. sadly i can't find a good reference for this, but books such as "real-time collision detection" and/or "computational geometry in C" should have something about logN searches involving convex shapes.

however there will be a point at which this is a waste of time (i.e if you have <6 verts per poly, it's probably best to just test all the points).

you could also use bounding boxes and/or some sort of bounding-volume-hierarchy if you have really large polys.. but again, it's best to just to the braindead thing until it becomes a problem.

finally, you could try "coherence" to speed things up; once you've found the closest vertex/edge on the polygon, you can cache it and use it next frame -- chances are that the closest point hasn't changed much, so you can start your search for the new closest point by looking at the verts/edges neighboring the previous closest point.. usually this saves you from testing vs everything.

hi,
you might have already seen this, but definitely the best place to start is to try the original stick-based method of Jakobsen (see section 5, fig4a,4b here http://www.teknikus.dk/tj/gdc2001.htm)
this is probably your best bet, it's pretty simple and works well.

collision detection is much more involved, you'll always want to collide the end of one stick vs. a point on the other stick. to figure out which stick's end and where on the other stick, you can use sweep tests or static intersection tests.

you can sweep the sticks against each other (sweep each stick's endpoints vs the other stick's lineseg, see this thread http://bulletphysics.com/Bullet/phpBB3/vie....php?f=4&t=1041)

for static intersection tests, you want to intersect two linesegs. here's a page with an explanation of this: http://softsurfer.com/Archive/algorithm_01...orithm_0101.htm

one way to do this is to compare the endpoints of one stick vs the line containing the other stick. lets call your sticks sA=p0,p1, sB=p2,p3. if p0 and p1 lie on opposite sides of the line passing through p2,p3, AND p2,p3 lie on opposite sides of the line passing through p0,p1, the sticks are intersecting. try this on paper to see how it works. you can use the formula for the signed area of the triangles (p2,p3,p0),(p2,p3,p1),(p0,p1,p2),(p0,p1,p3) to figure out what points on on which sides.

i'm sorry for not providing more info, there's too much to say! i would suggest inflating the sticks into "capsules", and then use distance queries rather than intersection tests. really thin objects are hard to collide since it's so easy for them to spin through each other, and most of the time you don't really want to model objects that are that thin anyway.

If i remember correctly, the tiles are drawn in "retained mode" (i.e you create the graphics object once and Flash draws it each frame), and the moving object/ray/etc are drawn in "immediate mode" (you need to call a draw function each frame). Retained/immediate is just a metaphor for how things work, which parallels the similarly named usage idioms for openGL.

This should be a pretty simple thing to swap out, one way to do this would be to always call each object's .Draw() function each frame. If you want an object to use the retained-mode paradigm, you just need to make sure it constructs its graphics when it's created (via attachMovieClip() or similar), and then it can position/rotate/etc the movieclip inside its Draw() function. For immediate-mode objects, you can simply do nothing on creation and draw to a shared empty movieclip in the .Draw() function.

This sort of thing is definitely something we're still learning a lot about though, designing the structure of the program and how the various systems interact is a lot more involved than solving specific problems like collision!

well, it depends on how you look at it -- in a c++ game there are both layers at work, since the game code may be using a retained-mode approach (i.e load the 3D model at the start of the level, and then just change position/orientation each frame; the rendering system will draw the model appropriately) while the lower level rendering code _is_ drawing it each frame from scratch (i.e telling openGL to draw a bunch of triangles). you could just have the game code calling openGL, but it's usually a good idea to have an intermediate layer so that you can for instance change to directX without having to dig through your whole game.

for one thing, there are two parts to this -- representing it visually (drawing the shadowed regions), and representing it logically (being able to test if a point is in shadow).

for the visuals, you can use stencil shadows (as seen in gish); this is the same idea as doom3's shadows, except in 2d. if you google around you should be able to get the idea.

for logic, you don't need to explicitly construct the shape of the shadow/lit region -- you can just cast a ray from the query point/object to the player and see if it hits anything before the player. if so, it's in shadow, if not then it's visible.

this makes it more difficult if not impossible; stencil shadows are sort of a cheat since they let you implicitly calculate the shadowed area -- they're a process whereby you end up with the result you want, without having to EVER explicitly figure out the exact shape of the shadowed region. the basic algorithm is: find all the geometry near the lit region (cone shaped in this case), extrude geometry out to infinity in direction away from light, draw extruded geometry into stencil buffer, draw fullscreen shadow masked by stencil buffer (or, alternately draw the lit scene masked by the stencil). gish uses this for box shadows.

the only thing i can think of is to use a portal-rendering type approach, where you start with a single frustum (truncated pyramid in 3d, parallelogram in 2D) and recursively clip it against geometry. this will require you to split your levels into convex chunks, and is almost certainly going to be rather work-intensive. i can't seem to find any good tutorials on google, but there should be lots since this sort of method was used for rendering 3D games..

well, this is where it gets complicated

for one thing, there won't always just be the one contact point -- if an edge of the box is parallel to an edge of the tile, there will be two contact points (or, more correctly, a contact lineseg.. but this is approximated as two points, on on each end of the seg).

your idea of intersection can be simplified a bit using information you know from the axis tests; if you keep track of the edge+object that "generates" each axis, then at the end (when you have a single axis and penetration amount) you'll also have an edge on one of the objects -- you know that the contact point lies somewhere on this edge.

you then need to find the feature on the other shape which is "furthest" along the -ve axis.. this is hard to explain, basically if the penetration axis is from the box (and points outward from one of the box's edges) then you want to find the point (or edge) on the tile which is furthest "towards" the box.

this "furthest in a direction" idea is usually called "support mapping".. if it's a point, you can find the point on the other object's edge closest to it, and then you have the contact point on both shapes. if it's an edge, you need to clip the two edges together.. it's messy.

to be honest we've never bothered to implement this -- for one thing, if you don't need real boxes but just want some box-like objects, two circular particles connected by a stick can be very convincing


also, this can get a bit tricky with circular shapes!

mostly, once you move from particles to full rigid bodies, SAT in 2D becomes less simple and attractive. for special cases like box-box it can still be great (see box2d for some source), but to handle a broader range of cases without lots of complicated code, you might be better off looking at other algorithms such as GJK or XenoCollide.

one thing is that you're using weights (0.5,0.5) if none are dragged, and (0,2) if one is dragged -- you should probably use (0,1) for the dragged case, since you want the sum of the corrections to be 1.

what you're doing for the positions looks okay, but it doesn't make sense to apply the same correction to the velocities -- what you really want to do is remove the "inward" component of the balls' relative velocity.

It probably works okay, but what's actually going to happen is that the inward velocity will be gradually reduced over a period of frames instead of perfectly cancelled in one step.

To see why velocity should be treated independently of position, consider that two pairs of objects could be overlapping by identical amounts, but their relative velocities could be arbitrarily different (i.e one pair could be travelling inward at 10x the speed of the other pair). But, since your correction is purely based on position, both will receive the same amount of velocity correction -- which can't be right.

Then again, your way could potentially avoid some of the "bounce" problems that position-based solvers have: objects that overlap and are pushed apart gain outward momentum, and by not fully correcting the velocities, the remaining inward velocities would counteract the outward momentum.

#454234 2 2008-07-21T18:55:22Z Raigan
There's two parts to the wrapping -- the collision detection (figuring out how the rope should wrap), and the response (adding points/segments to the rope). And then there's also the reverse -- figuring out how to "uncollide" (remove points from the rope).

I'd really like to know how this works in Umihara, because it's on SNES so it must be simple! Our method is a bit complicated.. I'll try to quickly explain, but also it doesn't work great and we're still in the process of coming up with something better, so you might want to try your own thing since it's likely to be just as good.

What we do is add a second set of collision data that's only used by the rope; this data is a set of directed linesegs (think of them as arrows), where one end of the lineseg (the "head") is located on a convex vert of the world, and the other end (the "tail") is located inside the world somewhere -- this doesn't need to be precise, we usually just made the lineseg extend along the vert's negative normal for a decent distance.

To collide the rope, you just do a static yes/no intersection test between all of the rope's segments and all of the world's "arrows" -- if you find an intersection, you split the rope segment (add a point) and set that point to the head of the arrow. You then need to recursively check the newly created segments neighboring the new point for collision, etc..

You also need to note which direction the rope is wrapping around the point, CW or CCW (this is equivalent to the sign of the signed area of the triangle formed by a rope point and its neighboring points). You use this CW/CCW info during subsequent frames to test for "uncollision" -- if the rope wrapped around a point CW, and currently the rope is making a CCW turn at this point, you should remove the point.

This sort of idea doesn't handle moving shapes though (Umihara does though!), and it's a big pain -- whenever you add/remove a point from the rope you have to recurse over the rope checking for new collisions/uncollisions that may have been generated.

Our new method uses sweep tests and can handle arbitrary moving shapes, but it also has problems of its own.. it's actually the polar opposite of the above method in terms of pros/cons, so we're currently trying to figure out how to combine the two approaches.

Also, note that for that demo we just painted the rope collision data by hand, you'd ideally want to figure out how to generate this data from the tile shapes, however this isn't as easy as it may seem -- you can't just generate data for each tile, you need to correctly consider the global world shape (i.e two adjacent solid tiles should be considered a single shape)..

Hope this helps, if you come up with any interesting ideas please let us know! This is definitely a topic that's really interesting and also not really covered online, and Umihara proves that there's a fast, simple solution that deals with moving tiles.. but we haven't figured it out yet.
