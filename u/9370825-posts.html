Here's just some ideas I had while playing n. The likelihood of these being implemented are infinitesimally slim, I realize; if I post 'em maybe they'll go away and I can start thinking about new stuff. Maybe they'll do some good and inspire new ideas in someone else. I dunno.

1) Vector-based maps, instead of block-based. Give map creators the ability to create completely freeform maps using tools similar to the pen tool(s) in Illustrator, including a pathfinder for boolean operations to create complex shapes easily. For an example map, see attached image.
Why it won't be implemented: I'm going out on a limb and guessing one would have to rewrite n entirely to do this. That, and giving users increased ability to create maps gives them increased ability to create bad maps.

2) Wrap-around levels. Deleting the borders in ned open up those sides to create bigger levels. Removing the sides creates a "cylindrical" level, removing the bottom creates a bottomless pit, removing the top creates a sky.

3) Attaching "inert" objects to enemies. This would allow level designers to create moving landscapes, like the sliding platforms in Super Mario Bros. Likely combinations would be attaching a vertical wall of one-way platforms to a thwump (crushtastic!) or surrounding a zap drone (non-seeking) with one-way platforms.

4) "Duck Hunt" style 2 player action.
One of my fondest NES memories is playing Duck Hunt with my brother. At that time it was a somewhat of a secret that the on-screen ducks can be controlled with the 2p controller. Using certain manuevers, I rarely had more fun confounding my brother's attempts to shoot some skeet.
This would be a hoot in n. While "player 1" uses the keyboard to control the ninja, "player 2" would use the mouse to target missles and gauss turrets, clicking to trigger zap drones, floor drones and thwumps to dash.

I voted "To give your mind an excercise", and, please, allow me to expound on that a bit...

One of my most favorite gaming experiences is with Ninja Gaiden for the Xbox. That game redefines "difficult", but not because of the level of difficulty, but rather the curve. There's something magical in that game, in that the next level, or the next scene, is just barely beyond your skill level. The game will beat the daylights out of you, but it's fun enough to try again, and each time you get a little bit further. Eventually, after a long session, you reach an amazing level of mental focus. It's like the nerves in your hands and wrists are on fire, electric. I'm sure there are some gaming veterans out there that know what I mean.
IMHO, the thing that makes n worthwhile is that it does the same thing with so much less. Think about it, n only uses three buttons. Three. And with those three buttons, so much is possible. n acheives something that I really admire: elegance. It's fun, it's *truly* challenging, and it's simple. n reaches the pinnacle of gaming, and embodies something that many console and pc games have lost in the past couple of decades.

I've noticed that some of the best n maps use good "pathing". By pathing, I refer to the path that n follows as he chases down switches to access the exit. By good, I mean that these paths take the player all over the map, exposing him/her to new challenges, even when different parts of the path overlap. (This doesn't necessarily involve clever wall placement, but also includes well-placed jumps, dangers to avoid, etc. But I digress.)
So, this *is* the Custom Levels forum, how would one create a map with good pathing? I'm not here to answer that question but I am here to help. The attached file is a zip archive of gif's I've slapped together to help map makers "sketch" out rough outlines for their maps - including walls, objects, paths, switch connections, etc.
A bit of detail on these files. Each gif is created from one of the grids introduced in 1.4, and named for the key used to summon it. The file name also includes two denotations, CB and CP. CB stands for Center Block, and denotes grids in which the center crosshairs outline the center square in the grid. CP is Center Pixel, where the crosshairs are pixel-thin and show the center pixel of the map.
The Jump Arc guide doesn't show any grids, but is a rough *approximation* of the arc that n follows, if the player were to press the jump key and hold the left key all the while. To use it, place a printout of the jump arc guide underneath the printout of the grid you're using - the arc should show through and help plan whatever jumps you want to put in your map.
Hope this helps.
