The argument is that because no one was there to hear the tree, that no one can really know that there was a sound. This is all completely theoretical, because we know scientifically that it made a noise. So there are now two different paths. Scientific and Philosophical. Scientific deals with what we know to be true, based on what we have learned previously. This means that gravity exists in the unoccupied room next to you. You know gravity exists in your room, so it only makes sense it would also exist in the other room. The Philosophical path is that there is no proof of gravity in that room because you aren't in that room to know it. This doesn't make sense in the scientific path, and will only make sense if you define 'know' as a personal acknowledgment of being. So why is the Philosophical path wrong? Because if you accept that argument, any debate that ever exists is completely re-opened and unsolvable, under the basis that we don't 'know' what happened. The whole 'know' idea means that it I don't 'know' what is behind me, because I can't see it and thus don't 'know' it. It also means that I don't 'know' you exist because you are just text. It will then mean that I don't 'know' if I exist, because I can't prove that I 'know' otherwise. This all comes down to 'If you accept X, you must accept Y. If you accept Y, you must accept Z. If you accept Z, then.... (And should one ignore the 'if' in a debate, the opposition would call them out.) So pretty soon the debate is no longer a debate, because nothing is provable. Then the point arises; Why debate something when the argument will just be that nothing is provable? That accomplished nothing. The goal is to come to an agreement; to settle the debate. Finally, it is simplified into; The Philosophical path accomplishes nothing and is therefore unusable.

We are now left with the only other path; Scientific. Scientific path deals with what we know to be true in this world based on previous observations, and ignores the Philosophical definition of 'know'. In the Scientific path, you know that the room next to you have gravity because you have either been in there, or that you know the entire world has gravity because of it's mass. Scientific paths deal with what is known based on what is true in the world, ignoring other skewed meanings of 'know'.

So you see, it is incorrect to say that there is no sound from the tree because no one is around to know if there was a sound. The only possible thing you could argue is that sound is based off of perception of the ear. I strongly believe in the absoluteness of our universe. But there's simply no way to prove the existence of anything which you do not perceive directly. And it's just as hard to prove the existence of things you do perceive.

Knowledge is a weird thing. 99.9999% of the time, we need to assume the absoluteness of the world. But when a question like this one comes up, you have to remember that you're taking the epistemology of your environment for granted. To see the world as it is, we have to make ourselves naive. That naivete allows for science, knowledge, and discourse in other fields. Even the simplest of arguments takes something for granted and works upward from that foundation.

But it still doesn't allow for knowledge over something like this; a question which addresses the metaphysical, something which no one can know. Because there is no truth which can be built up from in this instance, there is no way to prove any events which are happening.



If asked as a scientific inquiry, surely the tree makes noise as it falls. But science makes an assumption about absoluteness.
