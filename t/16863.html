I read this book, House of the Scorpion, recently and it was about a world in the future where clones were harvested by scientists for their organs. The clones would grow until they reached maturity and their organs would be transplanted into the original as and when needed. (There's more to the plot, but it's unimportant. Read it to find out.)

We really aren't that far away from mastering genetics. Give it 25-30 years and we'll have cured just about every genetic disease. We will have mastered genetically altering fruit so that they taste better, last longer, contain less fat, etc. We might even have started to genetically modify humans based on preference. Would it be right to for a couple to go to a geneticist and alter their child? Could they influence his height, weight, intelligence, eye color, size of his dick ( ), etc? Do we, as humans, have a right to create our children? I mean, sure, no one would say fixing a fatal genetic problem such as Alzheimer's was unethical, but modifying humans is taking it a little too far. Your thoughts?

Imagine a generation where children were 'created'. Everyone would be bright, thin, tall, and would be socially acceptable. Everyone that could afford genetic manipulation, that is. What about the 'normal' kids that were created normally by parents who were against genetic manipulation, those too poor to afford a geneticist (it's going to be expensive, you just wait.), or those kids that were created by accident? Would they be 'left in the dust'? Would they have less of a chance for scholarships? Or would they have less chance of getting jobs? Would they be segregated against? For example, would an insurance company charge a higher rate for children who weren't genetically altered because they had a higher chance of doing something stupid?

Just because we can do something, doesn't mean we should. I can just see people spending millions of dollars to clone champion horses, award-winning dogs, etc. in the next decade. Comment, people. This is important.

#311703 6 2007-04-10T01:11:05Z Atilla
I think the whole idea is short-sighted and foolish. Natural variation plays an important part in the ongoing survival and development of a species and a culture. Take, for example, sickle-cell anemia, which is considered a burden in most countries, but a boon in regions rife with malaria, since it greatly increases resistance to the disease. Trying to create perfect people through genetic tampering also ignores the influence of the environment on human development - even with the "best" genes, there's no guarantee a child will turn out as planned. To make such alterations is merely chasing an unrealistic and unhealthy ideal based on a warped idea of a person's value.

This does bring up interesting ethical issues. I think that attempting to create "perfect" humans is silly, we'd create more problems than we'd solve, and it's a mess ethically. However, if genetic diseases such as Huntington's chorea, or Tay-sachs disease were curable, I'd be all for curing them. Of course, these are obvious genetic diseases, which do no one any good. Also, things like near-sightedness and other such problems, if curable, should be cured, because they provide no advantage, at least that I know of. I also think sickle-cell Anemia should be cured if curable, but only in populations safe from malaria.

I don't think that cosmetic genetic changes should be made, because this can lead to racial tension, and nazi-type ideals very quickly. If it must be done, cosmetic eugenics should be based upon a combination of the two parents' genes. This way, diversity is ensured, and no racial cleansing ever comes into play.

No one should be allowed to know the genetic information of another person, much like no one should be allowed to know another person's medical records. It is personal, and such knowledge could lead to genetic discrimination.
