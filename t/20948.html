001X - All-Purpose Writing Tips
For starters, remain consistent. No one likes having to try to figure out where the heck they are in a paper, story, etc. Remain in the same style, tense, and theme throughout the entire writing process, and it'll turn out quite a bit better. Also, check your grammar. I don't care how cool it looks to have the period on the outside of quoted text from your dragon-riding barbarian who has +2 to Charisma, making him one dashingly good looking ax-wielder. Don't do it! Also, don't be "flashy" with your writing. Keep it literate, and keep it legitimate.

002X - Basic Essay Writing
This is a small section on writing a basic 5-paragraph essay.
The one rule-of-thumb is that no paragraph may be smaller than 5 sentences, and may not have any two sentences start with the same word in the same paragraph, unless one sentence separates them.
  • The Introductory Paragraph - Start out with your thesis statement, opening statement, general thoughts on the subject, and/or you mission to accomplish with the next four paragraphs. Explain why you're writing this essay - not who is writing this essay. If they wanted to know who was writing it, they'd find out, or they'd read your autobiography. Keep the facts here, and present your ideas boldly, strongly, and clearly.
  • The First Body Paragraph - "But, I thought that other one was the first paragraph??"; WRONG. This where your essay really starts. Before you start writing, have three ideas in your head, clearly laid out. This is where you state your first idea or concept. It should slowly transition from your intro into the main idea, and it should almost have a "WOW!" factor to it. This is where you either hook or lose a reader.
  • Second Body Paragraph - "I like your bod(y)" is what readers should be thinking by now. Here is where your essay/writing venture really needs to shine. Bring out the second-meatiest guns you have, but leave the BFG in storage for now. State your second concept or idea, which should be relevant to the first idea in some way, and keep the words short, sweet, simple, and to the point. Here is where many readers ditch the paper/essay if it doesn't keep them hooked. You nab this paragraph, and they're pretty much yours.
  • Third Body Paragraph - "Mmm.. I want your bod(y)" is what the readers should be thinking by now. Delicious concepts in the last two, I'm sure; and a clear representation of facts. Continue this paragraph by staying on-topic, expressing your third idea/concept clearly, and also by slowly bringing your ideas to a near-close. Try to tie this in with your conclusion in some way.
  • Conclusion - "MOAR MOAR!!" is what they'll be screaming by now. Bring your ideas to a close, and allow the readers to reflect by using very simple, smooth sentences. This should express your overall idea, should include references to all three of the other paragraphs, and should reference your first paragraph in some way. Allow readers to feel a sense of accomplishment and insight after reading your essay. Keep it on topic, and you'll have them amazed and satisfied.
Consistency must be followed through all paragraphs, and on-topic sentences must be easily noticeable in all paragraphs. Your essay should not jump around at all - keep it relevant.

003X - Creative Writing; In-Depth
This includes my previous, updated, now locked guide for creative writing. It also includes a new guide for poetry as a sub-section.
What is this?
-This is a quick guide to improving creative writing skills, assuming you know the basics of the English language.

--Why does this help?
-This method has swifted me through every essay I've ever needed to write in my entire life with the minimum score of 95%. It's quick, painless, fun, and it'll actually work for you. The only problems might come around if you're dyslexic or if you have a learning disability. In which case, seek one-on-one guidance from an English professor or some-such.

--How?
Easy. There's only five steps, and anything that is fiction will easily begin to formulate. Simply grab some scratch paper and start answering these simple questions. They are as follows:
  • Where are the characters to begin with?[/i] I was never fond of having "setting", "background", "backdrop", or "ambiance" being separate things. Sure, if you want to go all "Grammar Nazi" on me, they are, and I know they are. But unless you're writing a thesis, they can stay one-in-the-same. During this step, you need to decide where the characters are, and what condition that area is in. War? Peace? Random roaming party-goers running amok? Rainstorm of fish? You need to define where they are in great, overkilled detail. This will help complete thoughts later on. Make it mundane. List every possible thing about the area where they are.
  • Why are the characters there?[/i] I've always loved those stories where a character wakes up and has no idea where the hell they are, but lets step away from that idea for a minute or so. This is the step where you need to define why characters are in the area of step #1. Be very creative, and be sure to think it through. Don't make it absurd, but don't make it pointless either. The characters need to have a realistic goal. If you're writing a story in the shadow of the Final Fantasy games, or anything along the lines of near super-human power, feel free to step into the absurd, but don't jump into it. Keep it believable and interesting.
  • Where were they before they arrived?[/i] Every story needs a backstory. This is where you define it. During this step, make a rather detailed time-line, paragraph, list, Venn diagram, table, etc... of where your characters were before Step 2 or Step 1. Where were they last week? Month? Year? This will help add personality to your characters, and it will also help them evolve into semi-real people.
  • What is, or will be, in their way of completing their goals?[/i] Every good story has a villain. In some stories, the character is the villain. In some, the characters psyche is the villain. Here, you need to define whatever force, person, thought, metaphysical summon, etc... is holding them back from completing their coal. Make sure it's not too weak or pointless, but don't make them have to face an army of Agent 47's infused with Satan's DNA, either.
  • How will it all end?[/i] Here is the one part that people get most confused over. They usually think, "Of course, the good guys win" - but they never consider the most important feat: At what cost do the 'good guys' win at? What price was paid? Who had to die? How many innocents were harmed or killed? Who had to be betrayed? These are all excellent questions to ask yourself. Also, how is their victory (or defeat) going to effect the world around them? What will change if they win? If they don't? This is the step where you must decide how the story will end, how the end will happen, what happens after the end, and why this ending and not any other ending had to be chosen by the characters. OR you could go rogue and make an open ending. A cliffhanger, maybe? Present the reader with a "HOLY WTF" moment, perhaps? All stories are different. Make your ending as unique as the person who wrote it. Once you get that down, it's as simple as done.... almost.
  • Wash, rinse, repeat[/i] Okay, I lied. There are 6 steps, but this last one is the easiest to follow. Once you have Steps 1-5 done, just go back and repeat Steps 1-4 for every new city, town, mountain range, bar, etc... the characters arrive at. Just keep going through these steps until you're satisfied and finished, and once the "biggest obstacle" is out of the way. Or, depending on your story, you could mix up these steps in any way you'd like. Don't do that unless you're confident in your abilities, though. Remember - you don't learn from success; only by mistakes do people grow.
For mystery stories, for example, the goal and biggest obstacle could be to catch a killer and prove it was them. In an action story, it could be to take out a huge enemy base and escape silently into the night. Romance stories could have love-triangles, and dealing with the "deceitful" member of the trio of romances. Each story is what you make of it - no two stories should ever be the same. Don't be afraid to branch out into new territory; it's usually for the better if you're thinking, "Wow! I don't think anyone has ever done that before!"

Note: This is a basic layout for a specific type of fictional story. There are an infinite amount of styles out there - these are only basic, "cut and paste" elements for a basic story. I encourage you to develop your own writing style.

003.5X - Poetry
Poetry comes in many flavors and styles. There are dozens of styles. No really. Dozens. This will cover three styles of common poetry; but feel free to take these teachings to other formats.
A stanza is like a "poetic paragraph", and it is usually four lines of poetry. Poetry doesn't follow conventional grammatical forms in a sense, and can contain fragments or "on-purpose" errors; just like a song can.
  • ABAB Poetry - It is set up so the first and third lines of one stanza, and the second and fourth lines of one stanza rhyme. ABAB poems can continue for as long as the author would like, but they tend to be four or five stanzas long. Each line is generally no larger than 7 or 8 syllables.
  • AABB Poetry - It is set up so the first and second lines of one stanza, and the third and fourth lines of one stanza rhyme. AABB Poems are usually longer than ABAB poems, up to 10 stanzas on average. Each line is also generally no larger than 7 or 8 syllables.
  • ABCB Poetry - It is set up so only the second and fourth lines of one stanza rhyme. These are the most "storytelling" poems, since more of it is left up to open-endings, but they are also the hardest to make flow. Since they use half the rhyming of most other poems, it's important that they retain their consistency when it comes to structure. They are usually the longest four-line-stanza poems; up to 12 or 13 stanzas per average poem.
004X - Autobiography
Autobiographies can be broken down into three basic categories, and is very simple to sum-up. Each of these is multi-paragraphed; the writer generally decides how long the autobiography will be.
  • Before - This includes far past events, times, happenings, and gatherings. This is usually the most vague, unless a huge event happened back then that is very prominent. This sets the tone for the rest of the Autobiography. Nostalgia should drench each sentence, and a foggy, dusty-air feel should be apparent. This helps the reader to understand the distance of time from the time of writing and the past events. Emotions should be included; both past emotions, and emotions based on these recollections.
  • Main Recollections - This includes all major recollections that the author wished to convey in the autobiography. It should be very, very vivid and should get points across very clearly. It should include emotions that were and still are apparent; or the "then and now" type of explanations of emotions. It should also include what you were thinking during these events. Include minute details if necessary. Be sure to be very clear in this section.
  • Recent Recollections - Include recent events and recollections that are relevant to the "Main Recollections" section. Allow it to flow from references from the first two sections, too. This should include your closing statements, ideas, and reflections based on your previous events.
005X - Website Contents
This will help you to fill in any basic 5-page informative website. It also includes a 6th section that is rather website-specific. This will NOT assist in graphical development, but generally, graphics should be the same throughout. This is called a "Layout".
  • PAGE1 - Home - It should include recently updated info, news, and should include a basic description of a recent project, event, service, offer, etc... Keep it simple. Here's your chance to hook a prospective customer or reader.
  • PAGE2 - About Us - Include detailed info about you, your team, the company, etc... Include info about the history, the present, and where you intend to "go".
  • PAGE3 - What We Do - Feel free to rename this anything you'd like. It includes general info of what you do, where you do it, how you do it, what you sell, etc.. Keep it detailed and specific.
  • PAGE4 - Our Products - Show examples of things you've made, products you've finished, services you offer, etc. Make it clear and simple. Include pictures if you can.
  • PAGE5 - Contact Info - Include detailed methods for people, customers, employers, commissioners, etc. to contact you at. Phone number if possible, but an email will work just as fine. Include a brief paragraph or two about what people should contact you regarding, and any special subject-lines for certain topics.
  • PAGE6 - The Team - OPTIONAL - Include short biographies of each team member if a team exists. Pictures are a must, because no one will straight-up read about people they don't know. Detailed and random information is a plus.
006X - Business Letters
This is a VERY short guide, since there are dozens of formats, and dozens of different types of businesses a business letter could be written for.
  • The Three-Piece Method - Start off with an introductory sentence. This should introduce yourself, your position in a company (or lack thereof), and your basic intentions. Continue on as you would in a normal introduction. Then, in the "body" of your letter, go on about different topics you'd like to discuss with the company in question. Each paragraph should only cover one subject; or multiple joint-subjects if appropriate. Don't forget to list sources if necessary. The final paragraph should sum it all up, and include a professional signature at the end, such as simply: "Signed,"
007X - Business Cards
This guide is broken down into two short, simple, to-the-point guide.
  • Front - Include the name of the company, the company slogan or motto, the company logo or a graphical background relevant to the company, your name above your job title, UP TO three addresses for the company, a general phone number for the company, your phone number WITHIN the company, a fax number if available, an email address for you, and the company website address.
  • Back - The sleekest way would to simply have the company logo centered on the back of the card with a glossy finish. Although, simply having the company name in bold letters across the back would be fine as well. The company website may also be added at the bottom of the center of the back of the card; as well as the company name at the top-center location.
008X - Video Game Story-Lines
A look at the in-depth writing guide should help you a TON on this one, so I'm not getting that in-depth into Video Game story lines. But I'm touching on three very primly needed areas of expertise.
  • Big Bad Guys - The game should include a few sightings of the biggest badass this world has ever seen in the beginning of the story. If not, it should gradually be revealed. Make the main enemy, quest, or challenge larger than life. Then, break it down into a few steps (or one gigantic battle instead) to make it easier for the player, but still hard as hell. The multiple steps will make the player "enjoy" the game on different thought-levels, and will make the challenge larger since you're taking something on in pieces because it's too difficult to straight-up manhandle.
  • It's A Small World - Don't make the world of the game differ too much from the storyline. If the game is a "save the world" type of game, don't let all of the adventure be in only one town. Really, folks, thats a no-brainer, but it's messed up on countless times by big-name publishers. Heck, even Mario had to travel around to beat the game. It's all about scale. Small gang violence can be city-wide, but stopping a menacing group of super-baddies in an airship armed with a rotating nuke-revolver cannon won't be a one-town-stop kind of story.
  • Short Skirt and a Looooong Jacket - If you're going to have female lead characters in your game, don't give them a short skirt, or armor that impractically shows off their breasts or behinds. Really. That's just lame. Suit them up all proper and such, and maybe even make them a bigger of a badass than the guys. Hell hath no fury like a woman's scorn.
009X - Pen'N'Paper RPG Story Worlds
Here are three ESSENTIAL things to have in your Pen'N'Paper RPG World. I will use Shadowrun - 4th Edition as a basic example in two of these.
  • Creating a memorable experience ----- The number-one reason some people sit down to a round of died cola on a poker table, stat-sheets around their general area, a 15x15 grid in the center, with little miniature figurines positioned about it is that such a time is a memorable one. To write a Pen'N'Paper RPG story, you'll need a memorable world. In Shadowrun, the world is, well, Earth. But it's the high-tech, cyber-infused, gritty, slightly metallic world that Shadowrun exists within which makes it a memorable one. Think up a list of your 10 favorite things about the current world you live in, and then make a list of your 10 least favorite things. Additionally, make a list of your 10 favorite aspects of this world. Then, inject them all with MLB-Approved steroids until they're too awesome for words. This will be the basic foundation[/b] of your world. You'll need to fine-tune it for general use, since GM's (Game Masters - Admins of a Pen'N'Paper RPG Session) are allowed (and free to whether or not you say they can) to modify any rules if necessary.
  • Open-Ended is a Plus ----- Unlike console/PC RPG's, Pen'N'Paper RPG's change on the fly. If the character is shot in the forearm with a cannonball, and his arm is severed, he doesn't have any type of godly health-pack or special healing item to heal that, unless the GM invented one. Damage is realistic (for the most part). If you're cut in the throat, you'd better hope your Luck attribute is pretty high. You need to allow players to get themselves neck-deep into deep doodoo; no safety nets. Allowing players to completely ruin their gaming experience allows them to think about their decisions. If the P'n'P Roleplayers wanted to have X-Number of lives before it's "Game Over", they'd hop onto a console game; or they'd play WoW. They chose to play a P'n'P RPG because of the risk involved.
  • Complexity is Sectioned ----- In Shadowrun, a game much more complex than D&D ever could be, there are two game worlds. The Matrix, a digital recreation of the world in which a character uses their real-life abilities to create their avatar; and the self-explanatory "Real" World. If your character is a white-knuckled brawling character named "Tyrone", who's choice weapon is a shovel, then you're probably not going to be hopping into the Matrix any time soon. Just like in actual life, you don't play every course at once. Similarly, a player who uses Martial Arts and hand-to-hand stealth attacks won't every have to roll dice to try to get a snipers-shot critical strike. Also, a Techno-Mage type character won't ever need to roll dice to see if they can land an extra hand-to-hand combat strike in. Make your P'n'P game as vast as possible, with all applicable options. Just because one player doesn't play every aspect of your game at once doesn't mean that one thousand players wont touch all bases you've created.
These three basic aspects will help to develop all amateur Pen-And-Paper Role Playing Games, and will give them a solid foundation; once a THEME is developed. Unless you have a basic, compass-rose direction of your RPG, you shouldn't progress to these three steps.
