Prescribed Title



Evaluate the role of intuition in different areas of knowledge

Mason **** ****
12/12/07

Word Count: 1280



Intuition is one of the less easily-copied aspects of human behavior. It can be defined roughly as belief without thinking-though this provides a somewhat cynical idea of its applications. A somewhat better definition may be the direct perception of substance, what Robert M. Pirsig referred to in Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance as a sort of romantic view as opposed to the classical view dependent entirely on deconstruction. One might humourously quip that to understand intuition requires intuition. However, though difficult to define, intuition nevertheless plays a part in almost all human activity-and as a result in many fields of knowledge. Some major issues arise with the direct application of intuition-most importantly, the lack of verifiability of the source of knowledge and any personal biases of the intuiter. These gaps in the utility of intuition can be bridged in various ways, and so intuition typically plays varying roles in different areas of knowledge.
Mathematics is rarely regarded as an intuitive pursuit. Indeed, the foundations of mathematics are commonly considered some of the most firmly based in logic, and mathematics falls definitively on the classical side of what Pirsig called the dialectic split. Mathematical proof insists on complete certainty, which is not a hallmark of intuition. However, the process of proving a statement in mathematics is rarely straightforward; many statements do not give immediate clues as to their truth or falsehood. It is in this application that intuition may be seen to play the largest role in mathematical inquiry. Mathematicians often credit some ingenious proofs to intuition-I usually consider most mathematical proofs I write to be the result of intuition. These proofs, however, also highlight limitations in the application of intuition; my proofs are rarely the shortest or simplest way to prove a statement.
The main two knowledge issues that arise from the use of intuition are biases and the limitations of the source. Generally, in mathematics, biases are rare-a statement can be tested for veracity. As a result, the main knowledge issue that arises from the application of intution in mathematics is the source of the information. For example, in my mathematics class last year, the class was unable to solve a group problem-find the number of ways a circle may be divided by lines through a given number of points on the edge-until a friend of mine stopped playing calculator games and figured out how to find the solution. Also, Euler, despite extensive effort, was unable to prove that any given number may be represented as the sum of four squares, though Lagrange solved it rather quickly. In that way, the knowledge that one can gain from intuition in mathematics is limited by the intuition of the person.
Another viewpoint states that intuition has a significantly smaller application in mathematics. If proof is considered as the object rather than the mathematicians pursuing it, one can argue that proof, by its nature, must be an entirely conscious process. Therefore, intuition is not a source for knowledge but rather a direction, and given the counterintuitive nature of many ideas in mathematics it may be misleading as often as it is accurate. Here we see Pirsigs dialectic split: from the viewpoint of the mathematician, intuition plays a large role, while from the viewpoint of the person reading the proof, intuition plays a much smaller role. This is similar to the dialectic split in many other areas of knowledge.
An area in which the dialectic split can be particularly predominant is ethics, and there is also a divide between views on the role of intuition in ethics. Unlike mathematics, biases are frequent in ethics; in particular, historical and regional bias, and these are evident often in intuitive ethical concepts. For example, many people today see the practice of dueling as inherently barbaric, while in the 18th century, it was rather common. Historical and regional bias therefore presents a significant barrier to the application of intutition in ethics. Also, expectation influences many peoples ethical judgements; in America, where alcohol use is widespread, many people view the sale of alcohol as less morally questionable that the sale of illegal, though safer, drugs such as LSD or marijuana.
However, intuition can play a role in ethics. Nietzche argued that objective ethics are impossible, and that therefore all ethics must be considered from a particular perspective. Nietzche argued that It is our needs that interpret the world; our drives and their For and Against. In this view, a bias is not only inevitable, but a necessary part of ethics. From this view, the biases in intuition are as meaningless as they are inevitable. On the other side of the dialectic split, one might argue that ethics are real, and that the biases inherent in intuitive ethical judgement are not acceptable. Proponents of this view might argue for ethical objectivism; however, this raises the question of how effectively ethical questions can be resolved by reason alone. One major strength of intuitions role in ethics is the ability to answer questions which are difficult to answer from a rational perspective. This raises another question-what is the source of this judgement. If I am ever faced with the decision to take few lives to save many, such as in the famous problem of the trolley about to hit five people-yet I am able to divert it so that it will only hit one, I intuitively believe that I ought to save the five people and kill the one. However, I cannot explain how I make this decision.
Another area in which intuition has variable applicability is the natural sciences. Leo Szilard, for example, famously invented the idea of a nuclear chain reaction while waiting at a traffic light-a friend of his remarked that the hardest part of that story to believe was that Szilard actually stopped at a traffic light. Much of theoretical science occurs in thought experiments, which usually apply intuition to reach their final conclusions. However, these are occasionally found to be simply wrong, highlighting an important knowledge issue: in science, intuition is not the intended source of knowledge. This is one of the most prominent issues in the application of intuition to scientific inquiry.
Another important issue is personal bias. This can be evidenced no better than the famous thought experiment which implied quantum entanglement-action at a distance-which Einstein used to imply that quantum mechanics is an incorrect theory. However, experiments later showed that Einsteins original conclusion was correct; action at a distance does indeed occur. It is likely that Einstein may not have been so convinced had he not already been attempting to invalidate quantum mechanical theory.
These issues usually cause people to believe that intuition has no suitable role in the natural sciences; experimentation ought to be the norm. However, as Robert M. Pirsig pointed out in Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, the hypothesis step of the scientific method cannot originate from experiment. This idea supports the use of intuition in the natural sciences.
The main knowledge issues that arise from the role of intuition in various areas of knowledge are the sources of knowledge and the biases in the knowledge. These are closely connected; intuition is said to come partially from a persons mind as well as from the idea it concerns, and therefore knowledge gained by intuition will necessarily carry biases. Also, the mental mechanisms of intuition are not fully understood; it is not known where this knowledge comes from. These views are consistent with a classical, deconstructivist perspective according to Pirsig; from a romantic perspective, intuition has more validity, and biases are less important. In either view, intuition continues to have a significant role in many different areas of knowledge.
