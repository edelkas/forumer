THE SURVIVAL MAP MANIFESTO
by blue_tetris



Survival maps. They defy the basic intent of the game. They are, like DDAs, a game within a game. They are an application of the N objects used in a way that was not intended--to create a map not to be completed, but rather endured.

In creating this manifesto, I intend to instruct map makers on how to create the perfect survival map. Why am I qualified to do this? I like to think I make good survival maps. It's an aspect of the map-making scene which I feel some amount of dominion over. I believe I have more survival maps than any other map-maker and I also believe that I withhold secrets which may help prospective map makers. I cannot, in all conscience, keep the secrets hidden any longer.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Before we get into the steps involved in making the ultimate survival map, we should discuss the elements that make a map a survival map. These elements are:

1) Mobility and motivity
2) Access
3) Persistence and evolution of challenge



Let's first discuss mobility and motivity.

Mobility is the element of a map which defines the ability for the player to move the ninja based on the selection and placement of tiles and solid objects. Examine this map. The rarity of the simple horizontal tiles deprives the ninja of a large amount of mobility. The sloped tiles around the map hinder the ninja's mobility in certain directions, as well. The one-ways and flat walls provide the ninja with the mobility to wall-jump and slide as well. Mobility is largely defined by the elements of the map that the ninja can easily make use of to move around. Most of this map cannot be used to mobilize the ninja, because there are many mines to prevent those areas from being accessed. Limiting the ninja's mobility will allow you to place enemies which can more effectively test the player's skills.

Motivity is the element of a map which defines the ability for the player to move the ninja based on the placement of objects which contain some amount of stored energy. The types of objects that can motivate the ninja include bounceblocks, launchpads, and thwumps. These objects store energy which can propel the ninja faster than normal or allow the ninja to travel into areas which normal mobility would not as easily provide access to. Examine this map. The ninja has the ability to use the reactive nature of the bouceblocks and thwumps to access areas of the map that would be more difficult to access with the tiles alone.

Now, let's talk about access.

Access is the element of a map which describes which areas that the ninja and enemies can move into or attack into and how easily those areas can be "accessed". Examine this map. The ninja can easily access almost all of the middle area, but has no access to the outer areas of the map. The tiles in the center provide the ninja with sufficient mobility to access this area without any trouble at all. We would call this area highly accessible to the ninja. The attacks from the chaingun and laser drones are obscured by the tiles on the left and right. Because those enemies only have intermittent access to the center area, we would say that this area is moderately accessible to the enemies. A survival map is defined by the dispute over areas accessible to both the ninja and the enemies. The ninja is vying for perfect positioning in the accessible areas, while the enemies are vying to dominate these areas.

If there are any areas of the map which the ninja can access easily but the enemies have difficulty accessing, the survival map may become too easy or the player may not have any incentive to guide the ninja out of these "safety zones". If there are areas accessible to the ninja which the enemies cannot access at all, the player can move into one of these areas and survive indefinitely. Alternatively, areas which the enemies can access but the ninja cannot can sometimes be considered as "unused areas". Unused areas are not necessarily bad, though, as they can help focus gameplay.

How about persistence and evolution of challenge?

Persistence of challenge is the element of a survival map which refers to the continuity of the challenge over time. If the challenge of a survival map isn't persistent, there will be periods of time during which the player can take a break or pay less attention to the gameplay. Inability to supply a persistent challenge may make your survival map boring. Conversely, you may want to insert breaks within the persistence of challenge to allow the player some solace between the continuing assault. A challenge which remains too persistent may discourage the players from laboring on or trying to get better demos on the map. A challenge which lacks persistence may give the player too many opportunities to plan ahead and will give the player extra "free frames" on their demo for doing nothing more than playing the map. A survival map maker should seek to find a good balance. Examine this map. There are temporary "safe spots" which open up near the sides of the pillars that allow for temporary breaks in the persistence of challenge. Making use of these moments of safety allows a player to think of a new strategy or simply cool down after a series of daring leaps. Examine this map. The challenge of this map is very persistent; players don't have time to take a break from the action to strategize or recuperate. This persistence seems to fit the general intent of the map, because there is not much variety to demand time for planning.

Evolution of challenge is the element of a survival map which refers to how the overall challenge alters over time. If a map's challenge remains the same for too long, a player may be able to develop a pattern to overcome the repetetive challenge. Patterns are the bane of a survival map. Patterns prevent the players from having fun and force them to engage in menial repetition of events just to "win" the map. In order to prevent the development of patterns, you have to set up the map so that the challenge becomes different as time goes on. Examine this map. As time passes, the drones currently tucked away from the main area of the map gain access. When those enemies gain access to the area that the ninja can access, the challenge increases and the player must adapt to this greater level of difficulty. The evolution of challenge doesn't necessarily need to be an increase in difficulty. Simply altering the configuration of enemies without making things more difficult will alter the challenge and break player patterns. Examine this map for another example of evolution of challenge. Notice the enemies that currently cannot access the center, but will gain access over time.




~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
So, now you know what elements make a good survival map. The next big question is: how are these elements controlled? To indulge this topic, we will break it down into steps. The order of these steps can be followed to create the perfect survival map, so pay particular attention! The steps are:

1) Conceptualize the map
2) Mobilize and motivate the ninja
3) Place the enemies
4) Tweak!



First, let's conceptualize our new map.

Conceptualizing involves selecting a concept to pursue for creation of the map. The concept will be an over-arching principle which aligns the focus of the gameplay. Knowing how the map will be played will give you a pretty good idea on how the map will need to be designed. To conceptualize, you'll need to consider all of the elements of a survival map, which were discussed at length earlier. Should the map offer very little room for the ninja to move? Should it have a lot of physical space, but only a few spaces for the ninja to stand? Should it be a wide open area with many gameplay options, but a host of enemies swarming over it? Should it be a single one-way contested by a few drones?

To break down the conceptualiziation process, you may want to consider each element separately. How much mobility will be offered by the map? How much area will the ninja access? How much area will the enemies access? How persistent will the challenge be? How much will the challenge evolve? Here are a few concepts to consider before starting on your map:

High mobility, low ninja access, moderate enemy access, high persistence of challenge, high evolution of challenge. Example.
Moderate mobility, moderate ninja access, high enemy access, moderate persistence of challenge, low evolution of challenge. Example.
Low mobility, high ninja access, moderate enemy access, high persistence of challenge, low evolution of challenge. Example.
High mobility, high ninja access, moderate enemy access, low persistence of challenge, moderate evolution of challenge. Example.



After you've come up with your concept, you'll need to lay down the basic tiles, mines, and other simple objects to manage the map's space. After you have created areas of accessibility, you can consider mobilizing and motivating the ninja.

Consider the area which the ninja can access and consider how much mobility the ninja needs to have within this area. Will the ninja be taxed just trying to get around? Will the ninja be able to easily move about in the accessible region? Alter the tileset and the object placement during this phase to ensure that the ninja has exactly as much mobility as you want. Also, consider motivity. Consider how certain objects can throw the ninja quickly across the map. Consider the disruptive effects of a launchpad, the special tricks available using a bounceblock, and the special access granted by riding a thwump. All of these will modify the ninja's movement in the map.



Place the enemies. If necessary, alter the tiles and other objects to allow the enemies more or less access. Consider the timing of enemies, if you intend to make the challenge of the map evolve over time. Carefully consider the path of the drones during the continuity of the map. How often will the player get a break from the action? Do you need to modify the paths or the tiles so the action is more or less persistent? How likely is it that a player will find a pattern? How can you go about breaking a player's abiltiy to find "safe spots" or develop a pattern to "win" the map? Ask yourself all of these questions during this step.

Remember that a survival map is not a puzzle to be solved. It is a test of a player's skill against an increasingly taxing and variable challenge. Ensure that the number of enemies is not so great that there is no decent strategy to survive within the map. Allow the player to have some control over the situation. However, also make sure the the player is persistently challenged and forced to respond with innovation and cleverness--not with programmed reflexes and mindlessly repetetive stunts. Consider yourself as the player. In this way, you can engage in the activities of placing the enemies with an idea of the physical and mental challenge involved.



Lastly, tweak! Playtest the map over and over. Forget that you made the map! Play it like you've never seen it before. Try to beat the map. Try to foil your own best-laid plans. Hide in every possible nook and cranny. After you get a feel for the map, think of how to improve it. Add things. Subtract things. Modify the challenge. Keep in mind the persistence and evolution of the challenge while you playtest.

Throw down some gold for an optional challenge. Maybe even add something small last minute without playtesting! This can sometimes help the map, because you may have been over-thinking your map.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
In the end, you should have a well-made survival map. Practice makes perfect. After a few tries at making a survival map, you should be able to throw down objects with less planning and less playtesting.

I hope that my secrets in the art of survival map creation can come in handy for you up-and-coming survivalists. Keep the general concepts of survival map making in mind when you open up Ned. Even if you already have a developed system of your own, you may find that these elements and principles give you a new and helpful perspective for perfecting your art. Remember that one of the best aspects of map making, in general, is diverging from the trend. Use whatever you like from this manifesto, and sprinkle on copious amounts of personal ingenuity for the best results.

This has been a blue_tetris manifesto. Carry this wisdom with you.

THE THWUMP MANIFESTO
by blue_tetris



Thwumps. Arguably, the thwump is the most versatile object in the game--nay, the universe. The thwump can serve as a way to motivate the ninja, a way to let the ninja gain access to difficult areas, and of course as a way to kill the ninja.

In creating this manifesto, I intend to instruct map makers on the many uses of the thwump. Why am I qualified to do this? I use an unparalleled number of thwumps. Map-for-map, I have the highest thwump usage rate on NUMA. I've spent more time figuring out every possible use for a thwump than I would willingly confess to friends and loved-ones. Also, I believe I possess the secrets of thwump. My spirit remains hindered so long as I retained these secrets alone.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
We'll split this topic into sections, to make it easier to digest. Each section will highlight a separate function of thwumps:

1) Thwumps strike
2) Thwumps race forward and slink back
3) Thwumps manage space and time



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Thwumps strike. They quickly race forward to kill the ninja. As a map-maker, it's your task to make the thwump more effective at killing the ninja. Anyone can place more thwumps in an effort to kill the ninja. A true expert at map-making knows how to place the fewest possible thwumps to get the same result. In fact, this rules holds true for maps in general. Less is more! The objective of map-making is to place objects where they are most effective, rather than placing an excess of objects to clumsily attempt the same result.

Thwumps are triggered. In fact, the most potent aspect of the thwump is that it stores energy and operates without an algo. The thwump's activity depends solely on the movement of the ninja. In this way, the thwump is a player's object--that is, an object that is easier to utilize when you consider the map from a player's perspective than from a map-maker's.

Thwumps are triggered under specific conditions. You can assess the conditions which cause the thwump to trigger with the following rules:

RULE ONE: The thwump is triggered if the ninja moves into a grid space which the thwump can enter. The thwump can enter any space in the direction of its "attack side", until these spaces are limited by a tile (of any type). This rule is the one which, when properly used, results in more ninja deaths. Why? Because these are the instance in which the movement of the thwump is already aligned. Any other movement of the thwump requires special circumstances to be able to catch the ninja. The set of spaces accessible to the ninja using this rule will hereafter be called the strike range[/color].

RULE TWO: The thwump is triggered if the ninja moves into a grid space which is adjacent to one of the grid spaces which would suffice rule one. This rule is the one which the player can more often use to trick the thwump, or activate the thwump for other purposes. Granted, either rule can be used to the player's advantage, but this is the one which does so more often. In almost every circumstance, rule two is exercised before rule one. After all, the ninja will need to approach the thwump before she can be directly within its strike range. Rule two will be referenced frequently in this manifesto.



Thwumps, like every object except the ninja, disobey gravity. They function at equal rates, regardless of which way they're facing. The physics which the ninja is forced to obey can supply the ninja with an increased or decreased amount of mobility, with which to avoid the strike. Let's examine the different facings of the thwump to determine how each thwump-facing affects the gameplay:

A down-facing thwump is one which strikes downward when triggered. This thwump is typically the easiest to avoid under normal circumstances, because the physics of N provides the ninja with more mobility when traveling downward. Gravity will help the ninja avoid the thwump which strikes downward, by providing extra downward velocity. Also, the ninja usually passes left to right quickly and can be out of this thwump's range in a very short amount of time.

An up-facing thwump is one which strikes upward when triggered. This thwump is typically the most difficult to avoid, because the ninja is aerial within the strike range. The ninja has little to no mobility in this range--that is to say, the ninja has very little control over his own movement. Additionally, gravity will help pull the ninja towards the thwump as it strikes. Conversely, because the ninja can move left and right rather quickly, he can be outside of the strike range relatively quickly if his jump is well-performed.

A lateral-facing thwump is one which strikes to the left or right when triggered. This thwump can be easy to avoid while falling, because gravity will pull the ninja out of the thwump's strike range rather quickly. This thwump can be leapt over if the ninja responds quickly. Tactics to avoid a lateral thwump are much different than tactics to avoid a down- or up-facing thwump.



The usage of these differently facing thwumps can vary strongly with the placement of certain tiles that prohibit motion in certain directions. Consider a down-facing thwump in a tall column, where the ninja cannot move left or right. This thwump is much more likely to strike the ninja, because the ninja is unable to move left or right to escape the strike range.

When you place a thwump, you have to consider how much time the ninja will be forced to remain in the strike range. The ninja will, of course, try to find the quickest way to escape the strike range in an effort to stay alive. Your placement of tiles and other objects can help make the strike range difficult to escape.

Again, the most important question to consider about thwumps striking is: How long will the ninja be within the strike range.[/color] Every time a ninja is faced with a striking thwump, she faces an isolated race against time. To effectively manage thwumps, you have to manage the length of time (albeit a very short length of time) that it will take for the ninja to escape the strike range.

Additionally, other objects can be placed within the strike range to manipulate the movement and mobility of the ninja. Consider how one-ways, mines, bounceblock, and other interferences will alter the way the ninja can escape the strike range. Remember that even though it is a race against time, it is a very short-lived one. Thwumps move fast, so too much intereference will quickly turn an exciting and challenging task into a frustrating or impossible one.



Take a look at these examples to understand strike range and how to effectively manage the ninja's progress through it.

Example. Look at the first thwump that the ninja will be forced to encounter. Notice how the area to the left of the thwump cannot be entered without a jump. This limits how the ninja will be able to utilize rule two for activating the thwump. It is an up-facing thwump, so the ninja will have to move swiftly from left to right to prevent gravity from pulling him down into the thwump for the length of time he remains in the strike range.

The second and third thwumps can be activated by the ninja using rule two, preventing the ninja from spending any time within the strike range while the thwumps are striking. Notice how thwumps combine elements of quick-moving action and planning. Every thwump situation must be handled differently by the player in an effort to avoid the strike.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Thwumps race forward and slink back. It's important to acknowledge the distinction between racing forward and striking. Certainly, both actvities involve the same action on the part of the thwump. However, in this section, we will discuss the thwumps usage as an object of mobility rather than its usage as an enemy. We will discuss how the player can use the thwump to assist the ninja's movement.

It's useful to visualize the strike range of the thwump as a virtual platform[/color] which can be utilized by savvy players to get around. Whether you'd like them to be used this way or not, a player can always attempt to make use of a thwump in this fashion. From this point on, we'll call the activity of using a thwump as a virtual platform "thwump riding".



Depending on the facing of the thwump, different sorts of thwump riding will occur:

When thwumps race forward laterally, they create a mobile platform upon which the ninja can stand. If the ninja is moving at the same rate as the thwump, this platform can be as useful as a series of tile which are the full length of the thwump's strike range. If you want to require or provoke a player to ride a thwump in this way, you must first provide the player with the ability to activate the thwump. This is typically done using rule two, as the player will be adjacent to the thwump's strike range during the entire activity of thwump riding. When the ninja is thwump riding, she will have a limited ability to react to events. Most of the velocity will have to be in the direction of the thwump's movement. In this way, the ninja will have the ability to use the thwump as a virtual platform, but will have to do so at the cost of mobility. Remember this when you create challenges to be managed during a thwump ride. Examine this map. The left-facing thwump at the bottom can be utilized by the palyer to ride over a long portion of the mines. The only difficulty in using the thwump in this manner is the thwump striking from the other direction. Many players find that thwump riding offers enough mobility to manage this challenge. If players are uncomfortable with thwump riding, they may elect to forgo this process. Consider the difference between forcing players to ride a thwump or allowing them to do so.

When thwumps race forward vertically, they create a different sort of mobile platform. This mobile platform is more useful for wallsliding and riding directly upward. There is a much smaller amount of mobility that the ninja can attain from them. If the thwump is down-facing, the ninja can ride on the solid top of the thwump like an elevator. Alternately, the ninja can attempt to wall jump or wallslide using a thwump. This activity is difficult and you have to be careful when you make use of it in a level.

Examine this map. This is a relatively nasty thwump-riding level. In the top part, the player is forced to ride atop a thwump and manage a challenge. In the area on the right, the ninja is forced to slide on the sides of thwumps racing downward in an effort to avoid mines during the fall. On the left, the ninja must walljump off of the thwumps to climb through the passage.



While a thwump is slinking back, the ninja can ride it with a higher level of ease. The ninja is granted much more mobility, because the thwump is moving slowly and the ninja is not compelled to run full-speed just to stay atop it. Challenges can have better pacing and more freedom for the ninja. Examine this map. The ninja must trigger the thwump and ride on it to reach the left side. On the return, however, the thwump moves more slowly and the ninja has more time to react to the movement of the drones. In this way, the ride to the left is more puzzle--the ninja has to start his journey at just the right moment to cut through a gap in the drones. The right back to the right is more action--the ninja has to expertly dodge the drones and survive the lengthy trip home.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Thwumps manage space and time. This aspect of using thwumps can be considered the most expert, in that it requires not only a command of the map's layout, but also a command of a third dimension (time) as it affects the map. Understanding the usage of objects on the map is an easy task compared to managing the progress of the map and all the future states of the objects in the map.

Thwumps are, as mentioned earlier, a player's object. Their function within the map is exclusively dependent upon the activity of the ninja. Using a thwump to manage space is the process by which you, the map-maker, predict the activity of the thwump and use its continuing position to influence where the ninja can go or what areas of the map the ninja will gain or lose access to. Let's take a quick look back to this map. Notice the thwump at the top of the map. When activated by the ninja, this thwump will race across the ceiling. This ninja must use this thwump as an object to prevent his jumps from reaching the row of mines. This is an aspect of using thwumps to manage space.

Examine this map. Notice how the thwump at the top acts much like a wall. If the player moves over to the right and attempts to grab the switch on the top, she'll be blocked from entering that area due to the position of the thwump. In order to access that area, the player will have to activate the thwump to cause it to move. However, that thwump can only be activated from the far left side of the map. In this fashion, the thwump manages space.

..and it also manages time.



In the example above, the player will have to move quickly to access that area of the map before the thwump returns to seal it off. Activating the thwump creates a timeline.[/color] During this time, more space is accessible to the ninja. The ninja has a short amount of time to make use of this new space and do what is necessary to complete the level, before the passage of time (and the movement of the thwump) ends these opportunities.

In order to use thwumps to manage time, you have to limit access to the areas which can trigger the thwump. Only by moving into these areas will the ninja be able to start the virtual timer[/color] which allows access to other areas. If the ninja can trigger the thwump under other circumstances, you may find that the time allowed to access the new areas is too generous.

The only way to monitor the passage of this time and how well the player will be able to utilize it is by playtesting[/color]. It is nearly impossible to use thwumps correctly in map-making without taking control of the ninja and playng the level for yourself.

Examine this map. Notice how thw strike ranges of those thwumps are often inescapable. The only way to complete this map is by triggering the thwumps and entering the areas they block access to while they are still in motion. In moving around the map to access those other areas in time, the ninja will trigger other thwumps which will set off additional virtual timers that must be raced against.

Give the map a go to see this effect in action: http://numa.notdot.net/map/24512



Utilizing thwumps in this fashion requires:

1) limiting the areas which trigger the thwump,
2) creating areas which the thwump's initial position blocks access to, and
3) figuring out the amount of time it will take the ninja to get from the trigger areas to the newly accessible areas.

Maximizing the strain it takes to get from point A to point B will provide excitement and challenge to the map. Make sure not to overestimate the speed and abilities of the ninja. Consider your own skills against the skills of the average player when you're playtesting a map. Just because you can beat it with a second to spare doesn't mean every player will be. Just because you can't get there in time doesn't mean your player won't be able to. A map-maker often won't be able to complete some of the challenges in his own map as well as the average player could.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Understanding how thwumps work should be able to let you use them more effectively in map-making. There are a lot of other tricks that thwumps can perform which are not typical of N maps. Experimentation and exploration of NUMA will reveal some of these thwump glitches to you.

Thwumps, when used very effectively, can singlehandedly make a map. I hope these tips provide you with the confidence and ability to make the best use of each and every thwump you place in your levels.

THE GOLD MANIFESTO
by blue_tetris



Gold. Let's face it. The game would be drudgery without those glistening bits of hope guiding the way through each stage. For episodes, gold is a chief motivation for the player. Without it, players won't have the time to complete the episode. So why should you use it? Because it's a staple of the game, that's why! If you don't use gold, you're missing out on what makes N so... N.

In creating this manifesto, I intend to instruct map makers on how to appropriately place gold within their maps. I want to show how gold-placement helps to manage time, player motivation, replay value, and design.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
After that brief summary of why you should place gold, we'll focus most of our efforts on describing where you should place it. The where is best described by discussing the accessibility of the areas which will contain the gold. The accessibility of those areas will relate directly to the accessibility of the gold.

In this manifesto, we use the term "accessibility" to refer to how easily the ninja can enter a certain area. An area is highly accessible if the ninja can enter it and move around in it with ease. An area is poorly accessible if the ninja will have difficulty getting there or difficulty moving around in that area. An area is inaccessible if the ninja cannot reach the area or cannot do anything (or becomes stuck) when entering the area.

We'll split this topic up into the following sections to make it easier to manage:

1) Highly accessible gold, also known as "main route gold" or "main area gold"
2) Poorly accessible gold, also known as "optional challenge gold"
3) Mission gold, also known as "gold caches"
4) Gold design



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Highly accessible gold is gold which can be collected by the ninja through the ordinary course of gameplay.



One type of highly accessible gold is main route gold[/color]. Main route gold is placed throughout the regions of the map where the ninja is expected to go. The purpose of main route gold is to supply the ninja with extra time that you believe will be necessary in completing the map or the episode. Main route gold is gold that you expect all players to try to pick up.[/color]

Don't presume that main route gold must be easy to collect. Main route gold has the potential to sway the ninja to alter his gameplay style or step slightly outside of his planned course. Main route gold can be used to lead the ninja into areas along the main route which are slightly more dangerous than others. Even though they are along the general path, the main route gold will have great control over gameplay. Players must perform specific manevuers to grab the gold, in an effort to increase their time. Remember that to classify as main route gold, gold must be placed in such a way that player assume they are supposed to pick it up.

Most of the gold in this map is main route gold. In one regard, the gold seems to follow the path of the player. But without the gold, the map might suggest an entirely different "quickest route". Currently, the quickest route is to pass through the areas with gold using the types of leaps and jumps implied by the paths of the gold. In making the map, the map-maker presumed the player would attempt to collect most, if not all, of the gold.



Another type of highly accessible gold is main area gold[/color]. This type of gold placement occurs in maps that are more freeform. Instead of implying a route, the gold placement in these maps simply implies areas of the map which should be visited during the course of playing. This type of highly accessible gold can be easily ignored. It certainly doesn't force a player to choose a specific gameplay method, but it does imply that certain courses will be better than others. This type of gold placement makes speed runs a more arduous task, because players will have to do a lot of planning to formulate the best way to gather all the gold placed in different areas of the map.

While main route gold is gold you presume all players will collect all of, main area gold is gold you presume all players will collect some of. Main area gold is too tempting (and, occasionally, necessary) to pass up. However, not every player will be expected to collect all of it. Players will still have to select a route through this main area, which practically requires them to collect some of the gold. Players are not necessarily required to plan a route which can collect all of the gold, however. In this way, main area gold is gold that you expect all players to collect some portion of, regardless of skill.[/color] The more skill a player has, the more main area gold you can expect them to pick up.

Examine this map. There is a lot of main area gold in this map. In planning a route through this area, the player will certainly move through some amount of gold. In fact, it would be difficult not to pick up any main area gold. This is common with main area gold--it's assumed a player will pick up some of it when playing through the level. It would be difficult, but possible, for a player to plot a course through this map to pick up all the main area gold. An expert player would attempt this task.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Poorly accessible gold, also known as optional challenge gold[/color], is gold which lies slightly off of the main route or lies slightly outside the main area of the map. Optional challenge gold poses a risk, which the player has to weigh.[/color] The risk may be an extra challenge--like mines or enemies--which the player would otherwise have less difficult managing. The risk might just be time: "Is it worth trying to get that gold, even though it might not amount to more time than it takes to get there and back again?" Optional challenge gold is expected to only be pursued by skilled players.

Optional challenge gold should not require too many steps to acquire. The optional challenge gold shouldn't be so far off of the route that players have to make an additional excursion just to reach it. The gold should be in more difficult areas close to or even within the expected area where the ninja could travel.

Examine this map. The gold in that map doesn't require an addition excursion just to reach. Some of it, however, lies just far enough outside of the normal path of the ninja that a player would have to take a short aside to reach it. Some of the gold puts the ninja in greater danger from mines if she tries to collect it. This map has a lot of optional challenge gold.

This map also has plenty of optional gold. The gold in this map is risky because it comes singularly (only providing two extra seonds) if you go for. A player must weigh the danger and time involved in getting each gold.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Mission gold, also known as a gold cache[/color], is a special gameplay opportunity which presents the player with a lengthy or risky excursions in exchange for a large amount of gold. The benefits for accessing these areas are usually great, to give players an incentive to take the risks necessary in getting there. Gold caches are the areas of gold which are accessed after lengthy and risky excursions[/color].

Examine this map, by KinGAleX. The player can complete the level without going for this extra gold, but it provides an extra and exhilirating experience and allows for more replay value. Also, it allows the level to be enjoyed by players with different skill levels. A novice could attempt to complete the level without risking the mines and guasses for the extra gold, while the expert would probably attempt the extra challenge.

Having gold caches in your maps usually isn't about providing extra time to the player. It's more often about providing extra enjoyment and challenge and replay value to the map. Consider using gold caches in levels which you might feel are otherwise relatively simple, so that players of higher skill level can enjoy them as well.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Gold is one of the only objects in N that can serve the purpose of looking good, with no strings attached. Placing mines or bounceblocks to capture a certain artistic style will have a profound effect on the gameplay, as those objects affect the way the ninja moves and the places the ninja can go. Gold, however, does not obstruct or mobilize the ninja. Although gold certainly does have an effect on gameplay, the effect is not so strong that gold cannot be played around with--in an effort to create style.

Gold design can benefit from following some basic theories of style:

1) Line and repetition
2) Shape and pattern
3) Unity



Gold can be used to create lines on the map. Lines suggest imaginary borders and help move the eye along the interesting features of the map. Using gold to create lines can transform a flat tileset into something that resembles a figure. These lines can visually suggest an active or mobile environment, even though the objects themselves are static. When the ninja is moving quickly through the map, the gold will pop upward and the effect can help render the map into a dynamic artwork[/color]. Examine this map. The row of gold through the center makes the experience of playing the map all the more exciting.

The recurrence of lines is called repetition[/color]. Repetition can give a sense of connectedness within the map. Random gold placement and breaking of repetition can provide visual dissonance to the map. Although the map may play quite well, the broken and disjointed nature of the gold could render the map ugly. Much of the enjoyment in playing a map is getting to watch the ninja dance from object to object artfully. If the gold looks awkward and disconnected, this experience could be dulled. Repetition of lines, including the use of symmetry, help to create a good-looking and stylized map. Check out these lines. And some of these.



Shapes are fields or blocks of gold which are not path-like enough to be considered lines. Gold caches are practically required to come in shape form. Shapes, in themselves, can be artistic and help lock the focus of the eye on the design of the shape itself. The shape itself may even direct or push the gaze to slide across it in a natural progression, maybe even hinting at the route the ninja is intended to take. Examine this map. The shape of the gold in this map offer roundness, which contradicts the tileset in a unique manner. The slope of the gold even slightly suggests the arc of the ninja's jump as he moves through these areas.

Pattern is the repetition of shapes throughout a map. Pattern gives a sense of purpose to the map. It make even suggest that the areas of gold are of the same origin--that is to say, they are to be considered some sort of substance or component other than simply "gold". Pattern will allow the gold to take on a style or meaning beyond what is expected. Examine this map, which implies that the areas of gold are, perhaps, globes of light.



Unity is the overall sense of artistic purpose for the map. This is the hardest to describe, but is amazing when done properly. The gold placement in your map should match the overall style of the map. If your map feels "organic", your gold might be loose and stringy like leaves or vines. If your map feels "industrial", your gold might be sharp and linear or highly repetetive. THE MINE MANIFESTO
by blue_tetris



Mines. The most common deadly object used by map makers. Not only that, but they're typically responsible for more ninja deaths worldwide than all the drones and floorguards combined. Seriously. Check your number of deaths on N to see if it's the same for you.

To make a long story short, mines are useful. In creating this manifesto, I intend to instruct beginner map makers on how to get used to placing mines to effectively limit access and create challenge. I also intend to give helpful hints to even the most seasoned map makers about how to better understand the way they use mines. In learning the theories behind mines, I hope you can all end up using them more effectively.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
In this manifesto, just like the others before, we use the term "accessibility" to refer to how easily the ninja can enter a certain area. An area is highly accessible if the ninja can enter it and move around in it with ease. An area is poorly accessible if the ninja will have difficulty getting there or difficulty moving around in that area. An area is inaccessible if the ninja cannot reach the area or cannot do anything (or becomes stuck) when entering the area.

Access will be covered in greater depth in this manifesto, so let's stick to a good definition to help us along. Access is the general term used to describe the areas of the map into which the ninja can move and how easily the ninja can enter those areas.[/color]

Mobility will also be discussed. Mobility is the general term used to describe how easily the ninja can move.[/color] Different areas will provide different mobility for the ninja.

We'll split this topic up into a number of sections to make the topic easier to follow:

1) Access management
2) Mobility management
3) Aerial minefields
4) Surface minefields



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Access management is the process by which map makers reduce or increase access to the different areas of the map.[/color] Mine placement can greatly affect where the ninja can and cannot go. A strip of mines between regions of the map can prevent or add access. Many map makers aren't quite sure how far they can put mines away from each other to create a continuous barrier[/color]. Continuous barriers are the backbone of access management using mines.

It's important to note that mines don't need to be densely packed together to create a continuous barrier. It's also important to note that a skilled player can make use of even the smallest gap in the continuity of a mine barrier. So how far apart is too far apart? Let's take a look at this pic to clear things up:



The mines in example A are exactly one half of a grid space apart. These mines are too close together. Why? Because the middle mine is unnecessary and useless. The other two mines form a continuous barrier which can kill the ninja. As a map maker, you must eliminate all useless objects from the map.[/color] They simply add clutter to the appearance of the map and have no effect on the gameplay. Unless you think it actually makes the map look better, let's not use that middle mine.

What about example B? These mines are exactly one grid space apart. They form a continuous barrier. No more mines are needed. The ninja won't be able to slip through.

Example C also forms a continuous barrier. These mines are one grid space apart laterally and one half of a grid space apart vertically. The same idea applies to example D. The mines are one grid space apart vertically and one half a grid space apart laterally.

Example E does not form a continuous barrier. A ninja can slip diagonally between those mines with a little skill. These mines are one grid space apart laterally and vertically.

Example F, however, does form a continuous barrier. These mines are 3/4 of a grid space apart both laterally and vertically.

Now, you might be saying "I'm a veteran. I already know about all this." Did you know there's an easy mathematical way to tell if a mine barrier is continuous or not? Sure! It's simple. A mine barrier is continuous if the distance between each set of adjacent mines is:

no greater than one grid space laterally, no greater than one grid space vertically, and the sum of the lateral and vertical distances is less than (but not equal to!) two grid spaces.[/color]

Try it out! ^_~

Check out this map, which uses two continous barriers to prevent the ninja from accessing the left and right sides of the map. Notice how the barrier is continuous, yet it still flows and changes direction during its course.

Examine this map. At the center of the bottom, you'll see a continuous barrier of mines which prevents the ninja from moving from the left area to the right area through the gap in the tiles. Each of those areas is accessible, but the access is limited due to the placement of the mines. In order to enter those areas, the player must make an extra trip. You could say these areas have less accessibility, due to the placement of the mines. At the top of the map, mines are also used to prevent access to certain areas, until other conditions are met: the activation of the door switches.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Mobility management is the process by which map makers reduce or increase the ninja's ability to move around within the accessible areas of the map.[/color] Mine placement can greatly affect which surfaces and objects that the ninja can use to move around, thereby controlling mobility. Using continuous barriers can assist in mobility management.

The difference between access management and mobility management is that access management blocks off or separates entire areas of the map, while mobility management only prevents the usage of certain things which can mobilize the ninja. Placing mines typically does a little of both. No matter where you put a mine, some portion of the map will be less accessible and some of the ninja's freedom of movement will be taken away.

Examine this map. The mines around the perimeter of the map prevent the ninja from using the floor or walls to move around. The ninja will only be able to move around using the tiles and objects in the center of the map.

Take a look at this map. All of the floors in this map are scattered with mines. This severely limits the mobility of the ninja. The ninja is forced to mobilize by using the tiles hanging in the air. Take a look at the area near the end, at the bottom right. See anything wrong with the placement of the mines? If you notice carefully, you'll find a break in the continuity of the mines. The ninja has to make use of this gap to get maneuver through the level. In other words, the ninja has a small amount of mobility on the bottom tile and he has to make use of it.



~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Aerial minefields[/color] are regions of mines which serve the chief purpose of killing the ninja. Instead of being used to prevent access and mobility, these mines function to create a challenge. It is assumed that aerial minefields are regions which the ninja must move through to complete the level. Instead of trying to detour or redirect the ninja, they serve as a mechanism for gameplay.

The difference between mines and the other enemies is that mines demand delicate movement instead of rapid and responsive movement. The mines don't react to the ninja. The movement of the ninja has no impact on the status of the mines. That means, when it comes to mines, planning is king[/color]. Players will take some time to devise the best way to navigate the system of aerial mines before taking on the challenge. They are not compelled to move. The mines will wait for them.

Aerial minefields have two general properties: density[/color] and order[/color]. Density is the closeness of the mines within the minefield. An area with high density has less space for the ninja to pass through safely. Order is the tendency for mines to be in groups and have placement patterns. An area with high order has a more predictable or straight-forward path which the ninja must follow. An area of high order is not easier, it's simply requires less planning. The jump itself may require a great amount of delicate handling.

After these concepts are acknowledged, the best foundation for your minefield will be a process I like to call visualizing paths. This process involves plotting the general motion of the ninja required to navigate through each section of the minefield separately. It would be an arduous task to try to consider the entire minefield as a single entity to be traversed. Instead, consider the minefield to be a series of separate challenges. Visualize the path of the ninja for each jump and make sure that the jump seems reasonable, based on the physics of the game. Remember that a single jump can take the ninja beyond more than one mine, depending on the nature of the minefield.

In visualizing the path of the ninja, you only have to ensure that there is at least one path which the ninja can take to get through the minefield. After you've factored for this one path and fully created your minefield, try to visualize other possible paths that the ninja could take. If any of these paths seems too much easier than the path you plotted, you may consider adding more mines. If there's only one good path through the minefield, you may consider removing some mines to give players more options when trying to navigate the area.

Lastly, consider the other enemies. It's true that mines can work without any assistance from other enemies. However, part of the fun of playing N stems from managing all the variables and coming out on top. If you're working with a low density minefield which might be easy to negotiate, try throwing a gauss into the mix to keep the ninja on her toes. Throw in a laser drone to complicate the situation. Maybe even place a rocket launcher to really spice things up. Sometimes mines alone will not be enough of a challenge to hinder the ninja and more enemies will be necessary to acheive the same effect.

~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Surface minefields[/color] are scattered mines placed along tile surfaces to cause the ninja to use those surfaces in a specific fashion (in an effort to cause the ninja to die, if he makes a mistake). Like aerial minefields, surface minefields have density. The order of a surface minefield isn't important--the jumps will require equal amounts of precision regardless of the consistency of the pattern. In fact, order would be a bad thing in a surface minefield, because the jumps would most likely end up being too similar to each other and the gameplay would become repetetive.

Just as in aerial minefields, you may want to use the process of visualizing paths in order to create the perfect challenge. In visualizing paths using surface minefields, you'll likely end up considering destination points[/color] more closely. Destination points are the areas on the surfaces of the tiles which the ninja will move to after each jump.

Other than that, plan out your surface minefields just as you would with your aerial minefields. Bear in mind that surface minefields will allow the ninja more mobility while freefalling and making jumps. Also, consider the inclusion of additional enemies, based on the challenge of the minefield.

Sendy's Level Structure and Concept Manifesto (first draft)

In this guide I hope to give some general ideas and pointers to the factors which come into play in the overall structure of a level. Being aware of the spacetime topology of your level as you build it, and how that influences it's gameplay, is an essential design skill. If this guide gives just one person a small bit of inspiration, I will consider my job done.

Linearity vs. Nonlinearity[/u]

This is an age-old debate which has been running since the early 80's. As soon as the idea of coded levels was invented (as opposed to repeating stages that got faster or added more aliens, a la Space Invaders, Asteroids, etc), the obvious question was "do you force a fairly limited path for the player to follow, or do you give them multiple routes to explore?".

The generalized answer, is, of course, that either way can work, and there have been classic games and levels in both of these modes. Each mode has it's pros and cons, and I believe a good level designer should master both.

Level design in N is unlike any other platform game I've ever designed for, in that the movement in N is, by it's very nature, very open-ended anyway. All but the most strictly linear (and we're talking enclosed tunnels forcing the route, here) level will have a gamut of ways it can be approached. Some people may walljump and leap over a certain part. Others may use the bounceblock, and others still will do a triple-reverse corner kick (if indeed there is such a thing) off of a moving thwump and catapult themselves right to the end of your level. So, controlling the linearity factor involves being aware of the diverse range of moves available to the player. Try and watch as many demos as possible - experts and beginners and everyone inbetween.

The crux of this message is - look out for shortcuts. If you've designed a puzzle or a timing challenge which is the centerpiece of your level, the chances are you don't want people skipping right over it. But the process doesn't stop there. Sometimes these alternative routes are desirable and add depth to the map. An experienced mapmaker will have a trained eye to spot the different potential routes, how easy they are to take, and hence will position gold and impassible barriers (mine barricades, walls, one-ways) to shape the level into a dynamic affair with little to no trivial design features. A well-designed shortcut may allow a player to skip some of the level, but at the cost of being very tricky to do and/or not being able to get some gold. You get the idea.

Now you know the special-case rules for N (or have a vague idea of them), you can make a decision on the level of linearity you'd like in your level.

Linear levels:

Linear levels have a sense of progression and composition to them which is satisfying in it's-self. Usually, the player is forced to pass all of the challeneges in sequence and therefore you may get more use out of your object placement. Furthermore, in a decent linear course, there is a microcosm of sub-routes the ninja can take, and so we see that a good linear level is not linear at all, apart from in appearance and basic concept.

Making linear levels is generally (much!) easier than making nonlinear ones, because there are less possibilities for player routing and hence outcome. Effectively it's easier to manage the time dimension of a linear level. However, when using linearity you should be very aware of the difficulty contour you are creating. This is the hardest part of making a good linear map - getting the balance between thrilling and frustrating the player. If you make a very hard part, or a part that requires a skill that the player doesn't have, they have no choice but to pass through it, which could lead to problems and the player quitting.

A good strategy is to put more difficult challenges at the beginning, and have challenge peter out towards the end as excitement at beating the level mounts. Obviously, though, sticking to this plan would become predictable and samey, so I use it only when I'm more unsure of my design intentions.

Nonlinear levels:

The possibility for these kinds of levels in N is immense, due to the complexity and intricacy of the ninja's movements and interactions with terrain and objects. Anyone who's made a DDA knows how the tiniest detail in timing and motion can affect the ninja's trajectory. Nonlinear levels are less frustrating and generally more fun. You feel free, master of your own destiny as you hop around the level accepting and avoiding challenges as your skill permits.

Designing a good, fluid nonlinear level in N takes some skill and familiarity, since you need to consider each area of the map and what direction the player may enter it from, not to mention which objects may be in which state of play at that point in time - for example : a thwump might have thwumped and be slinking back into place, a rocket may have fired and followed the player into this part of a map, the player may or may not have triggered switch or trapdoor x, y, or z, a stray seeker drone may be complicating matters. All of these need to be considered when balancing the difficulty of a nonlinear map.

But don't let that put you off.

One tool you have at your disposal is forcing a certain amount of linearity. Your map may consist of four seperate challenges in isolation which can be completed in any order. There may be several sub-sections to your otherwise nonlinear map which are linear and even are one-way systems (enforced by one-ways, doors, or even mines). This will give your level structure as well as making the time dimension of your map easier to manage. Breaking your map down into hermetic sections sealed with normal doors or easy 'safe zones' which allow the ninja to shake off any following dangers such as rockets or drones, before moving onto the next section, may also help.

Things which can provide extra player routing are:

- Sloped floors (especially the gently sloped '3' tiles) allow the ninja to jump much higher - providing access to any routes above. Other types of slopes may be used for double-jumping off of and reaching much further than a basic jump. Be aware how tiles guide the ninja around the map in various types of flight.
- Thwumps can act as lifts and moving platforms to distant parts of the map.
- Doors can be opened (or not) if a switch has been triggered. How difficult is the switch to trigger? What are the rewards for the player? How difficult/rewarding is the route opened by the switch? Is this route essential to completion of the level?
- Bounceblocks can provide the player with exceptionally long and high jumps. They can also be jumped off of from the side (walljumped).
- Launchpads are kindof obvious
- There are others, and many combinations.

Nonlinearity is by it's very nature highly complicated and is a more advanced subject of map-making. In writing I can only scratch the surface and perhaps give you some ideas. The true mastery can only come through nonverbal understanding of how it works, and lots of experience in building maps and playing yours and other people's maps (good and bad). In the past I have made many attempts at making good, open-ended maps, and failed. Only after about two years mapping experience do I feel comfortable with the challenge.

There-and-Backs[/u]

Touted by some (and often correctly) as the bain of N, 'there-and-backs' are situations where the ninja starts at or near the exit, and the exit switch is at the end of an (often quite long-ish) course of any type.

The there-and-back's cheif benefit is psychological. There's nothing quite like the sense of venturing out into the danger and then returning 'home'. Many or all of the obstacles will need to be crossed twice, once in each direction. This also opens an interesting design challenge - making your level dynamic and right in both directions.

Done right, a there-and-back is an adrenaline pumping rush. Done wrong, or too often, and it'll have you reaching for your NES emulator faster than you can say "It's-a-me, Mario!". And as I see it, the line between good and bad is quite distinct.

First there is the difficulty: Because there-and-backs are generally longer than most levels, you need to remember to lower the difficulty to compensate. Going 'there and back' through a hail of bullets, rockets and lazer fire is more soul-destroying than anything else. Typically I find going 'back' should be easier than going 'there', though by no means is this always the case (consider the infamous but lovable Trench Run from episode 5 of N). That way the hard work is got out of the way first, and as you return the difficulty is provided by the players nerves, knowing she must now get 'home' and with one mistake she'll be right back to the start.

You can make the return trip easier with clever positioning of level elements, but sometimes that's not enough. To further aid this cause, you can try adding advantages on the way back such as short-cuts past hard bits, jump-pads to aid vertical climbs which were drops on the first leg, or platforms made of trap-doors which appear over difficult jumps, neutralizing them for the return journey. As a last resort, consider closing some turrets off with trap-doors. Sometimes this looks very tacky but occasionally it works for the good of the level. Note that when you do this, it's neat-looking to put the turret in a 'notch' in the wall which you can seal off with one or two trapdoors, as using multiple trapdoors can often look even tackier.

The second thing to consider is what the there and back gives a level. Since almost any level can be a there-and-back with minimal editing, there needs to be a good reason. It might enhance the mood of the level. For example, you may start above ground and need to descend into some caves, get the switch, and then spelunk your way back to the tunnel mouth to the exit on the surface. Another example is 'infiltrating' a building and escaping. Providing an open choice of routes for both legs of the journey can reduce the linearity and severity of the there-and-back. A player can take one route there, and the other coming back, and try different routes to find the one that suits his style and abilities the most. Bear in mind, though, that as the nonlinearity increases, it loses it's identity as a there-and-back. An open-styled map with the exit and start near the bottom and a switch up top is hardly what I would term as a there-and-back.

The level might be very easy and thus need this extra challenge. The jumps might work very differently in one direction to the other, providing a technical showcase. Make sure there is a good reason for the there-and-back because I for one can vouch for the reduncancy of adding it into levels willy-nilly.

Also, there is the 'round trip' there-and-back, where the 'back' leg of the journey uses a completely different route to the 'there'. The order may be enforced with switches or one-ways, or left open to the player to decide. This variant doesn't have quite the same psychological connotations as the straight there-and-back, but can sometimes be more suitable to the level you want to create.

Finally there are there-and-back-and-theres, or there-and-back-and-there-and-backs, ad nauseam, which are made by nesting doors and switches in a certain way. The golden rule is that these should be avoided AT ALL COSTS unless the level is trivially short and will evolve (such as drones changing phase or a slowly drifting lazer drone).

Have a go at making maps with some of these strategies. Done right, they are one of my favourite kinds of map.

What is a DDA?[/u]

"DDA" stands for Dont Do Anything. A DDA is a map that the player watches as the ninja is moved around by various means. Most DDAs have the ninja finish the level, but some dont, and either purposely kill the ninja off, or make him move around the level endlessly. Some DDAs are more complicated than just a ninja moving around by himself. For example, some maps require the player to hold right or left for the ninja to complete the level. Other variations on the DDA are maps that, for example, keep a rocket alive the entire time, or use only one type of propulsion. Propulsion is what keeps the ninja moving, and without it, very little could happen in a DDA.

Demo Does Everything (DDE)

This is by no means the definitive list; all these different types of DDA can be combined, to make all sorts of different DDAs.

Propulsion[/u]

Propulsion is the mechanism that keeps the ninja moving in a DDA so he can travel from A to B. Unlike a normal map, the player does not control the ninja, so the objects in the level have to move the ninja instead. Here is an overview of the most used types of propulsion:

Tiles

It goes without saying that nearly every DDA involves the ninja interacting with tiles. Tiles change the ninjas velocity upon collision. When colliding with tiles the ninja will either change direction or speed, depending on the angle of the tile and the angle of collision. Tiles can also give the ninja a safe place to land if he is falling from a large height. Tiles also add a protection value to the ninja; he is less likely to be hit by enemies if he is protected by tiles. Tiles can also be used to change the ninja's speed. If a ninja is placed inside a tile, it pushes him out, adding to his speed (this is normally used at the beginning of a DDA to create an exciting, high-speed aspect). Consider the following map.

In this map, every time the ninja collides with a tile it changes his velocity, either changing his direction, his speed, or both. The tiles have been placed to manipulate the movement of the ninja so that he avoids the rocket and the mines.

Example

In this map, the ninja is propelled into some tiles, which then propel him out, into the next tiles. Some of the tiles trap the ninja in a smaller are than he is supposed to be able to fit into, squeezing him, so he gains speed. The tiles also change his direction, allowing him to make turns in the map. The ninja was pushed through thin areas of tile, as to avoid risk of death, yet at greater speeds it's possible to go through larger areas of tiles, because the game is based on frames, and the ninja's velocity can be expressed mathematically in pixels per frame. If he's going fast enough, in one frame he can go from one side of a seemingly impassible tile to the other. This is called teleportation, and it will be discussed in more depth in the launch pad section.

Launch Pads

Launch pads are the most common methods of propulsion, probably because they are by far the easiest to use and manipulate. They instantly change the ninjas speed to a set value (based on the power of the pad, discussed later), meaning the ninja can around a large distance without much thought involved in the placement of the launch pad. They also instantly change the ninjas direction, so they can be used to make very close calls (also discussed later) for the ninja. Consider this map.

Example

The launch pads in this map are used to move the ninja out of danger at the last possible moment, and to push him into a safe area. A launch pad only needs to be placed exactly where the ninja is going to be, and it means that timing is not an issue, as with some other propulsions. A launch pad can also be used as a way to safely slow down a ninja who is travelling at a great speed in any direction, because (like I said before) a launch pad sets a ninjas velocity to a certain level, it doesnt simply add to it. That said, it is possible to change the speed that a launch pad will set for the ninja. A launch pads code looks like this.
2^396,468,0,-1
The first number (as with any object code) is the identification number of the launch pad. The second represents the launch pad's x-coordinate (this value begins at 0 for the very left of the screen and can go up to 768 for the very right). The third represents the pad's y-coordinate (this value begins at 0 for the top and goes up to 576 for the bottom). Every object has these three numbers at the beginning of their code. The following numbers represent the special attributes of whatever object it is. For a launch pad, there are two. The first (the 0) represents the x-component of the speed setting (how fast the ninja will move horizontally once he hits the pad). A negative value represents left, while a positive value represents right. Because the code in question has a value of 0 there, you know it will be a vertical launch pad. The second number (big surprise) represents the y-component of speed. A negative value represents up, while a positive represents down. So, we're looking at the code for a standard, upward-facing launch pad.
By changing the last two numbers, one can change the speed the launch pad will set the ninja to, in either direction. If the speed set is great enough, it is possible to teleport the ninja through objects, enemies or tiles. However, after this, there is generally something needed to slow down the ninja, otherwise he will die from high-speed collision or go flying out of the border and never return. It takes a while to get the schematics for teleporters just right, but the teleporter tutorial might help. Here's a map that shows a mastery of launch pad use.

Trap Door

Aside from the way trap doors can propel the ninja, a trap door (or locked or even normal) can be used in the same way as a tile, to change the ninjas velocity on collision, or offer him protection from most enemies.
Now, on to propulsion:
A trap door can be used to speed the ninja up in whatever direction he is going, or change his direction completely. To speed him up, a specific method is used, combining the switch and the door. A general rule of thumb for creating trap door propulsion to speed the ninja up is to place the key 3/4 of a space (using C-snap) from the door (see diagram). This increase in speed happens because the ninja hits the switch, thus locking the door, while some of his body is still behind where the door appears. When this happens, just like with tiles, the ninja is pushed out, and it adds to his speed. The more of the ninja behind the door when the switch is activated, the faster the ninja will be ejected.

Normal Door + Trap Door + Locked Door

This method of propulsion is more complicated. Firstly, to understand it, you must understand that if a trap door is created or a locked door is placed on top of a normal door, the set of doors acts as one normal door, allowing the ninja to pass, but deterring (most) enemies. However, if the ninja activates a key (either opening a locked door or creating a trap door) while he is partially in the door, he will be projected out. However, the door still acts like a normal door, and so he may pass through it again. This happens because when the ninja hits the switch, for the one frame that the door is unlocked (locked door) or created (trap door), the ninja interacts with it like a normal door, pushing him out. Thwumps

Thwumps are one of the most versatile objects that can be used in a DDA, and effective, and a popular method. In terms of propulsion, they can be used like tiles to change the ninjas velocity, they can be used to push the ninja, and they can be used to expel the ninja at high-speed by squeezing him between the thwump and a tile or another object. A major advantage to thwumps is that they can also be used multiple times, without creating another object or employing gold delay (discussed later) but they can be difficult to use because they move around, so they need to placed so that they move the ninja when wanted, and dont interact with the ninja when unwanted.
Firstly, the thwump moving the ninja like a tile is self-explanatory. If the ninja collides with a thwump, he changes velocity. If he finds himself inside a thwump for a frame, he is expelled out with force (similarly to with tiles or doors). Secondly, if the ninja hits the correct side of a retreating thwump, he is pushed along with the thwump. This can be used to push the ninja into objects, and then squeeze him out. How to Make False Tiles

Making false tiles is fairly simple.
Step 1: Pick the space you want your tile to be.
By playtesting the map, youll know where the ninja will be in his path, and be able to decide where is the most effective space on which to place this tile.
Step 2: Place any tile you wish there.
I use E tiles.
Step 3: Return to the main menu and look at your level data.
Home, Page Down, ~. Also, I suggest highlighting the data to look at it, just to save you time, because the gray on white is not terribly easy on the eyes.
Step 4: Find the data for the tile you placed, and replace it with one of the false tile codes.
If you used an E tile, the code will appear as a 1. Refer to the next subdivision of the Manifesto for what codes create false tiles.

Close calls are, as their name would suggest, a point in time in which the ninja is in extreme danger of being struck by a given enemy; a time in which the user may question the ninjas fortuity to escape the danger. The biggest problem with enemies in a DDA and the reason many people shy away from using them to their full potential is that adding enemies can cause problems. Once youve added an enemy, and it works perfectly in the DDA, you can add another and have the whole thing go down the toilet. However, there is a trick to making it possible to add enemies without shooting yourself if the foot. The old method of this was to simply add all of your enemies at the beginning, and then shape the path of the DDA around them. This map is probably the best example of this.

This is a good, fool-proof way of making a DDA, but if you want to go with the more add as you go approach, a simple method can be employed. When you want to add an enemy, do nothing after you add it. Then go to the main menu, and highlight the code of the last object you placed (at the end of the code). You should know how much of it is the code for the one object from the earlier discussions of code editing. Cut that and paste it at the beginning.
