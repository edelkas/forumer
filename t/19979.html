#376287 3 2007-11-02T16:10:38Z Raigan
hi,
you might have already seen this, but definitely the best place to start is to try the original stick-based method of Jakobsen (see section 5, fig4a,4b here http://www.teknikus.dk/tj/gdc2001.htm)
this is probably your best bet, it's pretty simple and works well.

collision detection is much more involved, you'll always want to collide the end of one stick vs. a point on the other stick. to figure out which stick's end and where on the other stick, you can use sweep tests or static intersection tests.

you can sweep the sticks against each other (sweep each stick's endpoints vs the other stick's lineseg, see this thread http://bulletphysics.com/Bullet/phpBB3/vie....php?f=4&t=1041)

for static intersection tests, you want to intersect two linesegs. here's a page with an explanation of this: http://softsurfer.com/Archive/algorithm_01...orithm_0101.htm

one way to do this is to compare the endpoints of one stick vs the line containing the other stick. lets call your sticks sA=p0,p1, sB=p2,p3. if p0 and p1 lie on opposite sides of the line passing through p2,p3, AND p2,p3 lie on opposite sides of the line passing through p0,p1, the sticks are intersecting. try this on paper to see how it works. you can use the formula for the signed area of the triangles (p2,p3,p0),(p2,p3,p1),(p0,p1,p2),(p0,p1,p3) to figure out what points on on which sides.

i'm sorry for not providing more info, there's too much to say! i would suggest inflating the sticks into "capsules", and then use distance queries rather than intersection tests. really thin objects are hard to collide since it's so easy for them to spin through each other, and most of the time you don't really want to model objects that are that thin anyway.

i'm sorry i can't write more but we're crazy busy today -- definitely try to check out some free physics engines such as (in flash) APE, fisix, etc as they all have this functionality and source code to learn from.

good luck!
