The office window stood ajar, letting the cold, high altitude wind stream through the executive office. It trickled through the blinds, which bounced and shook in playful mimicry of the shaking, huddled form amongst them. Johnson sat near the edge of the window, facing slightly away from it, as though this slim angle would lessen the menace of a seven-story drop. The wind buffeted around him, at times making him rock and sway dangerously close to the edge, but he seemed oblivious to his surroundings as he hugged his knees. Stray documents floated on the breeze, littering the office with trivialities.

The door burst open, a stunned individual in the doorway. He slowly took in the situation; paper floated past, a chair lay sprawled on the ground, recently overturned and Johnson, a colleague of years past, cowered near the gaping window. Spotting the hunched figure, the intruder became more distressed. He leaned back into the hallway, searching both directions for any sign of help. Finding none, he swallowed hard and finally let go of the door handle. As he prepared to enter further into the office, a voice rang out, “Fuck off Davis.” There was no malice in the voice; it was a level, clear statement. For a moment, Davis seemed to be considering Johnson’s advice, and then again realised the severity of the situation. The wind fluttered his tie, and it danced upon his chest.

They remained frozen for several minutes; Johnson completely apathetic of his visitor, and Davis overwhelmed, and at somewhat of a loss as to his next move. Davis edged carefully inside, and slowly closed the door. After this decisive action, he again lost his nerve, and stood, silently shuffling near the door. Johnson at last noticed him and looked around. He spared Davis a look of mild contempt, and then returned his attention to his feet and the nearby abyss. “Johnson?” ventured Davis warily. Receiving no answer, he took two steps forward, and tried again, with a little more insistence, “Johnson?!”

Johnson, resigned to his friend’s persistence, slowly span around, so that his back faced the opening. Having gotten Johnson’s attention, Davis was once more unsettled. The usual conversation starters seemed clumsy in this context. Noticing his co-workers impatience, he settled on “The very first bomb dropped by the Allies on Berlin during World War II killed the only elephant in the Berlin Zoo.” Davis gave this a moment to sink in, as Johnson raised an eyebrow, entirely taken aback by this statement. Finally, unable to withhold his curiosity, he asked, “And how does that relate to our given situation?” Again, Davis, who seemed much more at ease and in control of the situation, paused. Then said, “What you’re contemplating seems almost as pointless.”

Several more minutes past, as Davis marvelled at his own genius, smiling softly. Johnson let his head rest on his knees, warily eying his associate, also impressed with what had been said. He spoke, still with flat intonation, “Quite an assumption you made there.” Davis, who had been staring out the window, his eyes following a stray letter that had wafted outside and now fluttered lazily downwards, snapped his attention back to Johnson. He continued smiling, obviously pleased to have broken the ice and said rather jovially, “Well it wasn’t that far a jump, seeing you crouched by the window.” Johnson nodded slowly, though perhaps not to this comment, but to an unspoken idea that just now ran through his thoughts.

“I did not mean your rationale that I intend to kill myself, I referred to your assumption that death is pointless,” Johnson replied. This remark shattered Davis’s sense of satisfaction, and the smile left his face, replaced with a thoughtful frown. “But where are my manners, please, have a seat,” continued Johnson. Again, Davis appeared discouraged; an impression that was heightened when he again noticed that the only chair was upturned. Realising that Johnson was waiting for him to seat himself, he hurriedly moved towards the chair, clumsily righting it. Wheeling it over, he sat, and gazed at his companion. More time passed, as Davis carefully chose his words, feeling that another astute observation was expected.

Johnson remained motionless, prepared to allow his guest all the time needed in which to respond. Abruptly, Davis broke the silence, “How can taking your own life be anything other than pointless?” He seemed concerned, and the frown had not left his face. He peered intently at Johnson, as sweat trickled from his brow. Davis’s obvious agitation amused his host.

Johnson shrugged, in no hurry to provide an answer. His steely calm was off-putting, given the gravity of the situation. Davis had expected outrage or depression, and having received only nonchalance, was confused and nervous. A car alarm pierced the silence, drifting upwards from the street below. Johnson appeared to return to the present, and smiled absentmindedly at Davis.

“My friend, my life is all but finished anyway.” Johnson voiced. Davis did not like this verbal dance, which continued to dodge the issue at hand. He did not want to continue this farce any longer.

“How can you know that from this side of death?” Davis asked, almost angrily. This resentment put Johnson off balance for the first time. He didn’t seem to understand the fervour in his guest’s tone. Johnson narrowed his eyes, as they flickered over Davis’s face, searching for an explanation.

“I don’t expect you to understand, nor did I expect you to care.” Johnson said quite candidly. Davis again felt the awkwardness of the situation, and shifted uneasily in his seat. Johnson smiled. A grin that voiced an understanding known only to Johnson; it spoke of truths uncovered and the satisfaction gained thereafter. He rose suddenly, and Davis followed suit.

“Do not think ill of me.” Johnson said, putting particular emphasis on the last words he would ever speak.

He raised his arms like wings, and fell backwards, into the void. A soft thud drifted up, as Davis rushed to the window. A crowd gathered below, eager to partake in the spectacle. Davis staggered back to the door and hurried out, no longer comfortable in the dead man’s office. The wind blew the door shut, producing a dull sound that echoed, reminiscent of its owner’s recent demise.
