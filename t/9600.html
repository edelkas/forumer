#174793 17 2006-05-12T00:55:26Z blue_tetris
Without motivation for resources, people do very little. A free-resource community only works if there's a chance that members will be excommunicated for idleness.

Take a work group. Each member of the workgroup, by default, will receive equal pay, regardless of how much work they do individually. Provided their work project gets done, there is no motivation for the individual member. Therefore, management is necessary to create motivation. The default work state is somewhat like a communism. It makes it easier to allocate human resources and monetary resources under these procedures. Management turns this near anarchy into something... well... managable. You need someone in charge to deprive the resources of the amotivational portion. Although no member of the group actually exchanges resources (as in a capitalism), resource allocation is transformed to be equitable with performance--rather than for having a head.

In a true communism, management would be unnecessary. However, lack of motivation creates a feeling of inequity for those who are essentially competing harder for equal resources. Because, by definition, resources are scarce, people who cannot unify will compete. The likelihood of people being able to unify is directly inverse to the number of people in the group. With 3 to 6 people, group cohesion and unification is very high. They barely need any management to motivate them. With 7 to 15, you need a group manager. With 16 to 50, you need a front line manager. Any greater than that, you need (yes, need) multiple managers in a hierarchical system to allocate human and monetary resources--otherwise, you'll end up with 10% of the people doing 90% of the work.

That's typically how societies work, though. People complain about capitalism because 10% of the people get 90% of the resources. In practice, though, usually 10% of the people do 90% of the work. With a communism, the relationship between work and resource allocation often becomes inequitable. With capitalism, it's moderately equitable, but totally unmanaged. With some degree of socialism, it can be both equitable and managed

Socialism is the degree to which resources have been made social. For instance, in Canada, health care has been socialized. This means that the health resource is provided socially regardless of work. It is assumed that health and work don't need to be as equitable as, say, luxury and work. If everything is socialized, the government (or whoever is in charge) is essentially telling us that our work does not need to be equitable with any resource whatsoever. That does not stimulate work, which, in turn, does not stimulate the acquisition of resources.

From one of my previous discussions with B_T:

Quote: Atilla, 2005-10-02T09:42:13Z
Ironically, the Liberal Party are the conservatives in Australia.

A brief overview of Australian politics, for anyone who cares:

Australia has a bicameral system at both state (Legislative Assembly / Legislative Council) and federal levels (House of Representitives / Senate). We also have local councils. The leader of the Federal Government is called the Prime Minister. The PM is backed up by the Cabinet (or front-bench), members of his/her party whom he has allocated a portfolio (such as Treasurer, Minister of Defence, etc). The next largest party forms the Opposition, led by the Opposition Leader (duh) and the Shadow Cabinet (Shadow Treasurer, etc). The Queen's representative (Australia is a constitutional monarchy) is called the Governer General, and proceedings in Parliament are controlled by the Speaker. The same applies at state level, except that the leader of the Government is the Premier, and the Queen's rep is just the Governer.

The Coalition consists of the Liberal Party and National Party (formerly Country Liberal Party). The Nationals focus on rural seats. The Liberals are much more powerful and hold mostly urban and suburban seats. The coalition is moderate conservative, and their strengths are traditionally economic management and growth, fast and on-budget completion of major projects, and the encouragement of business, trade and free enterprise. It is traditionally supported by business owners, white-collar workers and the upper class. They usually get voted out because the public feels they are not supporting public services enough, or that they are not helping the lower classes enough. The Coalition is currently in federal Government, and has been since 1996.

The Labor Party (ALP) forms the Opposition, since it is the only other party which has a hope of taking government. It is traditionally supported by blue-collar workers, the lower classes (except for small business owners, who like the Coalition) and trade unionists. Labor is generally popular for increasing funding to public services and welfare, and increasing the rights of employees. They usually get voted out for economic incompetence, excessive union strikes, and inablility to complete projects on time or on budget. Although they have traditionally been moderate Left, Labor has wobbled towards conservatism in recent years, and started focusing more on the middle classes. There has also been a great deal of infighting between various factions within the party, which hasn't helped their election prospects. The ALP holds every state government.
