#265447 7 2006-12-06T06:19:20Z Atilla
My worldview is closest to empirical agnostic atheism, also known as "negative agnosticism" and "weak agnosticism". I have not been given reason to believe that the existence of gods is more or less likely than the alternative. Therefore, I consider the most rational stance is to withhold judgment. I also consider that the probability of any specific religion being reasonably accurate is quite small. I do not pretend to know what happens after death, other than the obvious physical effects. I have some leanings towards apathetic agnosticism (the contention that whether gods exist or not is largely academic and irrelevant to daily life). I've never really felt that gods influence my life; they exist, they appear to be largely uninterested in me, or at the very least have a policy of non-interference.

I do not claim to know why the universe exists. However, once it *did* exist, I believe it (mostly) adhered to certain physical laws, which we have modelled through scientific means. I therefore think that, more likely than not, the universe as we know it expanded from a point about 13 billion years ago. I consider everything beyond this point speculation; you can say it appeared spontaneously or that God created it, but I don't think you can prove either.

I am a moral relativist. I judge what is ethical based on the ramifications not only of individual actions, but the underlying reasons or philosophy. For example, while enforcing the law may not have the most desirable consequences in a specific case, in the long term laws must be applied consistently if people are to respect and understand them. I also recognise that, as a human, I have ingrained sentimental tendencies, and that my judgments are unlikely to be totally objective. It is my opinion that such tendencies likely developed as a mechanism which encourages cooperation between humans, and thus increases their chances of survival, in accordance with the principles of selection.

My worldview? It's pretty complex.

Worldview Name: Empirical Agnostic Theist, Objective Observationalist, Secular Pantheist/Nontheist (Self-term: Universalist)

Foreword: First off, I most readily classify myself as a Non-Theist (Formerly Anti-Theist, prior to that, strict Atheist), due simply to the depth of each term not fully classifying who I am or what I believe. Although, it isn't quite as simple as that, I do believe in something, although I'm not sure what that something is, and that's where the Pantheist/Theist ideals come from. Tonight, I think I've done a halfway fair job of classifying a part of what I believe in the above, but calling myself a strict Universalist seems to encompass enough of the smattering of my ideals so as to provide it with credence as a simple tag. Good freakin' lord. As extensive as this is, it's only scratching the surface. With that said, enjoy, and if you have any questions, feel free to ask.

Nature of God: The nature of a deity cannot currently be known. Separation of belief and observation should be key in how we live. What we can observe isn't any more truth than what we believe, but what we believe involves faith, and should be separated from what we can observe, as what we can observe is tangible to us in the moment, and the six senses that we can use (I consider thought a sense) are the only ways that we can perceive the world around us, and thus, are the best tools that we, as humans, can use to do so.

I consider myself marginally more enlightened than the average human, by my nature, although I have no explanation or train of thought as to why. But things that everyday people cannot wrap their heads around, I can do easily. On occasion, I get flashes of insight, I suppose you would call them, into what I can only refer to (For lack of an actual phrase to call it) as the very nature of the universe. It is not anything rational, logical, or contiguous, but rather, a visceral response to thought processes that occur in my mind. The processes don't so much provide me with words, ideals, phrases, or images, it feels as though they are more closely related to an inexplicable connection to the Universe itself. It's as though I am constantly connected to an otherworldly entity whose energy I can sense, or feel, sort of in the way a dog can hear things outside of the perceptible range of humans, and how certain telescopes can measure the UV spectrum, I feel as though I have an otherworldly sensory connection to something on a slightly different plane than others do. Most often, I refer to this entity simply as "Universe," as I believe that the Universe is, in some ways, self-sustaining, and it would not shock me if there were some "intelligence" to it (Whether said intelligence is one of rational thought, or simply a naturalistic, reactionary force). Amongst the processes that I can explain are a time sense and what I feel is a universal directional compass. I know what direction I am facing at all times, but not just in relation to North, South, East, or West, but in relation to the world as a whole, to the sun, the other planets, and so on. I seem to be able to mentally step outside of myself for brief periods in order to envision myself in a more universally spatial manner. In this form, I borrow from tenets of Naturalistic Pantheism, in that I see the Universe as something other than the purely physical, but not necessarily as something divine or intelligent. If you're not confused by now, kudos, because I think I just confused myself.

Akin to Atilla, I have no idea how the Universe began. I feel that there is something outside of the Universe, but as to what, I am not sure. I can envision what feels to be something larger than our Universe, and I think it an interesting concept that the Universe continues larger and larger, into infinity, the universe that we are in comprising but a single atom inside a single molecule in a living or inanimate object far larger, which in turn is encased in a similar universe, and so on. I enjoy the phrase, "A Universe within us all," to illustrate this, but again, it is not so much a theory as it is simply a feeling. There are certain doctrines, usually called "Multiverse" doctrines, that explain this more in-depth, but I don't really adhere to one or another in particular. I also feel that once the Universe came into its current form, that the observable physical laws that we have tested to be true are, at least in some form, correct. As I said elsewhere, mathematics works because it is provable. Physics works because it is provable. But the reason that the two Sciences are provable is because the methods we use to go about proving them are relative. The Arabic numeral 2 is a completely arbitrary term, and thus 2 + 2 = 4 means literally nothing without the language and the connotation behind it, but when looking at the idea from a higher thought standpoint, one can see that two apples and two more apples makes the number of apples that is represented by four. Whether the characters used to depict that are Arabic or Roman, the philosophical backing is there.

I am not so sure that the Universe is expanding, I feel that Existence theory is a very valid theory to explain the Universe, and not to ignore the ideals that are presented to call it into question, but for the idea that there may be causes beyond that infinity that could be the missing link to our equation. but I also feel that we, as Humans, may not ever have the answers to the origins of our existence, and thus, may be forced to settle for what we currently have at the time of our demise. The human ideal is one of pure wisdom and understanding, but even beyond that, to a transcendent thought where proofs and observation are no longer required. I feel very strongly that we, as humans, cannot claim anything to be entirely true beyond any reasonable doubt, because what we know is relative to our experiences here on this small, insignificant speck of the Universe. What we can know is what may work for us while we are here, and while our situation is such that we cannot know anything about the remainder of the Universe through observation, those things that are true for us now must always be under scrutiny, and must always be willing to be malleable and willing to change and update.

Thus, I also consider the most rational stance in the area of observation is to withhold judgment. In the area of belief, I choose to believe in some sort of higher power, although I am not certain what, who, why, where, or how the being came into existence, what it wants, if it has goals or thoughts or incentive. It is no more absurd to believe that we came about randomly than it is to believe that we came about through intelligent design, but of the two choices, my preference would rather stray towards belief in something larger than myself. Not for fear of divine punishment or retribution, but for the sense that there is something more intelligent than I; More wise than I; And who does have all of the answers.

I also fully believe that if there is a God, it is not a figurehead of miracles and human intervention or creation - it is rather something incomprehensible by the human mind, without form or thought, motivated purely by something completely intangible by you or I, that I can best refer to as its 'nature.' Nature of Death: The nature of life and death for humans is the same as any other living or cyclical organism or function that exists in the universe. Stars are born and die, planets are born and die, humans are born and die, and the nature of everything is quite cyclical. I am still unsure as to the idea of a soul, but I have begun to classify and structure the ideas and tenets of thought, which I compare most closely to the hierarchy of matter that we currently look at in our studies of the physical. I have termed this idea to be called "Ellisance," and to explain it would require an explanation of the Universe itself, but here are the basics:

The world can be divided quite easily into two areas: Physical and tangible things, and things that are not so easily classified. Emotions, intents, thoughts, chaos, entropy, willpower, even the unspoken laws that govern the universe would be categorized under the notion of Ellisance. Nature of Right and Wrong: Right and wrong are completely relative terms that only come into play when we speak of will and intent; In doing so, and in characterizing God, we can attribute right and wrong to God itself. Thus, I believe that making the concepts of Good and Evil relative to things that have willpower or intent or motives is a necessity. For instance, is it wrong for an asteroid to impact a planet and destroy the life there? Absolutely not - It would be chance. The asteroid had no motivations to kill, or to destroy, something was simply in its path. Is it wrong that a star supernovas, regresses, and forms a black hole? Not at all. It is a function of what the star is, and one would assume that it is a necessary function of what the universe is, since it seems to be naturally occurring in the cyclical productions of what the star is.

Thus, right and wrong are closely tied to will and intent, and will and intent are, as far as we know, organic and relative. But right and wrong seem to contrast with instinct and nature, for an animal who kills for food is only doing what comes naturally to it, and is not necessarily committing a "wrong" act, despite robbing its prey its right to live. It's particularly important to make the distinction between right and wrong in the area of what is natural, and it doesn't seem as though human law and human thought can correctly achieve that balance. As is illustrated in the sacred texts we have around the world, there are certain ideals of social right and wrong, but I am not sure if those social norms and expectations can be attributed to natural things, either.

For instance, cruelty to an animal can be portrayed quite differently than cruelty to a human, and we tend to give ourselves preferential status over other organisms on the planet due to our ingenuity, and the notions of right and wrong can be quite heavily debated as to their origins, purposes, and execution, and as to whether such laws can be universal, or whether they can only apply to humans, or whether they should be wholly flexible, and interpreted on a case-by-case basis. Of course, human err plays largely into this as well, and could never succeed with perfection. Thus, as a student of law and logic, but also as a free-thinker and a theologian, I tend to become torn between what is logical and what is inherently apparent.

Nature of Origins: Where we came from? Honestly, I have no clue. I don't pretend to know. I do know that we can't know, and the best shot that we have at explaining where we came from is to understand and analyze what we can observe, and classify the things that we can while we have the capacity and the ability, but at the same time, to not rule out faith as an answer for why we *can* observe. I tend to look at the notion of Universe as all-encompassing, with observational sciences falling underneath, as though the Big Bang is a valid cause for *our* immediate existence, Evolution is a valid cause for our even *more* immediate existence, but the possibility of an intelligent power setting those things into motion being fully within the realm of reason. Thus, I don't ever feel as though science and religion or science and belief systems have to be at odds, because the explanations that we come up with, scientifically, may be entirely correct, but also, those explanations could have unseen causes *themselves*. Thus, for us, science cannot be wrong so long as it is provable and have functional applications (For instance, mathematics and influences of electricity, computing, and so on), but insofar as Science being used to prove or disprove a given cause, or to further a given motive, it can't much be used as such.

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarcho-capitalism

This wikipedia article should take care of all the basic problems you guys are having.

One thing to keep in mind is that while all these ideas probably strike you as crazy at first, when given just a bit of consideration, I think you'll find that they make perfect sense. That doesn't mean that you will like or agree with the outcome or the consequences, but as a system, it's perfectly viable.

For instance, to answer Kablizzy's question, what would happen to a guy who murdered someone? Private defense and courts can take care of this problem. There's nothing about the nature of policing or our court system that requires a government. Government backed courts don't have any more intrinsic legitimacy than private courts would.

But, to paint a more full scenario, private defense agencies would arise, sort of like insurance companies. Unlike the police, the goal of these companies would be to make money, so they're not going to want to go around shooting people or causing problems. And they wouldn't want their customers to go around shooting people or causing problems either.

Individuals would sign a contract with whatever company fit their needs best.

Let's say the guy who was murdered belonged to a Defense protection agency (DPA). That DPA would be responsible for tracking down and finding the killer. If the killer was a member of another DPA, then the two companies negotiate and agree on which private court they will use to settle the matter.

So, basically, exactly the same thing that happens now would happen under anarcho-capitalism, except everything is privately (and therefore more efficiently) run.

Power isn't something a group of people elect to institute. It's a natural state. When no one is in power, individual entities move in to control a situation. It functions most obviously on the smallest level. A group of seven people will, quite swiftly, develop a single person who should maintain control over the group. In a place without a legitimate power enforced by the will of the people or a traditional system, power will form under the auspices of charisma or, more often coercion. Without government, it's not that there won't be systems of power in place. The systems of power will be elitist (mostly nepotistic or racially-oriented) groups, the folks who have the money, and, rarely, the highly skilled.

Consider America. There are already institutions in America which hold more power than the government. They're mostly corporations. The potency of money is so great that, in fact, wealthy institutions like the Church of Scientology have swayed the IRS to make exceptions for them anyway, even under the auspices that they are for-profit organizations. Coca-Cola's power in the States is only held in check by the nation's ability to double-tax its assets and turn those assets into funding for the common good. In fact, roughly 1% of the people and organizations in the U.S. hold about 90% of the wealth. This wealth would directly translate into raw power in an anarcho-capitalism where there is seemingly no hierarchy of power (at least, none greater than being born into money).

See, anarcho-capitalism would step us back into a time where your birthrite gave you power; where your skills might fit you into a low-level position with some company where you can at least pull minimum wage, but where a place like Walmart chooses which religions, races, and franchises hold power. If Walmart says one year that they're not going to sell any Christmas stuff and instead go completely secular, you'll have a bunch of people with diminshed personal franchise attempting to correct the situation (and failing at it). If you're lucky enough to be born with the last name "Walton" or "Kennedy" or "Rockefeller", you're like a prince in the anarcho-capitalism. You are endowed with a monarchial power composed chiefly of money. You are highly positioned in the fiefdom that is the U.S.

Taking down the government will not displace power or corruption. Freemasons, oil tycoons, and the Church will move in to become the government, where the new symbol of power isn't a law or a democratic decision. It's money.
