Im Not Exactly listed as one of the "Great Mapmakers" on NUMA, however I know the name of the game.

Firstly, when you are thinking about Joining NUMA, take a look at the site and peek at a few maps. Also a new mapmaker should look to visit topics like these in the forums.

Now to address a few of the topics above;
1. Spamming - Using many more objects then needed.
Everybody should take into consideration the players that have computers held together with string and a window fan (Nice to meet you all). Not all of our computers can handle too much in a map. Spammy Mines, or spammy one-ways etc. usually will not even add to a maps gameplay, but it will only hurt it. I learned this the hard way. Now It's said that the look of a map is important, and alot of spamming of one-ways and mines would make the map look more fluid, but remember: Quality > Looks! Make sure to playtest all levels in your game to make sure they aren't laggy before submitting them to NUMA
2. Clustering Objects - Forming a cloud of objects when there only needs to be maybe 1 or 2 of those objects.
The Biggest example is bounce block clouding (Or the BBC(I Just made that up, don't use it noobs nobody will know what you are talking about)). Clustering as well as spamming can cause lag on slower computers. The effectiveness of a single bounce block can show impressive understanding of not only the behavior of the bounce block, but as well as the behavior of the objects around it.
3. Checking your map - On the confirm map submission screen, click the thumbnail and look to see everything is there.
Also as previously stated, playtest maps in your own game to make sure there are no lag issues.
4. Tile Laying - Creating the tileset for your map.
The Tumbnail of a map is important, because people on NUMA (Unlike me) will determine whether or not it is worth their viewing time judging by the look of the thumbnail. Make sure that the tiles on your map though, don't make areas too restrictive or cramped. Also if you have a firm grasp of the enemies in the map, the tiles can help to compliment their behavior.

Just a few tips for now.

P.S. Dont come to me for NReality tips cause I have NOOOOOOO IDEAAA what the hell that stuff is about.

1. Spamming - Object overuse is usually called "spamming" when it falls into one or both of two categories:
a. Extreme difficulty. This is caused by adding too many enemies. To counteract this, play test your map. If you can't beat it once in ten tries, it's much too difficult. Reduce the number of enemies and try again.
b. Loading and playing slowly. Some maps just have so many objects, the player experiences frustration while loading or playing the map because it moves so slowly. Try to keep the play speed up to at least 30 ms/f (milliseconds / frame).
Eliminating these two factors will help you avoid spamming and allow other players to enjoy your map more.

3. Checking your maps before submission (what to look/watch out for) - NUMA won't accept your map data unless it's free from error. Make sure of the following:
- Check your object data for any "NaN" (Not a Number) data. If you put it there on purpose, leave it, but if it's accidental, delete the object from the code and replace it in Ned.
- Don't add "$Map Title#Author#Description#" before your map data or the "#" after your data. NUMA will add these automatically; all you need to provide is the tile and object data and any NReality code.
- Look for unwanted letters in your object data. These will turn into NaN values if submitted.
- When copying your data into the Map Data box on NUMA, delete any extra lines and spaces, so the cursor stays right at the end of your data. These can also potentially turn into NaN values.

#461232 18 2008-08-17T05:50:28Z Meta Ing
Here are my suggestions. Some of them may have been mentioned before but whatever; I'll reinforce them.

1. Playtesting your levels often goes beyond ned. Learn how to add your levels to your userlevels.txt if you don't know how to already, and play them from there. This way you can check if there is enough time to actually beat the level. You can also check to make sure you did not add too much gold. (If your timer goes into the 1000's it's probably too much)

2. Make levels that are neither too easy, nor too hard for you. You should be able to beat your own level. You should not be able to beat your own level in one try. Finding the "sweet spot" for difficulty can be tricky, (I have trouble with it sometimes) but once you find it, try to stick to it.

3. If the level lags on your computer, chances are it will probably lag on other people's computers as well. (Unless you happen to have an extremely slow computer.) Keep in mind that other people may not have as fast of a computer as you do as well. Try to learn what makes most people's computers lag, and try to avoid that. Nobody likes to play a laggy map.

4. In most cases, one rocket or gauss is enough. Sometimes two or three, and in rare cases, four. Putting a row of gausses and/or rockets at the top of the map is not "the coolest idea ever!". (Nor is it original)

5. In compliance with number four, try to use ample amounts of objects; don't place an object if you don't need it unless it is for aesthetic purposes. (Looks) Don't get carried away with using objects as aesthetics either. Try also to put your objects where they will best increase gameplay.

6. Going along with number five, it is usually enough to place mines one tile apart.

6. If you are a new mapmaker, (as this topic should suggest, seeing as this is made to help new mapmakers) browse through the maps on NUMA and ask yourself: "How would somebody use this in their map..?" As you get better, ask yourself: "What's some way that somebody hasn't used this in a map..?"

7. Unfortunately, people are less likely to play your map if it doesn't look good from the thumbnail. Either try to make your maps look as best you can, or tell people in the desription to ignore the thumbnail.

8. Putting "Awesome!" as one of your tags or as your map description will not convince more people to play your map.

9. Browse through the forums, watch demos, look at The tips and tricks page on N.wikia.com, do whatever you need to do to learn about all the tricks and glitches in N. Once you learn them, use them in your maps or do your best to prevent them.

10. If you make a map using NReality v6c2 and decide to add mods to your level, try your best to avoid lag as much as possible. This goes especially for anyone in TNCC. (Remember, you're trying to get your levels in a column for the next version of NReality. It would be very frustrating to not be able to beat a level in the main game because of lag.)

11. Moving objects (drones, rockets, etc.) are more like to cause lag than stationary objects. The same thing applies to objects that move using the custompath mods.

12. Relating somewhat to number five, the tileset of your level matters. Try to make your tileset as good-looking and playable as possible. This often means adding tiles that aren't even used in the map. (Browse through NUMA and you should see what I mean pretty quickly) You will often get plus points for a good tileset. Try also to make your objects look good.

13. Try to avoid making the player do the same thing over and over and over again. If you are going to use patterns in your map, add gameplay changing characteristics to avoid too much repetition. (Eg. use chaingun drones, laser drones, or seeker drones.)

14. As you get better, try to make your maps unique. People may rate you down if your map is plain and generic. (It might be hard to tell what "generic" is, so just look at the majority of the maps on NUMA and try to come up with ideas that you don't see)

15. There must be at least one half of a tile space between the ninja and the electrical edge of a thwump in order to be able to pass the thwump. If there is no space between the ninja and the electrical edge of a thwump, the thwump becomes impossible to pass. Keep this in mind when you use thwumps in your level.

These are the best suggestions I have for now. I might add more later if I think of any.

1. Spamming
2. Clustering objects
3. Checking your maps before submission (what to look/watch out for)
4. Enemies (placement, enemy choice, etc.)
5. Tile Laying
6. Using glitches, bugs and NaN's effectively
7. Nreality map tips
8. Maps in general

1. Spamming is generally the overplacement of un-needed objects, also known as overkill.

Avoid this at all costs as a new member, this is one of the biggest ones, however, it's easy to get out of.
No, you don't need that huge pile of gold, or maybe that intimidating wall of mines. To prevent people from getting somewhere with mines, all you need to do is place mines one whole tile apart, rather then the every C.

2. Clustering objects is basicly spamming as well, generally applied to enemies.

No, it's not a good idea to cram 50 rockets on top of each other, or maybe a chain of gauss turrets. One will do fine, however, you'll get to the point where you know what's best.

3. Checking your maps before submitting. What to look out and watch for.

First of all, aviod using any alt codes while titling your map. Here is the cyntax, and I'll tell you why-

$levelname#authorname#comments#leveldata#

This is a very sensitive system, but thankfully, if you submit a Map to NUMA, it does it for you. However, if you have an Alt code in your title (levelname), it is sometimes detected as a # symbol, and messes up the whole code. Trust me.

That handy little thumbnail peek arachnid put there for you is for good measure, so use it. Make sure it looks like your map (sometimes you have a different on copy...OW!).

Lastly, reset your map! It's fairly common, for people to forget to reset their maps after playtesting. This results in the ninja starting on the exit, and most times, ends up making the map impossible. It's easy to fix though, but it's annoying to go through.

4. Enemy Placement

This is a very hard skill to learn. As a new map maker, I would suggest sticking with the very basic rocket launchers, and gauss turrets, until your ready to move on. These little guys are useful to add a little flare to the gameplay, but if used incorrectly, it can annoy people, and make your map dreadful to play (we don't want that). I usually place rockets in big open areas open for dodging, but there are so many scenerios, I can't fit it all in this little section. You'll get to the point to where you can effectively place enemies with ease.

5. Tile Laying

Tiles are the blocks that the ninja runs around on. They can be very plain, to very stylish and slick. It's important to theme your tiles. This means that you do something all throughout to the map, and make it a sort of standard. Maybe you make a certain design, and showcase it throughout the map. The possiblitities are endless!

Once you master theme-ing your tilesets, you can try out all sorts of styles, and even adopt your own. The possiblities are still endless! I like it when there is enough ground to manuever, but not a flat wasteland. Give the player enough room for skill, but don't dumb it down to where no skill is involved at all.

6. Using Glitches bugs and NaN's effectively

A Nan is generated by N when it doesn't reckognize a part of code. This can alter the ability of the object, and map makers have learned to use this to their advantage.

Nan's work on objects very different ways. One of the most popular uses for a NaN is at the end of a laser drone code. Here is an example of the typical laser code generated in N.

6^420,444,2,0,1,0

To prevent the laser drone from moving, replace the last number in the code, with the letters NaN.

Doing this to regular zap drones, prevent them from moving as well. Experiment with this to make great and creative maps.

There are many bugs in N.

A very common one, is that you can wall jump through one ways, even if their barrier is facing towards you. To do this, simply wall jump a few pixels under it, and it's like it was never there.



You can place floorguards in thin air, and they will come towards you, but only if there aren't any tiles in the way, and it has to be the left of them. If they see you, they will proceed off the screen.

There are many more N bugs, waiting for you to find them!

8. Maps in General

An important thing to think about during a map is it's difficulty. While making a hard map is perfectly fine, you need to keep the difficulty constant. It's no fun having to skillfully dodge rockets, and going through speedy sections of a map, only to die at the end because of an unexpected spike in difficulty.

After tough sections in maps, I usually have slightly less difficult sections, to cool off. Experiment with this until you get it to where you like it.

A basic overview of how the game works.

There are two parts of the level data, tile, and object.

Tile data is 713 characters long. Each tile has it's own number/symbol.

At the end of the tile data, there is a | and the code seems to look different. This is where the object data comes in. Each object has a somewhat simple object tag, that contains it's cordinates, type of object, and also a direction, or something extra like drone paths.

The objects "tags" are organized by order of placement. The further towards the bottom of the code is, the earlier it was placed. The only exclusion is the ninja, which always appears right after the tile data.
This concept is often used to find a ceartain object code to NaN. For instance, if you don't want to go looking for that laser drone, just delete it, and place another one, and because it was the most recently placed object, it will be at the bottom of the code.
