#15664 1 2007-06-12T23:33:47Z DemonzLunchBreak
Please, correct me if any of my premises are wrong, this is only an idea that I came up with several minutes ago.

1. The Christian concept of heaven is a perfect place.
2. A perfect place has no flaws.
3. It is a flaw to not have free will - (people prefer to make their own choices)
.: There is free will in heaven

1. Free Will causes suffering (or is, at least, the justification for suffering that I hear from the vast majority of Christians who do not believe in predestination).
2. Suffering is a flaw.
3. Free will causes flaws.
4. If free will exists in heaven, heaven is not perfect. If free will does not exist in heaven, heaven is not perfect.
5. Heaven is, by definition, perfect.
6. Something cannot be both imperfect and perfect at the same time.
7. Heaven, logically is both imperfect (see premise 4) and perfect (see premise 5).
.: Heaven does not exist.

Are there any problems with this reasoning? What are the implications here?

#353828 35 2007-08-11T01:46:26Z Atilla
Quote: DemonzLunchBreak, August 11, 2007 11:13 am
Could you give me an example of an illogical truth?
That could be considered a contradiction in terms, given that "truth" is a value defined by logic. I think it would be more accurate to say things which do not have an adequately defined truth value.

There are some things which people accept - and which they must accept to operate - without logical justification.

For example, try to justify the use of logic itself. You can't use logic in doing so, because that would be a circular argument. Circular arguments are not adequate justification under the rules of logic, so you've just invalidated your own argument...

There's also the Problem of Induction.

EDIT:

Quote: mam, 2007-08-11T01:41:33Z
Also, I'm pretty sure predeterminism has been proven. Something to do with quantum physics - a beam of entangled particles is directed towards a separator. The separators were identical, each with two exit points, A1/A2 and B1/B2. It was found that if particle A chose the first route, particle B would choose the first route and if paricle A chose the second route, particle B would choose the second route. As they had no contact after separation, the only plausible explanation is that their paths were predetermined.

No. Entanglement particles will have correlated quantum states - for example, the decay of a pion (spin 0) will produce an electron and positron. By conservation of angular momentum, one must have spin up and the other spin down. As soon as you measure one, you know the spin of the other, regardless of how far away it is. However, this does nothing to prove determinism. Whether you get up or down for a given measurement is a matter of probability. Indeed, this experiment can be used to demonstrate that it is impossible for the state of the particles to be determined by any local "hidden variable".
