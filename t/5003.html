#4557 1 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z TornadoTK
Just got done with the first chapter of this story I'm writing. Opinions, grammar/punctuation/spelling errors, comments, and any positive feedback is appreciated. Hope you dig it.


It was a day like any other. Joe ran his fingers back through his hair and looked up. "Ah, the sun's gonna set soon," he muttered, "Better get home." The sky glowed with warm, soothing colors that seemed to fade with every step he took. As he made his way through the park, he couldn't help but notice all the parents flying kites with their children, laughing and running together. He had a kid of his own, David, who was having his second birthday in a week. Joe stopped to picture the thought of his son and himself, flying kites in the park at sunset, and resumed walking with a smile.

He walked towards the park bathroom, looking for paper towels to blow his nose. As he reached for the door, he noticed a peculiar looking man. Black suit, red tie, and his black hair slicked back and gelled, his appearance was as sharp as a pin. He looked like he could put Donald Trump into bankrupcy. He had eyes that were as golden as the buttons on his shirt, and they seemed to be fixed upon him. Strangely enough, nobody even took a glance at him, no matter how bad he stuck out. It was almost as if Joe was the only one meant to see him, like some kind of ghost. He shrugged it off and entered the bathroom.

The bathroom was filled with the stench of urine. He shrugged off the smell, walked to the paper towel dispenser and pulled down on the handle; it wouldn't budge. He moved to the toilet stalls to get some tissue paper instead, and as the door into the bathroom was out of view, he heard it open. He faltered instinctively, but he regained his composure in a heartbeat. Opening the stall, he pulled some tissue from the roll. Suddenly, he froze.

He sensed someone was behind him. In his mind, the image of the dressed-up man just wouldn't go away. That moment lasted a lifetime. Endless amounts of waiting, thinking, hoping; Hoping that nobody would be there when he turned his head. He wished that he was just imagining all of this, some kind of strange temporary paranoia. He would leave, go home to his wife and son, and continue his life as a normal citizen, as a normal, working man. He couldn't have done anything to deserve such a fate in the first place, right? His lifetime had became a moment once again, and the next thing he thought would be his last. "What is that?!?"

The sharp, cold, razor-thin wire touched his neck, giving him a feeling of death; so cold, so hidden, it made his eyes grow wide with fear. Within an instant, that cold, sharp feeling became painful and warm. He knew now, he knew what was going on, and realized why it was happening. Taking money from under his boss's nose, blackmailing his fellow employees, taking the company name and using it as a cover for his drug dealing; Every last crime, every last sin, it all began to heat the thin wire until it felt like a thread of molten steel, burning a line across his neck, through his throat. It was almost as though as this was brought onto him from Satan himself, and this man, this assassin, was the messenger. He was suffering for trying to have it all, but in the end, it would all be taken from him.

It couldn't have been a second later when he felt the blood spraying from his neck. Most of it sprayed across the walls of the bathroom stall, as the rest poured into the slice in his throat, seeping into his lungs. It was clearly over from there. The man pulls two thin, steel rods from his inner shirt pocket and stabs them into the wall above the toilet seat, leaving them a few inches apart. Placing the neck of the corpse between the spikes, he sets another rod upon the two and leaves it to support the corpse in a sitting position in the stall. Opening the stall door, he whips out a small, red handkerchief and makes quick work of any noticable stains and smears of blood. After the area is left spotless, he pockets it once again and leaves, shutting the stall and bathroom doors behind him.

As he walks away from the bathroom stall, he tilts his head back and looks to the sky with a face of remorse. It glares back at him with a deep shade of orange, as if that was an ominous message for him- A message of rejection. Looking onward, he begins walking the path out of the park, along with the few remaining people that are through with watching the setting sun. To them, it was a beautiful sight. To him, it did nothing more than remind him of who he was.

A murderer.

#104413 3 2005-09-29T11:53:04Z Atilla
Mmm.. pretty good. Your use of semi-colons was a little odd. Some sentences also have too many commas, too many conjunctions, or a poor choice of words. I recommend going through each sentnce and having a serious look at the length and structure, then running through it with a thesaurus.

A few examples...
Quote
The sky blazed with warm, soothing colors

I don't think "Blazed" and "soothing" really go together. Try a softer, gentler word... something along the lines of "glowed", perhaps.

Quote
He walked towards the park bathroom to get some paper towels to blow his nose, and as he reached for the door, he noticed an peculiar looking man.

Too long, and clumsy. Split it into two sentences, eg "He walked towards the park bathroom, looking for some paper towels to blow his nose. As he reached the door, he noticed a peculiar man."

Quote
looking as sharp as ever

This isn't normally used when referring to a stranger, because you don't know how they normally look. Up to you, though.

Quote
Yet, his eyes; He had two eyes that were as golden as the buttons on his shirt.

Phrases seperated by a semi-colon should be able to stand alone, as sentences. "Yet, his eyes." is not a coherant sentence. You have to have something like "His eyes were quick and intelligent; they shone gold, like the buttons of his shirt." Specification of two eyes is unnecessary and looks bad; since he has been called a 'man', the reader assumes he has the normal number of limbs, orifices and organs unless stated otherwise.


Quote: TornadoTK, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
Awkwardly enough, nobody even took a glance at him, no matter how bad he stuck out. It's almost as if Joe was the only one meant to see him, like a ghost. He shrugged it off and entered the bathroom.

You changed tense between these sentences. "It's" should be "It was". "Awkwardly enough" is a little... well, awkward. Use the more common "Strangely enough", or just "Strangely".

Quote
The stench was pretty bad, as you could imagine in almost any public bathroom.

Looks awkward. Cut some of it - "The stench was pretty bad, as you can imagine" or "The stench was pretty bad, as it is in all public bathrooms". You could also change the sentence completely - "It was filled with the stench that permeates public restrooms everywhere." "Like all public restrooms, it reeked of stale urine".

Quote
He faltered for the slightest moment, as it would be human instinct to do so. Yet, he regained his composure, opened the stall, pulled some tissue from the roll, and suddenly froze.

"He faltered for the slightest moment in response to some ancient instinct" or just "He faltered instinctively" would be more elegant. In the second sentence, the word "Yet" should be removed. I'd also consider splitting it, although it's not too bad - "He regained his composure, opened the stall, and pulled some tissue from the roll. Suddenly, he froze." I think the full stop gives greater dramatic effect than a comma and "and", but it could just be me.


Sorry if I sound too critical. As I said, it's pretty good. And it is, after all, just a draft. I'm sure the final version will be excellent.

#104432 7 2005-09-29T14:27:49Z Atilla
@TornadoTK: Heh, thanks.

And I just want to emphasize something - you need to be more careful with your tense. Changing tense suddenly really disrupts the flow.

@stepself: I think structure is very important. It looks so much more professional, and flows so much better, when a story is well structered. It's okay to use unusual sentence structure - especially for dialogue - but it needs to done deliberately, and you have to be clear about the purpose. Readers will usually find the 'proper' structure easier to read, because they are used to it. Writing the whole narritive with odd structure generally just confuses people (although it is possible to pull it off well). The majority of a story should therefore be written with normal sentence structure, and the unusual bits saved for dramatic effect.

#104833 12 2005-10-01T23:36:18Z blue_tetris
I went to edit style. That's how I edit. There's nothing wrong with the story, but the sentences don't captivate enough. I think you are too eager to bridge together multiple ideas into one sentence. Don't worry about making short, biting sentences. Make your sentences "feel" like what they represent. A sentence about a freezing storm should use words that are short and hard. Remember that, when writing a story as opposed to an essay, the shorter the sentence the better. You want to create imagery with as little words as possible.

It was a day like any other.
Urrr. I wasn't even going to read any sentences past this. Don't opt for the cliche. This sentence is just.... deadly. It killed me on the inside. Remove this sentence. It only hurts the story.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
Joe ran his fingers back through his hair and looked up.
Joe ran his fingers through his hair. (The word back is unnecessary.) He looked up.
The second sentence could be "His eyes trailed skyward" or something that generates a more concrete vision. This can also help us understand that he's outside without coming out and saying it.

Quote: TornadoTK, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
The sky glowed with warm, soothing colors that seemed to fade with every step he took.
Get rid of "seemed to fade" and put "faded". "Seemed to" is too common and distracts from the action.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
As he made his way through the park, he couldn't help but notice all the parents flying kites with their children, laughing and running together.
Again, this could be made into two sentences. English class teaches us to use multiple clauses to make a sentence, but a good story uses a more poetic style. "Made his way" isn't visual enough. "Paced", "strolled", and "walked" are more visual. Then, for the second sentence. "He noticed parents with their children flying kites, laughing, and running out across the field." You may even want to split these sentences up. Really sell all the visuals. Take some time to set the scene.

The next two sentences are about his kid and they help reveal his name. I don't much like them. I think you could put this at another part in the story. I still don't fully picture the park "scene". You may want to resolve the park scene entirely before you get into a "thinking scene". Imagine it like a movie. At this point, I stop "seeing" the world and start "reading" about his kid. I think it interrupts the story.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
He walked towards the park bathroom, looking for paper towels to blow his nose.
This is a good rule for stories that may permenantly improve how you write: Don't tell them, show them. I can't look at a person and tell why they're going into a bathroom. You shouldn't have to tell me, either. If I keep watching, I will see him sniffle. I will see him strip a length of paper towel from the roll. I will see him blow his nose. Instead of telling me what's happening, show me what's happening. As a reader, I will be smart enough to figure things out. For now, you should get rid of the "looking for paper towels to blow his nose". We'll find that out later.

Trust me, this kind of writing will lead to a much more fulfilling read.

Quote: TornadoTK, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
He looked like he could put Donald Trump into bankrupcy.
If you're writing this for a class, leave it. If this is for a novella or something, I would steer away from pop culture references.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
He had eyes that were as golden as the buttons on his shirt, and they seemed to be  fixed upon him.
Try using a metaphor. "His eyes were the golden buttons on his shirt. They were (smooth), (shallow), (emotionless), and were fixed into his skull." Spice it up a bit is what I'm saying. Feel free to spend a little more time on this guy's appearance. So far, all I see is Donald Trump. Like, just Donald Trump.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
It was almost as if Joe was the only one meant to see him, like some kind of ghost.
Who feels this way? Make sure you aren't telling the reader how to feel. "Joe felt that he was the only one meant to see this man. He was a ghost." By presenting this information in a certain way, you can convince us (the readers) that Joe's feelings are quite strong and are a fixture of the story, but we can accept what we want.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
The bathroom was filled with the stench of urine.
The bathroom stunk of urine. (For obvious reasons, remove "filled the". Nothing should "fill" anything, unless it is the only word you can use.)

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
He moved to the toilet stalls to get some tissue paper instead,  and as the door into the bathroom was out of view, he heard it open.
Get rid of "to get some tissue paper instead". Make it obvious to us that he needs to blow his nose, sniffling might help, perhaps he could grumble about the paper towels. Then when he goes to the bathroom stall, we'll be able to see why he's going there without being told why. Also, split these into multiple sentences, to extend the dramatic tension.

The next paragraph is kind of neat. It provides a look into this character's mind. Make sure to weed out the extra words. "strange temporary paranoia" could do without the word strange. People can become momentarily paranoid without being strange.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
The sharp, cold, razor-thin wire touched his neck, giving him a feeling  of death.
Some readers will have been in near-death situations and not be able to relate to "the feeling of death". To my knowledge, death has no feeling. Fear, chills, shortness of breath--those are feelings. It's even possible that he felt he was going to die. But he certainly didn't feel "death". You can't feel that. It's even better if you tell us how he acted, rather than what he felt. "His breaths became shallow and frantic." If a guy starts acting like that, we can assume he thinks he's going to die.

This would be a good time for the character to have a "thinking" scene. You could even simply announce who he is at this point, since he's soon to die. Feel free to "narrate" here. "He was Joe" would be a neat departure from the scene. "A smile crossed his face as noises of the park floated into the dank bathroom. He thought of his son, David, and how he might not be able to see him again..." You get the idea. The man is brushing with death, he might have a few things on his mind. Just make sure that you don't want us "to accept something". Tell us what Joe is thinking, but leave the rest up to us.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
It couldn't have been a second later when he felt the blood spraying  from his neck. Most of it sprayed across the walls of the bathroom stall, as  the rest poured into the slice in his throat, seeping into his lungs.
Be more concrete or metaphorical. Dont just tell us what's happening. Either make it very gritty or make it very artsy. Just make it something. "A second later, blood sprayed from his neck, decorating the walls of the bathroom stall with a sickening design." would be kind of neat. Again, spice this up. We spent the whole first scene meeting this dude and now he's done. We want to see some fireworks. "Blood channeled through the man's throat and formed a reservoir in his lungs."

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
It was clearly over from there.
Oh, we know it's over, you don't have to tell us this. Count on your readers to make most of these assertions themselves.

Quote: story, 2005-09-29T06:58:39Z
The man pulls two thin, steel rods from his inner shirt pocket and stabs them into the wall above the toilet seat, leaving them a few inches apart. Placing the neck of the corpse between the spikes, he sets  another rod upon the two and leaves it to support the corpse in a sitting  position in the stall. Opening the stall door, he whips out a small, red  handkerchief and makes quick work of any noticable stains and smears of blood.  After the area is left spotless, he pockets it once again and leaves, shutting  the stall and bathroom doors behind him.
You shifted tense here. "The man pulled two thin, steel rods from his inner shirt pocket. He stabbed them into the wall above the toilet seat, leaving..."
Make sure the entire story is in past tense. It was during this paragraph that you made a transition over from past to present and, unless there's a whole lot of explanation and the narrator is a member of the story, that never works. The next paragraph needs to be in past tense also.

The conclusory statement is fairly good. This isn't a whole chapter--even if you added a few more pages of description to the beginning. More exposition is required to really set up the first chapter, but it's a good start.



Bottomline: Less words. More sentences. Less telling. More showing. The principles to a good story.
