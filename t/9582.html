Map Theory

Map theory is the sum of procedures and techniques involved in producing quality maps. Map theory involves skills related to creating maps that are both playable and fun. Map theory involves four different sets of skills. For this reason, map theory has been divided into four sections:
1 Layout Theory,
2 Tile Theory,
3 Object Theory, and
4 Applied Map Theory.

The intent of this report on map theory is to provide the reader with an aptitude at making great maps. The last section of map theory unites the previous three and explains how to use them together effectively to create maps.



1 Layout Theory

Layout theory is the technique of determining how to manage the space of the entire map. Layout theory will detail the process of cropping off sections of the map, effectively utilizing the map space, and directing overall gameplay. In essence, layout theory is looking at the big picture.


1.1 Managing Space

Managing space is the technique of deciding where the player should or should not be able to go. It also involves creating zones of the map that players will have to play individually, separated from the main area of the map. There are three techniques invovled in managing space: blocking, limiting, and zoning.

Blocking (or cropping) is completely preventing the player from playing in an area of the map. Blocking can be used to narrow down the area of the map to a smaller, more manageable space. Blocking can be accomplished in a variety of ways. The following example shows how blocking can be accomplished with tiles:


Notice that there are two small open sections in the large cropped-out area. These areas are blocked, because there is no way for the player to play in those areas. Now notice how blocking can be accomplished with objects:


Notice how other objects can be used to block off areas of the map from being accessed by the player. Mines block players by killing them if they try to access an area; one-way platforms block players by creating physical barriers, not unlike tiles; launch pads block players by pushing them in directions which prevent access to certain areas; even thwumps can be used to block players, because they have solid edges. Areas blocked using objects instead of tiles can still be used for objects like drones, gausses, homing launchers, thwumps, or floor guards.

The purpose of blocking is to limit gameplay to a fixed area. Remember, the player can only move around in the areas of the map that have not been blocked. If the player can enter an area that you believe to be blocked, that area has not been blocked effectively. (One-ways are tricky when it comes to blocking off areas [section 3.2.4].)

Player can still enter limited areas, but they would have no reason to do so because there's nothing that can be accomplished in those areas. A common type of limiting is placing mines or launch pads on the sides of wall tiles to prevent players from wall-jumping out of certain areas. This is often employed to drastically limit gameplay in the lower portions of the map.

Zoning is creating separate sections of the map with implied barriers, doors, or specific objects. Zoning allows the map maker to channel gameplay in a specific fashion. Zoning a map can also allow for the creation of linear-style gameplay by forcing players to enter certain areas of the map before heading to others. Zoning is usually implied or suggested instead of physically restricting. Zones allow for the map maker and player to focus on certain areas of the map individually before moving on to other areas.

Remember that objects in certain zones can affect other zones. Zoning doesn't physically limit areas of the map. There is nothing that prevents players from playing the map without regard to the zones that you have created. Zoning is just a way to make mapping an easier process for you. It is intended to allow you to focus on certain areas before others.

Blocking, limiting, and zoning are all ways to modify gameplay, which is discussed in the next section. The map's overall layout will directly influence the way the players will attempt to complete the map.

1.2 Gameplay

Gameplay is everything that the player does (or has the ability to do) within the map. For map makers, gameplay involves the intended course of action for players based on the design of the map.

One important aspect of gameplay is creating the implied path. A path is the route which a player takes to complete the map. Some paths that a player chooses to take will be straight from start to finish. Others will involve picking up gold. Creating necessary objects like door switches will help to create a path. The simplest type of path involves that player moving directly to the exit switch and then directly to the exit.

The gameplay section of format theory is broken down into three different sections, each detailing a particular style of gameplay which the map maker can facilitate: freeform, linear, and branching.

Freeform is a type of gameplay that does not directly imply how a player should play a map. Freeform maps do not direct a player to take a certain paths. Freeform maps are characterized by the player's ability to use a wide variety of different paths to complete the map. To create a freeform map, you should have a small number of zones and very few (or no) limited areas. A map with many zones or limited areas will limit players' choices in creating their own paths through the map.

Here is an example of a freeform map:

Notice that there is only one zone (the entire accessible area of the map) and that there are no limited areas. The player is free to move about in the map without the pressure of an implied course to take through the map. Although the player must still move to the left to hit the exit button then move to the right to reach the exit, there is nothing within the map that implies that it should be done with a direct path. In sum, the player is free to make her own path through the map. The following map is mostly freeform. It suggests a series of goals for the player, but does not imply a specific path that the player must follow to accomplish those goals:


Notice that, despite the fact that the map may have a semblance of zones, it is for the most part a map in which players must invent their own method of completing it.

Linear is a type of gameplay that directly implies how a player should play a map. Linear maps have a very limited number of paths that a player must choose from to complete the map. Linear maps do not necessarily have to be one-way maps that take you from start to finish. However, to be a linear map, a map must provide a single preferred method for completing the map. To create a linear map, you may want to have a number of zones and perhaps several limited areas, though these elements are not necessary for a linear map. The following map is linear:


Notice how the left side of the map is limited. There are no elements in that area to provide much gameplay. The linear path implied by the map is to follow the right wall upwards until reaching the exit switch and then leaping (or climbing) down from the top directly towards the exit. Another example of a linear map:


In order to complete this map, the player must follow the path in blue. This map is very linear. It has very few gameplay options for the player. Notice that the map has many zones, also.

Branching (or optional) is a type of gameplay that does not directly imply a single path the player should take, but has a definite number of different paths to choose from. Branching maps typically involve a main area and a number of connected zones which a player must play (in any order) to complete the map. Branching maps include any map that is not entirely linear and not entirely freeform, but instead something in between. To create a branching map, you may want to have a number of zones that area not organized in a direct or linear fashion. The design techniques to create branching maps are quite varied. If you're not sure if your map is linear or freeform, it is probably branching. The following map is branching:

Your choice of gameplay is important to you before you even begin making your map. It is something that will give a unifying purpose to the map-creation process. Deciding upon a gameplay method is crucial during the concept phase of map-making, which is discussed at length in section 4.1.

2 Tile Theory

Tile theory is the set of techniques involved in using tiles effectively to create a map. Tile theory, unlike layout theory, will focus on a more limited scope. In tile theory, our focus will be on the placement of individual tiles and the purpose of the different types of tiles. In addition, tile theory will highlight the gameplay issues involved with the placement of tiles.

Tiles are the contents of an individual grid space. The generic term "tile" can be used interchageably with "grid space". Areas can be "filled" or "empty". The term area refers to any region of space (not necessarily just a grid space). A filled area can be of any shape and size, while a filled tile refers directly to the E tile from the N editor.


2.1 Tile Types

In this section, we will be detailing the different types of tiles and what they can be used for. The N game uses a totally empty tile, a totally filled tile, and eight other types of tiles (with four rotations of each of those types of tiles).

Totally empty tiles are entirely unobstructed. They are the "D" tiles in N editor. The ninja has nothing to stand on in these spaces. Drones can only move through empty tiles. Any other type of tile will prevent a drone from moving into that space. The same rule applies to other moving enemies, such as thwumps and floor drones. Objects can only be placed in empty areas--which are most often found within empty tiles. The purpose of an empty tile is to allow space for the player to move and to allow room for objects, particularly drones, to function.

Totally filled tiles are entirely blocked out. They are the "E" tiles in N editor. The ninja cannot enter these grid spaces. Totally filled tiles create platforms, which the ninja can stand on or wall-slide or -jump from. Objects placed in these grid spaces will have difficulty functioning normally. Moving enemies cannot enter these grid spaces.

Half-filled straight tiles are filled exactly halfway, perpendicularly. They are the "5" tiles in N editor. The ninja can enter these grid spaces and stand on them or hold onto the straight wall edges of these tiles. Moving enemies cannot enter these grid spaces.

Right-angle tiles are filled exactly halfway, at a 45 degree angle. They are the "1" tiles in the N editor. The ninja can enter these grid spaces and stand on them or hold onto the straight wall edge of these tiles. If a player stands on the angled side, she will slide downward at an average pace along this edge. Moving enemies cannot enter these grid spaces.

Concave tiles are filled with a inward curve. They are the "4" tiles in the N editor. The ninja can enter these grid spaces and stand on them or hold onto the straight wall edge of these tiles. If a player stands in the concave area, he will slide quickly at the top of the curve, but will slow down as the curve's angle becomes more gradual. Moving enemies cannot enter these grid spaces.

Convex tiles are filled with an outward curve. They are the "8" tiles in the N editor. The ninja can enter these grid spaces and stand on them or hold onto the straight wall edge of these tiles. If a player stands on the convex area, she will slide slowly while on the top of the curve, but will accelerate as the curve grows to a steeper angle. Moving enemies cannot enter these grid spaces.

Gradual tiles move upward at a gentle 22.5 degree angle. They are the "3" and "7" tiles in the N editor. One version of this tile is shallow, with very little empty space; the other is deep, offering a lot of empty space in the tile. The ninja can enter these grid spaces and stand on them or hold onto the straight wall edge of these tiles. If a player stands on the angled side, he will slide downward slowly along this edge. Moving enemies cannot enter these grid spaces.

Steep tiles move upward at a strong 67.5 degree angle. They are the "2" and "6" tiles in the N editor. One version of this tile is shallow, with very little empty space; the other is deep, offering a lot of empty space in the tile. The ninja can enter these grid spaces and stand on them or hold onto the straight wall edge of these tiles. If a player stands on the angled side, she will slide downward swiftly along this edge. Moving enemies cannot enter these grid spaces.


Here are examples of each of the tile types (except for half-filled straight tiles). Notice how some of the tiles connect to the surrounding tiles. This illustrates the property of connectivity, discussed at length later on in this section.

The primary purpose of tiles is to create accessible (and inaccessible) areas for the player. Certain angled and curved tiles will allow for special jumps, which will allow the player to gain access to areas of the map. Another purpose of tiles is to create lines of sight for objects, as well as create paths for drones, floor guards, and thwumps. All of these issues are discussed in the next subsection: tiling.

2.2 Tiling

Tiling is the act of, and the set of techniques involved with, placing the tiles onto the map to create good gameplay. In tiling, a map-maker's intent is to place tiles so that they create areas of the map that are accessible for gameplay and other areas of the map that are inaccessible. For example, if you wish for the player to reach the top-center of the map, you'll need to place tiles that allow them to do so. Conversely, if you want players to be unable to get to certain areas, you can use tiles to block that area or place no tiles nearby so that this area is limited from the gameplay of the map.

Tiling is more than just managing space, though. When you place tiles on the map, you have to view the map from the player's perspective. You must ask yourself, "What can the player do if I place this tile here? What can't the player do?". Certain tiles allow novice players to perform certain talented maneuvers to guide the ninja through the map. Expert players even know tricks that can utilize tiles in extraordinary ways, offering them access to areas one might initially think are unreachable. As a map-maker, you are entirely in charge of managing the map. Everything that can be done in the map, you must take control of.

Tiling is the art of controlling that factor. It is managing what the player can and cannot do.
