#287102 25 2007-01-18T11:26:51Z iangb
just as a thought... imagine you have an object with positive mass (m1), and an (impossible) object with an equal but negative mass (m2). The force between the two objects would be equal and opposite on each, as usual:
f = g(m1m2)/(r)
if m1 = -m2
f = -g(m1)/(r)

So the force on both m1 and m2 is negative. Now, imagine the effect which that force will have. f=ma so a=f/m, so...

for m1 (+ve mass)
a=f/m1 = -g(m1)/(r)

for m2 (-ve mass, = -m1)
a=f/m2 = -f/m1 = +g(m1)/(r)

So m1 will accelerate away from m2, but m2 will accelerate with equal magnitude towards m1. Wham, perpetual motion, energy from nothing, breaking ALL THE RULES. It makes my head hurt thinking about it.

EDIT: technically that situation isn't rule-breaking (possibly, it's a bit dodgy) because the sum of the system has zero mass, so the system has no centre of mass (or the system's centre of mass is simultaniously every point in the universe), so it isn't really moving, as such.
On the other hand, I'd hate to think about a situation where |m1|>|m2|.

#289863 35 2007-01-25T15:25:46Z crappitrash
Um, incorrect. We know there are forces acting over smaller ranges that are stronger than gravity- e.g. electromagnetism.

"De-beakering" refers to the superfluid in question (usually helium-4, by the way, not calcium or rubidium because those haven't been made in large enough quantities) climbing over the sides of the container and out.

This is not due to anti-gravity but instead due to the lack of friction in the superfluid. Indeed, all molecules of the superfluid being at the same quantum state, there is no friction between the superfluid and any contacting surfaces, and as such, the climbing out of the container is due to the strength of adhesion to the surface (the way water forms a meniscus), and not anti-gravity.
