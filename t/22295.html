[Here's the deal. I'm writing a philosophy essay soon entitled 'Responses to the Problem of Induction'. While I know what I want to say, I'm intrigued to know what the general reaction to this problem is. So if you feel like it, read the problem through, and post me your response(s). Feel free to go look up stuff if you want, but I'm most interested in your instinctive response, as there are some bog-standard replies to the problem.]

Firstly, you need to know what the problem of induction is. For that, you need to know what induction itself is, so we'll start there and work onwards.

Induction is the line of reasoning which is formalised by the scientific method, but which is also the method by which everyone lives their everyday lives. Essentially, induction can be phrased thusly: "If it's always behaved in a certain way, it will continue to behave in a certain way". We know from past experience that gravity has always pulled stuff downwards, and we expect it to continue to do so while we play basketball. We know that biting into an apple will taste nice (assuming you like apples, natch), and this knowledge of past experiences influences our future choices when we go shopping. Induction is why we are so surprised by magic tricks - they break our expectations as to how we think objects 'should' behave. All science does is formalise this relationship and describe more mathematically/quantitatively how things appear to work.

In short, if anyone asks you why, when they drop a rock, it falls to the ground, your inductive answer will be "because that is what always happens".

So, onwards to the problem of induction*. You must ask yourself 'how do we know that induction is a valid line of reasoning?' The answer sounds disturbingly familiar: "because induction has always given valid predictions" - in other words, "because induction working is what always happens". As this shows, the justification for 'believing' in induction is induction itself. This means that induction is a tautology (a self-supporting argument) and there is no reason for using it. The consequences of this are huge - not only that science is baseless, but that there is no reason for us to expect our everyday assumptions to be true. Because of this problem, there is no reason _whatsoever_ for believing that the sun will rise tomorrow.

I welcome your responses.

* In fact, there are a couple of other problems with induction, mainly involving black swans or the colour grue. I won't go into them here, unless I get really bored later this month.
