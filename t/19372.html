Sentience is normally thought of as having consciousness; thinking.
I don't believe in the existence of anything supernatural like a soul or spirit, so I think that we should be able to narrow down sentience and define it in terms of organization of matter and energy states.

Here's how thinking works:
Your brain is made up of billions of neural cells, which have a very simple "processing" center and a long tube called an axon that sends a charge when activated. At the end of the axon is a large number of dendrites, each of which connect to other neural cells. That way, when one neural cell fires, the ones in contact with it are affected. A neuron will fire (which means it will release a charge) when it receives enough of what is called an action potential, which basically means that a neural cell has to reach a certain higher energy state. Some of the other neural cells attached to our neural cell, when they fire, will increase our neural cell's activation energy. The others, though, will decrease our activation energy. When the activation energy of our neural cell reaches a certain threshold, it will fire, which will in turn increase the activation energies of some of the neural cells it's attached to and decrease the activation energies of the other neural cells it's attached to.
This happens at an extremely high frequency, in extremely large amounts, constantly. This is how cortical thinking works.

So what we call our sentience is really just a royal mess of biochemical interactions and electrical impulses. That's really ALL it is. It's just interactions between matter. Unless you believe in something like a soul, thinking doesn't have any kind of "magic, special rainbow power," in the words someone called Lucidium. At least I think so, because I don't believe in a soul, or similar.

So, knowing this, why do we call ourselves sentient? If we're just a mess of chemicals acting as chemicals act, and a rock is a mess of chemicals acting as chemicals act, what makes us different from a rock?

Here's how a rock will act:
A rock will lie in place, unmoving. If it receives any stimulation from the outside world, if it's poked, if it's yelled at, or if the sky turns to fire, it has no way of knowing or even processing this, and it will continue to lie there.

Here's how a human will act:
A human constantly receives sensory input from his environment, and his behavior is based in part on the stimulation he recevies from the outside world. A human being takes in sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and tactile sensations and responds to them, and he can respond in a relatively short time frame.

If you were to isolate a rock in a sterile, featureless room, and then put a bouncy red ball with stars on it into the room, you can observe that the rock has absolutely no mechanism for identifying the fact that the ball is even there at all. The rock can't perceive the ball, and the ball's presence does not change the behavior of the rock in the slightest.

If you were to isolate a human in a sterile, featureless room, and then put a bouncy red ball with stars on it into the room, the human would respond immediately. The human would respond to the ball's presence at the very least by tracking it with his eyes. The sound of the ball bouncing could frighten him and he could leap to his feet and give the ball its space. The color of the ball and its motion could anger the human, leading him to attack the ball. If the human tries to taste the ball and finds the taste pleasing, he could continue consuming the ball.

The point is that the human has:
-a mechanism to perceive the ball
-a mechanism to comprehend the ball
-a mechanism that directly adjusts his behavior based on his comprehension of the ball

The rock has none of these. These things, in my opinion, are the criteria we can use to determine if something is sentient.

NOTE: I am NOT saying that reacting to a change in the environment, in general, constitutes sentience. If this was true, it would mean that lighting a piece of paper on fire and seeing it burn is grounds to call the piece of paper sentient. I am saying that a reaction must be produced which is caused by an internal mechanism based on that mechanism's comprehension of an outside stimulus. The change in behavior MUST come from an internal mechanism that directly sends the signal to act differently to whatever portion of the system is to change in response. It is the difference between retracting your hand from a white-hot stove and letting it sit and sizzle.

We've already seen how we can test that something has the first mechanism experimentally, but we can also just take that something apart to make sure that it actually has the appropriate mechanisms if we want to be sure. For example, we know that the human eye does, in fact, send signals to the brain that are different based on the wavelengths of the photons that hit the rods and cones in the retina, that this signal becomes any number of things based on different combinations of signals sent through the optic nerve (e.g. to the 'chair' comprehension center if the pattern of signals resembles the pattern that would result from viewing a chair), and that the brain will then have active signals in regions responsible for thinking about sitting, which may be forwarded to the motor cortex on the frontal edge of the parietal lobe if the human's somatosensory cortex has signals associated with muscle fatigue, resulting in the human's movement toward the chair and sitting upon it. The human would not have acted in this way if the object was, say, a water faucet, because the photons that would bounce off of the water faucet would not be turned into a pattern of electrical impulses that resembles the pattern of electrical impulses resulting from the collection of photons bounced off of a chair.
Anyway, the brain is the human's mechanism to perceive stimulation, his mechanism to comprehend that stimulation, and his mechanism to adjust his behavior based on his comprehension of that stimulus. The human is therefore sentient.

So let's start applying these criteria to things we normally think of as sentient and non-sentient, and see if we run into any problems.

The obvious test case: a rock
The way a rock acts is not actually based on any kind of chemical interaction inside the rock. The rock doesn't have a processing center that directly commands any other part of the rock to act in a manner any different from a normal behavior (which it is lacking). The closest thing a rock has to a mechanism to perceive a stimulus is the shockwave created on a molecular level when the rock is poked, but that signal dies very rapidly and doesn't actually reach any carefully-arranged portion of the rock in which that shockwave can go different routes based on an internal arrangement of matter and energy states inside of the rock, so pressing on the rock with any intensity and with any object will never make the rock do anything other than act like an inert clump of matter. There are no complicated sequences of events that can result based on any special internal arrangements inside the rock to produce any change in behavior for the rock.
The rock is therefore non-sentient.

I'd move on to a human at this point, but I just did that two paragraphs up.

Test case: a bug
A bug usually, like a human, has eyes. That statement establishes that the bug has a mechanism to perceive its surroundings, that the photon-receiving cells in its eyes send a signal to a mass of nerves inside the bug, and that the signal then goes in a unique route based on the properties of the object viewed. But that's really no big deal, because a video camera does that, too, and we don't normally consider a video camera to be sentient. The difference is that the bug has a mechanism that then works with this information and develops a set of signals that it will send to its limbs to produce a unique reaction to a visual stimulus. That's what makes the bug sentient.
However, there is still an argument in the fact that more modern video cameras will work with the information they take in and automatically adjust how it handles, for example, automatic lighting or focus. These sorts of features make video cameras essentially computers, which I'll get to in a second. But a video camera without these features is clearly non-sentient because it doesn't have any internal mechanism for changing what it is doing or changing its behavior based on the information taken in.

Test case: a cat
Just like a bug and a human, a cat has many different senses that can produce a variety of different results based on an internal mechanism that the cat uses to "comprehend" the stimulus. The cat will send the appropriate signal to its limbs from an internal arrangement of matter and energy states, where that signal depends on how the stimulus was perceived by the mechanism whose job it was to carry out that perception. The cat is most definitely sentient.

Test case: a computer
A computer has a mechanism for perceiving stimulation: a mouse and a keyboard. A computer has a mechanism for comprehending that input: the CPU, not to mention any running programs that are looking to interpret keyboard or mouse input. A computer has a mechanism for making a change in its behavior as adjusted through an internal mechanism as the result of an interpretation of stimulus. I would therefore call a computer sentient. Well, provided that it was actually running a program that took in input, did something with it, and responded in a unique way specifically because of a decision to do so made by the internal mechanism that comprehends the input. A computer is not sentient when it's more or less a space heater or turned off.
However, consider that a computer, much unlike an organism, is entirely subject to the whim of whoever controls it. It does not grow or improve itself, it doesn't learn, it doesn't innovate, it is only active when we decide that it should be active, and it has an extremely limited capacity for "sensation" (keyboard and mouse input). The closest organic equivalent to a computer would be an extremely submissive, blind, numb, handcrank-operated autistic who is incapable of learning (though his capacity for factual memory is astounding) and can become inert matter for an indefinite amount of time upon request. Because I don't think that the ability to learn or to innovate are requirements of sentience (as many smaller insects or simpler organisms who are otherwise normally considered sentient do neither of these things), I have few problems calling computers sentient. It is a very, very limited form of sentience, limited more than even a bug in many ways, but is still sentience. Computers are most definitely not LIFE, and some people may not take too well to the idea that a computer is sentient because it is not organic, but I think a computer, even one produced today, has the potential to be just as sentient as a simpler creature that we would call sentient.

Test case: a tree
See "How Trees Work."


How Trees Work:

There is no internal, central mechanism that decides the behavior of a tree. A tree is actually an excellent example of an organic automaton whose only purpose is to propagate itself and generate copies of itself.

Let's compare a growing tree to a supercomputer whose goal it is to maximize its ability to produce mobile agents of its will. The supercomputer is constantly churning out new robots and sending them radially outward toward land that doesn't have robot factories built on it already. This sort of robot civilization doesn't really have any central hub because each robot constructed has the computing power of the supercomputer. Its procedure is essentially to find land that doesn't have a factory on it and build a robot factory there, one that produces robots who will do exactly the same. Let's say that the supercomputer starts off on land that has mineral-rich land to the North, very hostile land to the West, land with very scarce resources to the East, and an ocean to the South (the robots are only built to survive on land, and don't actually know what an ocean is or what to do if they find one).
North: The robots who voyage north will find that minerals are very easy to come by, and so the factories are completed in short order and have a very high output. The spread of the robots to the north is very rapid and successful.
East: The robots arrive at land that doesn't have a factory, but they spend too long of a time searching for materials out of which to construct a factory. When it is finally built, there are hardly anymore resources to start producing more robots. Expansion toward the east is very, very cripplingly slow, but the central plot of land is still happily churning out robots in all directions, so resources continue to be devoted to the East, even though production there is almost non-existent. The robots who walk around searching for materials eventually run out of battery power and die.
West: Resources are not as plentiful as to the north, but a factory can still be built at a reasonable pace. The only trouble is that that land happens to get hit from orbit by eleven thermonuclear warheads every two weeks. Every time the robots begin building a factory, it is annihilated, as are all robots in that area. The central plot still churns out robots and sends them in that direction though, so it's still expending resources on them even though there's zero progress.
South: The robots find the ocean and find that they can't proceed. They either stand at the shore until they run out of battery life and die, or wander in another direction.

The end result is that the robots have grown north, and not much of anywhere else. There's a little stub on their eastern side, but it's really not worth mention.
How can the expansion of these robots be helped? The answer is simple: introduce a mechanism that allows the robots to report back to some central database of the success of the expansion by region, and then have that central database determine how many robots are sent where so that expansion is more efficient (i.e. cuts down on the wasted resources). In other words, we can give the robots sentience. Unless we do that, if we were to look at the robots progress after some time and see that they've only expanded north, it would be a very silly thing to say that the robots "avoided" the east and west, or that they "chose" to grow in a particular direction. The fact is that they were trying to expand EVERYWHERE, and simply failed in many places.

A tree grows in much the same way. It devotes resources to growing everywhere. Based on its DNA, different proportions of it can grow at different rates (redwoods, for example, grow faster upward than they do outward), but it's still more or less growing every which way. Even so, the point of this is to show that trees don't "avoid" objects or "choose" to grow toward the Sun - they in fact grow full speed ahead completely regardless of obstacles, and the portions of it that grow toward the Sun are the portions that actually end up surviving the best, which is why we see those portions and not the ones that couldn't possibly have survived.
Let's put a growing plant in a similar situation as the supercomputer we were talking about earlier. North of the plant is an open patch of mineral-rich soil with full exposure to sunlight and rain. The land to the West of the plant is Mr. Perepelitsa's property, and he hires a gardener to keep the plant we're talking about out of his petunias. The land to the East of the plant is a sandbox, and it's also completely blocked from the Sun by another, larger plant. To the South of the plant is a concrete wall. To make things interesting, let's also stretch a thick lead tube, one meter in diameter, about two meters above the plant.
Let's see what happens when the plant devotes resources to growing in every direction. When it tries to grow into the sandbox to the West, it gets absolutely no resources and its growth in that direction is a complete failure... but that doesn't stop the plant from continuously trying (if the sandbox were to be removed and the tree blocking the Sun were to be cut down, our plant will become successful in growing to the east). If the plant tries to grow to the west, Mr. Perepelitsa's gardener is just going to weed-whack that portion of it out of existence... but the plant won't stop growing into Mr. Perepelitsa's petunias, and will just have to be cut next week. The plant can try growing to the south, but will fail miserably because it can't grow through a solid wall... but it will still devote resources to trying just that, and its trunk will end up flush against the wall. The plant can really only successfully grow to the north. It TRIES to grow in every direction, but after some time we will see that it only succeeded in growing north. It would be a silly thing to say that the plant "avoided" getting weed-whacked (because it never stopped trying to grow into that area), or that it "chose" not to grow into the sandbox or through the wall (why, otherwise, is the trunk of our plant flush against the wall?), because we're only seeing the parts of it that survived. And for the diehard, what about the lead pipe above it? When the tree grows tall enough, it'll just split itself down the middle to accomodate the pipe, no matter how thick it is. How is the plant "avoiding" that pipe, then? How is our plant sensing the pipe, relaying that information to some internal mechanism, and having that mechanism reach the decision that allocating resources toward the portion of the tree that would grow into the pipe is a bad idea? Otherwise, trees have been know to grow right through rocks and other obstacles. It's not an uncommon sight to see a tree integrating itself with a chain-linked fence. For an organism that some say has sentience, it does a pretty terrible job at making decisions and avoiding obstacles.

That leads into a more direct approach: the cellular structure of a tree.
Biologists have long, long ago identified the kinds of cells you can see in a tree, and none of them have any capacity that remotely resembles passing information about external stimuli, any capacity to actually interpret this information (i.e. some internal arrangement of matter and energy states that becomes a unique configuration of matter at varying energy states based on the information passed to it through a mechanism that handles external stimulation), or any capacity to send to different portions of the tree any command to adjust its normal operation. None of these cells exist in a tree. Senses, brains, and muscular nerves sure do exist in humans, but not in trees. A tree is incapable of comprehending external stimulus or adjusting its behavior based on external stimulus. It lacks the three essential mechanisms that I believe sentience must have, and so I do not consider a tree to be sentient.

Theoretically, traditionally, humans have two major mental traits that place them above the metal "level" of other animals. These are:
1. The ability to compromise and problem-solve through abstract thought.
2. Self-awareness.

Of course, recently we have been noticing some widespread crossover - and I can see where your argument comes from due to this - among animals, especially Octopi (Octopuses?) and primates. But for the most part living, potentially sentient creatures rely on a combination of
1. Instinct
2. Hormones (as do we)
3. Quasi-logical thought.
The latter is the term I use to describe animal behaviour in general - it may not be the most technically efficient and precise thing to do (as we describe logic) but, usually, unless confusion is rife, it is the best thing to do. (I don't mean this morally, in case anyone misinterprets)

Watching and repeating is not abstract thought. Is does not require an imagination or even a semi-imagination. It is, however, part of my "quasi-logical thought" process, and in fact all of the above list. The animal follows its parent's/pack's/primogenitor's (in the case of instinct) actions because it is driven by a combination of instinct, chemicals, and the thought of a reward or captured food (providing it has just happened to eat food before and remembers it - a newborn wouldn't, but it wouldn't be able to complete the task regardless). Abstract thought is, by contrast, almost wholly imagination and zero instinct (which often causes extreme emotions such as panic).
