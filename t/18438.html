It is often said that determinism and free will are incompatible. Here, in this topic, I will attempt to convince you otherwise.

Definition of terms:
Free will - the ability of a rational, sentient being to have complete influence over its own actions

Determinism - the belief that the universe follows one causal chain, and therefore the conclusion that there is one and only one possible future.

Incompatibilism - the belief that free will and determinism are incompatible. There are several kinds of Incompatibilists.

Libertarian - an Incompatibilist who believes that determinism is false.
Agnostic - an Incompatibilist who is not sure whether determinism is true or false.
Hard Deteriminist - an Incompatibilist who believes that determinism is true.

Compatibilism - the belief that free will and determinism are compatible. Compatibilists who believe that determinism is true are often referred to as "soft determinists."
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Each and every one of us, every day, feels the effects of free will. Many call this an illusion, or a quale. It's not. Every time that one feels as though he is making a choice, that is an actual choice. Every time that you are given two options, and are able, hypothetically, to choose either one, you have made a choice - completely regardless of which one you will choose in the future.

A person has free will when they make a choice in which an outcome is not forced - either through coercive means or through actual physical violence. The laws of nature are not sentient. They cannot force you to do something. Because an action is not forced, the choice is therefore free, and the rational agent has free will.

Not only are determinism and free will compatible, but free will requires determinism. The reason I say this is that the incompatibilist definition of free will would require a spontaneous and unpredictable result to every decision. Causality would have to be broken at some point - meaning that it is not the person who made the decision. People make decisions based upon their desires and values. Their desires and values are caused by a thing X. If their desires and values are not caused by some X, then they are not properly that person's desires and values at all!
------------------------------------------------------------------
Note: this argument for compatibilism does not apply to theological determinism. This is because of the nature of theological determinism. Theological determinism depends on the idea that a rational, sentient being created everything and began the causal chain, and a rational, sentient being can force one to do something, and does apply force when beginning a deterministic causal chain.

I don't really like to use the word "choice" when talking about Free Will for precisely this reason (though I usually end up doing so because it's the best I can think of).
There is a meaningful difference between Free Will and Free Choice. I'm a Hard Determinist by your definition in the OP, but I firmly believe that humans face choices every day, so I'll readily tell you that there is such thing as "Free Choice," though that term is somewhat awkward. However, I will deny (until convinced otherwise, if that happens) that there is some magical intending force that guides you to make any given choice, completely free of your natural tendencies (which reduces to experiences, which reduces to brain chemistry, which reduces to the deterministic laws of the universe).

Imagine such a robot:
The robot is designed to dig a hole in the ground. The robot receives sensory input and assesses the plot of land it has been told to dig up. The robot then considers the means by which it could accomplish this task - after some processing, it determines that the functional methods of digging up the dirt are with its hands and with a nearby shovel.
The robot is clearly presented with a choice.
The robot then considers both options and decides which method is most effective. After weighing both options, the robot has determined that the shovel is most effective, and chooses the shovel.
If you were to put the robot in the same circumstance multiple times, he would pick the shovel every time. He can't make any choice other than to use the shovel, and this is why he can't be said to have Free Will; his decision-making is all determined by a process that only has one possible outcome.

So, again, I will happily admit that we have "Free Choice," though that particular phrasing is a little awkward, but I am also convinced that we do not have "Free Will."

Your OP pretty much relies on the existence of choice to prove Free Will, when in fact they have little correlation. You have proven that we are presented with choices, not that we are free from deterministic forces that will lead us to make any specific choice. If you believe in a deterministic universe, you'd have to believe that some part of us is supernatural in order to believe in Free Will.

#344349 7 2007-07-18T03:17:28Z Atilla
Quote: DemonzLunchBreak, July 18, 2007 03:58 am
Many call this an illusion, or a quale. It's not.

Eh? Being a quale does not mean something is an illusion. A quale is the perception of something. If someone else breaks a leg, you know that the leg is broken, and that it would hurt, but you don't experience the feeling of having a broken leg itself. That is, you do not have the quale of having a broken leg. The typical example is the redness of red. We can measure the wavelength and so on and assert that light is indeed red, but this won't let a blind person experience how seeing the colour red feels. A quale is the sensation a thing creates in a given observer, not the thing itself, or a property of the thing. Qualia are by definition subjective.

---

Anyway...

Say you wanted to know what 5*6 was. There are two ways to do this:

1. Go through a mathematical process (eg. 5+5+5+5+5+5).
2. Read the answer from a multiplication chart.

These are clearly quite different ways of looking at it. In the second, we care only that the answer is determined. In the first, we care about the process by which the answer is determined.

This may be compared to compatibilism and incompatibilism, which are two ways of interpreting the same information. Even if the outcome of a choice is deterministic, the process of making that choice still occurs. Compatibilists hold that this process is what is important; it doesn't matter if the outcome is always the same - the choice is still made. Incompatibilism holds instead that the outcome is the most important part; if the answer is the same, it doesn't really matter how you got there - the process may be necessary, but has no true meaning.

The standard incompatibilist argument is that at any given moment, whatever you are doing is the only thing that you could possibly be doing at that moment. Moreover, you actions at that moment were determined way back billions of years before you ever existed. Since your actions were determined by the past, and because the past is not up to you, then it follows that your actions are not up to you. In other words, the incompatibilist line is that you have no control over your actions if determinism is the case. Not simply that you have no control over how you make your choices.

All that it requires is the illusion that those choices were real options. As long as my actions follow my desires, then I have free will. That's what I think free will means. It doesn't mean that the will itself is free from determinism, it means that the we have the power to act according to our will.

And I believe we have that power whether or not determinism is the case.
