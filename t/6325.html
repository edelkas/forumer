Over the next two weeks or so, I'll be giving a lecture on the intricacies of the enemies of N. I don't know how qualified I am to do so, but I got to thinking about just how much there is to each of these adorable little adversaries. Not only will this serve as a reference to others, but also as a way to organize my own thoughts on them. If this goes well, I may consider doing the same thing with the other, non-enemy objects. Oh, and if you have any comments or suggestions, I strongly encourage you to post them.

Each installment will be added to the first post. All right, here goes.



*****Segment 1: Introduction*****

There are nine types of enemies in N, the popular freeware game by Metanet Software. Each one possesses properties and abilities that the others do not. I will be discussing not only these abilities but also the proper techniques of taking advantage of them in effective level design. I have listed their proper, official names, but I will be using their common names for the remainder of the lecture.

Mines
Floorchasers (a.k.a. floorguards)
Homing turrets (a.k.a. rockets)
Gauss turrets (a.k.a. gausses)
Thwumps
Zap drones
Upgraded zap drones (a.k.a. seekers)
Laser drones (a.k.a. lasers)
Chaingun drones (a.k.a. chainguns)


The enemy types can be grouped in two fundamental ways, as follows:

1. Mines, floorguards, thwumps, zap drones, and seekers attack directly, while gausses, rockets, lasers, and chainguns fire projectiles.

2. Mines and zap drones are passive, meaning they do not purposely seek out the ninja. The rest are active (not necessarily mobile!), meaning that once the ninja is in their line of sight they will proceed to attack him.


The goal of enemies in N may seem exceedingly obvious, but there is more to it than first meets the eye. The goals of these ruthless homicidal machines, in the hands of a skilled mapmaker, are as follows:

1. To serve as obstacles that the ninja must overcome in order to achieve a certain goal. In conventional maps this will ALWAYS mean standing in the way of a switch, gold, or the exit. And of course, for this to be effective it requires the enemies to have a good balance of difficulty.

2. To give the illusion of being an obstacle. This can be used to take advantage of the player's sense of pride by projecting challenges that don't actually exist. This concept is illustrated by DDAs, in that there is no actual danger of death in spite of appearances to the contrary.

3. To be used for aesthetic purposes. Enemies can be placed to create artistic structure, balance, and color contrast within a map. Some mobile enemies are even walled in unreachable places on a level just to give a different feel than it'd otherwise present without.


Now that you understand the basic ideas and purposes for the enemies as a whole, we can have a look at each one individually.



*****Segment 2: Mines*****

Mines are by far the most versatile enemies in N. Put in the simplest of terms, they are tiny sections of space that the ninja cannot traverse. In spite of their apparent relative harmlessness, a single mine can render an easy level incredibly difficult (yet possible) to beat. They can be used for a vast number of purposes, and they will be laid out in detail in the following section.

One of the most fundamental properties of the mine is that it cannot move. How, then, can mines be so utterly difficult to avoid? The answer lies in the movement of the ninja himself. When the ninja moves towards a mine, the effect is more or less the same as if the mine were moving toward the ninja. When placing mines, we can take advantage of two basic elements. Note that these elements also apply to all other enemies; it's just that they are most visible in the case of mines.


1. Player movement: By placing mines closer to other enemies or solid barriers such as tiles we are giving the player a smaller margin of error and therefore a higher chance of death. As the player moves toward a mine, he is forced to make additional movements in order to avoid it. Also important to consider are items that dramatically alter the player's movement, such as launchpads and bounce blocks.

2. Gravity: This form of movement is often taken for granted since it is not controlled by the player. The downward direction of gravity pulls the ninja back down to earth, as well as into any mines below him. Couple that with momentum and a level with merely multiple, floor-ridden mines where the player spawns at the top could prove quite challenging.


Mines often serve as impassable barriers that the ninja cannot get through. These barriers can be used in conjunction with tiles or without them. The benefit of a lack of tiles is that all other enemies can pass through mines, while the ninja cannot. Conversely, using tiles with mines means that enemies cannot pass through either, and also may allow the ninja to traverse only specific areas of the tiles.

Mines are excellent for use as stationary guards of gold and switches when in close proximity to them; this is just a small touch that adds completeness to a map. Also, when used in conjunction with other enemies, mines are capable of forcing the ninja to negotiate difficult situations. Their presence means that there are less routes for the ninja to escape from other nearby enemies.

Using many mines together in an effective minefield can be one of the most challenging aspects of level design. It is extremely important to maintain a balanced difficulty throughout. Ideally, the mapmaker plans out multiple routes, each requiring differing jump heights, making use of the elements listed above. However, it should give the player the impression that he is finding an uncharted path through the mines on-the-go. Because the ninja's gravity is out of the player's control, going through a minefield in freefall is the closest thing one can get to having active, moving mines. Constructing a freefall minefield uses essentially the same strategies as a that of a normal one, given the obvious exception that momentum and gravity must be taken into account for.

Finally, there is the aesthetic element. Mines, quite obviously, are red. Together with the yellow of gold and the blue of some other enemies (primarily zap drones), a map can include each of the three primary colors, which can make a map much more pleasing to the eye. In addition, mines can be added to space not devoted to gameplay, sometimes along with tiles or other objects, to make a map appear less empty.


*****Segment 3: Floorguards*****

Floorguards run back and forth across any flat surface when the ninja comes into its line of sight. The line of sight extends to all the empty tiles to either side of the floorguard, and stops when it hits a tile. They have a few basic functions, which can be used in many variations.

One of the most important things to acknowledge about floorguards is that they are the only enemy that is faster than the ninja is. This point is vital in understanding the mechanics of gameplay involving them. When the ninja is running on a long flat surface with a floorguard following close behind, the floorguard will eventually catch up, forcing the ninja to jump and allow it to pass (and then, if the ninja must land on the same strip of land, rebound back for the other direction once more).

This aspect is especially useful in corridors. Dead-end corridors have only one opening, while through corridors have more than one. In some corridors, floorguards can serve as barriers that completely block an opening. In dead-end corridors this effect would be permanent, and is one of the most common causes of "undeath" (possibly also "pseudodeath," meaning that the level becomes impossible to complete). In through corridors, the player simply has to find another, unblocked opening.

A corridor that is only one tile high gives the ninja no room to evade a floorguard, which means he will either exit the corridor or perish. In both types of corridors, the ninja can be forced to scramble in and out before a floorguard can get to him. Corridors that are 1.5 or 2 tiles high, however, give the ninja just enough space to let the floorguard pass, creating a lot of high-tension moments. This is especially true when other enemies, particularly mines, are placed in the corridors.

The floorguard does not always have to serve as a "chaser"; it can also be used as an extra little nuisance when the ninja isn't moving much horizontally. When the ninja is jumping to get gold or switches slightly above him, the floorguard forces the player to make further small jumps that are outside of his original plan.

Floorguards are effective as active guards when gold and switches are placed on or above a small plot of ground. This forces the ninja to spend only a split-second touching the ground, lest he be smashed into oblivion by an electronic, bloodlusting little puppy. When on a one-tile long horizontal surface, floorguards can be used as slightly larger, intelligent mines. This comes in handy when one wants to add a tiny bit more challenge than a regular mine would offer.

Something to keep in mind when placing multiple floorguards: if they are placed away from the ends of their line of sight, the ninja can often attract all of them off to one side. Because they are on top of each other, they [v]overlap and more or less merge into one floorguard[/b] from that point on.

Now comes the aesthetic point. Floorguards have bluish-green eyes and border, which gives one element of color to a map that can be used along with gold and mines. They also make excellent centerpieces when surrounded by the aforementioned objects, albeit being slightly asymmetrical in design.


*****Segment 4: Rockets*****

Rockets are one of the most versatile and interesting enemies in the game. They can also be one of the most enjoyable when used correctly. Rockets launch from a stationary turret. They are fired when the ninja comes into the turret's line of sight. A line of sight is created anywhere where the ninja is not blocked by tiles or doors. The rocket will then travel in the ninja's direction until it hits a tile, a door, or the ninja himself.

The player will use tiles and doors to his advantage to destroy rockets. This generally involves either outrunning the rocket or getting out of the way quickly when the rocket is close to both the ninja and a door or tile. In enclosed spaces, the problem is not with how to destroy the rocket but how to keep from being destroyed oneself. The mapmaker must keep this in mind and create some means of escape. In other words, tiles and other objects are as much of an obstacle as the rocket itself.

When rockets are used in open spaces with objects such as one-ways and bounce blocks, they will seldom be destroyed. They will persistently attack the ninja, forcing the player to make use of more advanced evasion methods. The rockets will tend to circle around the ninja. When used in conjunction with bounce blocks, rockets often offer a very enjoyable challenge, as the player can use its added movement abilities such as dips and increased jumps while the rocket is just as much unimpeded by the blocks themselves. Good object placement in open-rocket areas can be one of the most exhilarating situations in a level.

While using rockets, it is essential to remember that the launcher will not fire another rocket until the first is destroyed. This principle is crucial to maps that force the player to keep a rocket from being destroyed. Placing the turret in a corridor housing the exit ensures that the it will fire on the ninja unless the rocket is kept alive. This concept can be used to make the rocket trail the ninja through the entire level, or only a small part of it by drawing the rocket out of a corridor. When playing a map, the player must be careful not to be too close to a turret when its rocket is destroyed although it has a minor reload time before blasting another one off.

In addition, one highly advanced trick a player can use is to confuse a rocket by initiating a close call and getting directly behind it, thereby drawing it away from the ninja for a short time. It can also be used to draw them away from or into a wall, as the situation demands.

When dealing with multiple rockets, keep in mind that over time they will converge and essentially become one. This makes things significantly easier, so the mapmaker should take this into account. Placing turrets close to one another aggravates this problem. If tiles are arranged so that rockets are easily destroyed, rocket convergence shouldn't be a very large factor. When multiple rockets are on drastically different paths, the difficulty of movement multiplies as well, unless some or all of the homing projectiles can be destroyed or outrun.

Rocket turrets are a very dark red, not a very good source of aesthetics as far as color is concerned. However, they can balance a map very well, even when the bulk of the map is on one side.


*****Segment 5: Gausses*****

Gausses, like rockets, consist of a stationary turret and a projectile. Unlike rockets, however, the moving part of the gauss is a crosshair which appears when the ninja is in the turret's line of sight (that is, when its view is not obscured by tiles or doors). The crosshair follows the ninja, and when it gets close enough fires an instantaneous shot in a straight line. If any part of the ninja intersects the line (however thin it is), the result is instant death.

Although they can be overlooked very easily, gausses actually have seven phases of tracking upon firing. They are as follows:

1. No crosshair
2. Small crosshair
3. Medium crosshair
4. Large crosshair
5. Red box & crosshair
6. Instantaneous shot (red box & crosshair)
7. Gray box & crosshair
8. Reverts to 2, unless the ninja evades the turret's line-of-sight by then

In rare cases, they may skip a step. The size of the crosshair, and therefore the gauss's tendency to fire, is related to the ninja's distance both from the crosshairs and from the line of fire. In avoiding gauss fire it is especially important to be observant for the aiming reticule's red box, because the turret will fire approximately a quarter of a second after it appears. The crosshair will also stop moving the moment the red box appears, allowing a slight chance for a reprieve.

An interesting behavior is introduced when multiple gausses are tracking the ninja - their crosshairs get closer and closer together until they eventually converge. When they converge completely they will fire exactly in unison. This formation is broken when only one gauss shoots (when they are not perfectly aligned) or when the ninja is out of one of the turrets' line of sight. It is also extremely important to keep track of which crosshairs belong to which turrets when working with multiple gausses, as it is not always readily apparent since their targeting reticules show no link to their original turrets until they fire or upon initial appearance.

Gausses placed in open spaces are usually quite easy to avoid and outsmart, particularly when the ninja isn't moving back and forth across the line of fire. Keep in mind that the farther away the crosshairs are from the ninja, the faster they move, which means that most of the time it's virtually impossible to outrun a gauss completely for any lengthy period. The speed of a turret's aiming reticule acts something like a radioactive half-life (chemistry), in that they slow down the closer they come to the final point. If the ninja is, in fact, required to zigzag multiple times across the line of fire, objects and tiles should be placed to allow free movement in the immediate area.

Always remember that gausses can fire from a great distance across the playing field. This often occurs when one least expects it (because the line of sight appears to be blocked at times), and results in many frustrating deaths. When designing maps it is good to keep this idea in mind.

In tighter spaces maneuvering past gausses can be considerably more difficult, especially when coupled with mines and other enemies. Because the ninja is closer to the crosshair and line of fire, gausses tend to fire more often than in other situations. They are good for creating tense moments, but be careful to give the player at least a little breathing room. In general, avoid "dead-ends," where the gauss gets a cheap and easy shot at a cornered ninja. When used in corridors that are one tile wide, gausses present a different sort of challenge. The player must often lure the gauss into firing in one direction while getting through the turret to the other side before it fires again. This can often require a good amount of quick thinking and strategy.

Gausses are somewhat reddish in color, but have very little bearing on the overall color balance of a map. They act as excellent balances and centerpieces, however, and can bring a sense of completeness to a map.
