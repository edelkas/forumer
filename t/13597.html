#250365 6 2006-11-05T13:41:15Z Tsukatu
And if you're thinking of those crazy menopausal women who can immediately spot the difference between thirty eight shades of goldenrod, that's all about how much they've used color differentiation when they were young.
Babies are born with a vast overabundance of neural connections. The ones that are only used rarely for the first half a dozen years are lost, and they continue to drop steadily after that. If, as a child, someone had a great interest in colors or spent a lot of time around colors (maybe the child was allowed to draw instead of having overly loving parents attempt to teach it calculus), then not only will the brain preserve a large amount of its overabundance of neural tissue that relates to color perception, but a larger part of the brain will be devoted to such as the child's exposure to colors continues.

#250983 12 2006-11-07T01:27:21Z Atilla
The overall number of cones just alters your sensitivity to colour, not what colours you actually percieve. Having more cones would increase the saturation of colours (that is, things would look more colourful, and less grey). There are also different types of cones (three in normal people), which are sensitive to different wavelengths to varying degrees (check the absorbtion spectra on Wikipedia). Naturally, there is some variation in the number of cones an given individual has, and in precisely how sensitive they are to different wavelengths, but this is rather minor in most people. It might make you disagree with someone about whether aqua is blue or green, but it won't make you claim an object which someone else thinks is orage is actually purple. Therefore, having more cones overall should not produce a preference to certain colours, since it effects all colours (approximately) equally.

Rods, on the other hand, only detect the intensity of light, rather than the colour. They are more sensitive than cones, but they cannot provide as much fine detail and they are slower to respond to light. They are primarily used for night vision, which is why everything looks grey and blurry in the dark. One could hypothesize that men have more rods than women because they have traditionally served as watchmen and guardians, and therefore have more need to see in dim light.

Also, the whole blue/red/pink thing is almost certainly due to conditioning rather than any inherant preference of a gender for those colours. The colours have been opposite at several times in hitory - blue for girls and pink for boys.
