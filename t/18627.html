Ever wonder if the guy next to you who is speaking very rapidly in a foreign accent is thinking in the same language as you? I mean, to some extent, it makes sense right?

Well, consider this before you jump to conclusions:
When scientists use electro-stimulation to cause the human brain to think, imagine, or remember something, it is not adjusted in any way to accommodate what language they learned first, or use most. So, on a crude and technical level, our minds use the same electric patterns to produce relatively similar thoughts, imaginations, and relatively similar images of fear.

So, if our thoughts were to be translated, what would they translate to? Would someone who grew up speaking Japanese think in a different language than someone who grew up speaking German? How do you distinguish ones language of thought, anyways? Or rather, do we all think in the same language, but just interpret it differently?

My idea of this is that we all think in the same language and do, indeed, interpret our thoughts into our native language.
What do you guys think?

#348911 9 2007-07-30T05:55:12Z southpaw
haha, this is my kind of topic. I just did a paper on second language aquisition and how one language differs from another.

To start, let us use the Sapir-Whorf Hypotheses (more commonly reffered to as the Whorf Hypothesis if you are going to look it up on Google), which states that a persons language is like a barrier, one that limits a persons thoughts to the extent of that language. At the surface level this may seem like a pretty strange argument but let us delve into it.

Edward Sapir was the person who first discovered the fact that Inuits or Eskimos had multiple words for snow. There has been some debate but I think that Inuits have one word for one type of snow, and they just happened to define 9 types (like powdery snow, icy snow, melting snow, etc.).

Benjamin Lee Whorf was Sapir's student, and one of the more interesting things he discovered was the idea that a person who knew only one language could express their thoughts to a certain point and then they were left without words. Let us say this person spoke English and was trying to explain their thoughts on some issue. Whorf found that some languages had untranslatable words that helped people who spoke other languages reflect on the given idea more thoroughly because these words thought in the abstract sense. Whorf believed that this kind of difference in expressing thoughts led to a difference in the way people constructed their thoughts when they spoke.

I'll elaborate more when I find my paper, but this is basically what I can remember so forgive me if there are any mistakes. I'll post my paper once I find it.

I once developed a cyberpunk campaign world for an RPG that involved a series of programs which ran on something called Vicar. Vicar recorded human stimuli as programs using a simple series of 1's and 0's where 1 was an occurence of synapse and 0 was the lack thereof. The breadth of the produced program was mapped one-dimensionally along a series of nodes which could be easily "calibrated" to map those nodes positions in the consumer's mind. Using these programs, someone could feasibly record themselves riding a rollercoaster, then send someone a file so that they could experience the act vicariously. Needless to say, in a gritty future, this served more for abuse than "proper" usage. Criminal syndicates would really only need to spend the time torturing a single victim; making a recording of the sensation of having ones fingernails hinged out saves a lot on "bamboo expenses".

As such, Vicar was made illegal. Programs were in development for several decades which would allow for "selective perception", with built in thresholds which prevented certain applications of perception entirely. Then, eventually, a breakthrough! Instead of mapping nodes to sensory input, one would merely need to find nodes which could approximate the reception of language. The nodes would be those of the brain which, specifically, do not receive the language, but instead take language whose meaning has already been decoded and turn that meaning into imagery--which, in this sense, includes any sensory input the user attaches to the imagery. So, while Vicar would show you an object with four wooden legs and a red velvet back that you could discern to be a "chair", this new program would insert the linguistic meaning of "velvet-backed chair" and your mind would create the sensations necessary to interpret the object. It was "weaker" than Vicar, in that its input was of the second order. It was safer, too.

It was called PlowShares, for reasons ingrained in the motifs of the story. PlowShares was the first multi-user system of its type. Different languages had different programming nodes for PlowShares. Although it might be assumed that once you fully extract the meaning from the English phrase "velevet-backed chair" you will get the same imagery as you (personally) would from the meaning extracted from the Spanish equivalent; PlowShares plotted different nodes (if only slightly) between the various language cultures--even some variations between cultures speaking the same languages. The UK, US, and Australia had similar enough linguistic traits to use the same verision of PlowShares, but South African English required a different one, for certain nouns. Oceanic languages (like those of Fiji and Micronesia) and Aboriginal languages were the most different from the global standard. Even ubiquitous concepts like "chair" and "Western-looking house" were difficult to globalize to these minor languages. Some of the smaller Aboriginal languages didn't receive a version of PlowShares (not that most of them cared), because users with those versions would, during shared usage, cause "cognative asymmetry"--an internal systems error which occured when the way one user viewed the situation would make it difficult for another user to interpret their actions within the system. Instances of cognative asymmetry would be announced throughout the current environment to all users and would disconnect the user (or the smallest group of users experiencing the asymmetry) from the shared environment--shifting them into one by themselves or with only a subset of the total users within the environment.

Anyway, because of the way the system worked, an Eskimo could have a miniscule file properly describing an arctic scene, while a Brazilian would require a much larger file for a similar interpretation. Mind you, this was all speculative fiction, but it was well thought out--if I do say so myself.

Metallic, thick spike with a large eye for holding ropes. I think the problem we're approaching is that people think that because it's possible to think without language, language is not an aspect of thought. I think language and thought go hand-in-hand. So much so, in fact, that I believe language is simply an extention of thoughts. Everything has the potential for language, at its crudest order. Even when you're thinking without words, you're forging a network of labels in your mind to identify items--and these labels are yours alone. This network is language. Formal language gives people a common method of thought--and since most languages are very similar at their syntactic core, nearly every cultured person shares in practices we could call "basic human cognition".

I think most of our cognition has formed as a product of our socialization--the things instilled within us which make us people. Clearly, animals think, but they do so on a very different level. A different enough level that no animal could ever grasp most of the words in these posts, even if they were somehow translated into "animal thought language". There are many aspects of thought which require processing labels (mental qualia) as individual units; and this requires language.

As language itself is not universal, these qualia are not universally understood. Ergo, no "universal thought-language" strong enough to bind every person, even though certain aspects of thought might be universal.

#350393 40 2007-08-02T21:57:15Z southpaw
Quote: Ethrel, August 02, 2007 04:53 pm
The only problem with dealing with someone who had those senses at one point would be that they had already had time to learn to 'think' in those senses, and there would be major parts of their thinking process that would be left untouched due to unconscious habit. IMO, of course.

But habits can be broken. Your brain is systematic, so that as long as something is being frequently used it thinks it is important. If you suddenly stop seeing your brain starts to forget visual memory because it no longer sees that as important (pun slightly intended). Your brain won't forget it all at once, nor will it forget all of it, but you will lose a lot of your ability to store visual information. If you happen to get your sight back, then your brain will start receiving information through your eyes again and start that visual memory up again. Although a person who had only one sense would be ideal for testing this, it would not be much of a long shot to test someone who had been born with the other four senses.
