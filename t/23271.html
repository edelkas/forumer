As I understand it, that's weak atheism and (at least) weak agnosticism.

(Wikipedia:

Weak agnosticism, or empirical agnosticism (also negative agnosticism), is the belief that the existence or nonexistence of deities is currently unknown but is not necessarily unknowable and therefore one should withhold judgment until/if more evidence is available.

Strong atheism is a term generally used to describe atheists who accept as true the proposition, "gods do not exist". Weak atheism refers to any other type of non-theism. Historically, the terms positive and negative atheism have been used for this distinction, where "positive" atheism refers to the specific belief that gods do not exist, and "negative" atheism refers merely to an absence of belief in gods.)

Atilla, I think you missed my point. It's just as feasible to regard Unicorns as omnipotent as it any god / deity. They don't have to be invisible either, but it helps unicorn-believers explain the lack of evidence.

This is false. Atilla's distinction is rather important. The existence of a unicorn on earth is falsifiable, and so it's not a long shot to say that, after a certain amount of evidence to the contrary, such a thing doesn't exist on earth. Now, if you're using worldly evidence to disprove that unicorns exist anywhere or any kind of unicorn exists (particularly the invisible pink sort), you're making some leaps in logic. Information about worldly species is much more complete than information about God, and claiming agnosticism about worldly species takes more of a commitment.

Agnosticism isn't about belief or disbelief. In fact, it's denying the choice to believe or disbelieve. To affirm atheism, you have to go out on faith that the evidence you have for the nonexistence of God is perfectly complete. You have to say "Alright, I may not have the answers, but I think the amount of evidence suggests that God doesn't exist." You'll find a similar regard with theists. They say "I may not have all the answers, but I think the amount of evidence suggests that God does exist."

The final step in affirmation for theism or atheism is a step of faith, to fill in the evidence which does not exist to derive an answer. But this doesn't mean that atheism is a weaker argument!

Epistemology, that is, our acquisition of knowledge, is always a final leap of faith. No information is absolutely complete. This is not to say it's foolish to learn things because there's never complete knowledge. In fact, it's usually a pretty good idea to take what you know and apply it, at some point. But that requires a final cut in logic, where you disavow the possibilty of evidence to the contrary in the remaining gap of things not known about the subject. For something like "the sky makes rain", the gap you have to cross is infinitely narrow. For something like "evolution", it's still pretty narrow, but a shade wider. For something like "God", the gap in our complete knowledge is quite wide.

Agnosticism is choosing not to cross that logical gap in either direction, and it can pertain to knowledge beyond that of God. It can really pertain to any subject.

Sure, at some point, it's wise to cross that gap. But agnostics feel that it's not wise to cross the logical gap of missing knowledge about God. For some, agnosticism is as good as atheism. If you have yet to cross the logical gap to say something exists, and that something will have zero effect on your life until it exists, then indecision will evoke the same behavior as choosing disbelief.

Quote: Arona Daal, August 07, 2008 02:53 pm
Try to disprove they exist under those circumstances. Like I said, it would be like telling a kid that there's no invisible monster under his bed.
Right! This is the crux of agnosticism. It's saying "it's impossible to disprove it". I fully believe that unicorns don't exist. Given the amount of evidence, I'm willing to cross that epistemological gap. Invisibile pink unicorns on other worlds? It's hard to say. I'm still willing to cross that narrow gap to affirm "no, I believe in no invisible pink unicorns", but the gap broadens as you reach outward into more metaphysical realms. The results become less meaningful and the answers become less determinable until, at some point, there's a meaningless something way out somewhere which may or may not be real... and no one should care.

A child, given what he knows about the world, can affirm "There's no monster under my bed!" and he can also affirm "The light in the fridge is always on!" But there's no way for the child to prove that the monster isn't there and the light doesn't go out when the fridge closes. Notice the disparity in these examples. Absense of evidence is not evidence of absense and, maybe at some point, the child could learn that the light in the fridge does turn off when no one is looking--as difficult as that is for the child to believe at first.



As for the Suki bit, we've all read that thing a hundred times over. And I say now what I said way back when. Language isn't prescriptive. I think that's probably what maddens you about the religion argument. There's some prescription you've devised for concepts and terms which is very close to a workable model, but models simply can't exist in the study of religion. And no one uses just one model of language. So, any diversions from the model you prescribe sends you back onto the semantic wheel to drive through your terminology again.

So, even though you want the terms "agnosticism" and "atheism" to refer to those things you made up; in the vernacular, they don't. And in the end, people who call themselves "agnostics" wouldn't agree with the definition you've attributed to them. So, do you assume your definition is wrong? No. You assume that those people need to change to use your definition of their beliefs. It's a wacky cycle, and yeah, I've watched you churn through it a number of times. It's the fundamental flaw of prescribing language. It's neat how you've crafted a binary system to house all possible belief systems using words which already exist, however.... However! Again! Those words aren't as you've prescribed. And the people who use them do not fall into categories based on the words you've used. This doesn't mean that they don't understand the way things work. It means that language is descriptive, not prescriptive, and you need to hone your observations. (Note the word "observations".)
