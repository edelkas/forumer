#276968 52 2006-12-25T10:12:03Z crappitrash
Alright.

1. You have your basic infinity omega, defined as the first real number above the real numbers, and reachable ONLY by counting for an infinite amount of time. Now, according to Cantor, 1+omega is still omega (because you just start counting from a later point but still for the 'same' time) but omega+1 is more than omega, because you add one on top of the already existing infinity, and not before.

Transfinite numbers are not commutative. And not associative.

You can have 2 x omega and all that, and even powers. This is a cardinal infinity in the same way you call 1, 3, 230875 or 8247847 cardinal numbers. There are also an infinite range of infinities here in the same way as there are an infinite number of natural numbers. (omega, epsilon, alpha, etc.)

2. The other kind of infinity is the ordinal kind. These are used to denote the size of the infinity in question. For example the set of all natural numbers has order aleph-null, or the set of even numbers, or the set of integers, or the set of primes. Or even the set of all fractions. AS LONG AS THEY CAN BE PUT INTO A ONE-TO-ONE CORRESPONDENCE WITH THE SET OF NATURAL NUMBERS (i.e. they are countable) THEY HAVE ORDER ALEPH-NULL.

Cantor also proved that the infinity of real numbers between 0 and 1 (and thus between any two numbers) is of a higher order than aleph-null. (suggest further reading so this post won't be too long) It is of order aleph-one (also called the continuum; okay I know the fact that c = aleph-one isn't proved but overwhelming evidence exists to point that way) and aleph-one = 2^(aleph-null). And so on.

There IS a sense in comparing infinities. But you cannot take ordinals and operate them in the same way as you do numbers. It makes as much sense as saying Wednesday + January = Porcelain mug.

I suggest reading Rudy Rucker or Martin Gardiner. Good source.
