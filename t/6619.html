You make it seem like an amazing thing that we're alive right now. Someone once told me that one-tenth of the people who have ever lived are alive today. Interesting theory, huh? There's such a large population nowadays that it would be slightly more unlikely to have been born at any other point in history. If the population keeps growing, more and more people will find themselves "lucky" to have been born in that era. In fact, these people aren't lucky, they're just among a larger population. Your marble example assumes that we were equally likely to be born at any point in time. The truth is that we are more likely to be born during a time when the birth rate is the highest--and for most countries, it just keeps getting higher.

That said, the whole marble idea relies heavily on the gambler's fallacy. It suggests that certain events are more likely to occur, just because they have already occured once. If I roll a standard die and get five 6's in a row, what is my likelihood of rolling another 6? The answer is 1 in 6. The occurence of previous events has little to do with the occurence of future events in an exclusive probability system.

Stephen Baxter, right? He comes up with some cool ideas, but his writing style and understanding of physics leave a lot to be desired.
This bugged me too when I read it, and though I know other people have said most of what I'm going to say here, I felt compelled to weigh in anyway.

Obviously incorrect though it is, the Carter Prophecy is a subtle thing, making it hard to disprove clearly. There are however several ways to explain the error.


-
Quote: Solarion, 2006-01-04T11:16:16Z
Just think that if humans survived much longer, you'd be more likely to be in the future. MUCH more likely.
This is just incorrect.
You weren't in some kind of limbo, with all the possible human lives layed out before you, randomly choosing which one you'd live. You aren't more likely to be born in any period of time any more than a dot on a die is more likely to be on a certain side.
Think about it, the "one" dot could say "It's far more likely that I should be on the six side", but it's wrong. There has to be a dot in the "one" spot.
Thinking "It's unlikely that I'm alive now" doesn't make any sense. Just by the fact that there are people alive now means someone has to be born into this time.


- The balls analogy is misleading. The balls are supposed to represent the entire human race. Having just one person look for their name is the error.
Every person has a ball somewhere in there, so someone's going to get their ball first. But equally, someone's ball is going to come last.
If you had every person's name ever written on the balls, and the entire human race standing there waiting for their ball to come up, then the analogy would be equivalent.

- The population of the human race goes roughly in an exponential curve. The thing is, by definition, exponential curves look exactly the same no matter what part of them you look at.
This argument can be applied just as easily to cavemen, or a forming ant colony. If it's true now, it must have been true then.


Glad someone brought it up though. It's quite interesting.

#129853 17 2006-01-05T06:19:32Z Aries_Griff
Just to clarify the human population thing, it's not all exponential. There are NOT as many people alive now as have ever lived. This is because human population has not always been exponential. People died much more often when human technology was not as advanced, and in the early more savage-like days, people in parts of the world couldn't reproduce exponentially and survive, because the population carrying capacity of that area would be reached, and then the population would stabilize around that (like any other kind of animal). Plus, there are lots of things that take big chunks out of the ideal exponential curve that other animals with "unlimited" resources would expect. War, Plague, Famine, etc. Europe had to spend a while recovering from the black plague (killed 1/3 of them I believe?) and then there is always wars which kill many people and keep them from feproducing. So the population doesn't always go up exponentially, and you have to take into account how much higher the death rate was and how much shorter people lived when humans had less technology.
