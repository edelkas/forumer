I have found that there are certain levels of trust that you put on someone throughout your meeting with them.

1. The trust of another human being to save your life when only they can. For instance, if I was walking down the street, and all of the sudden something horrible happened, and I was about to die. I would trust the person on the other side of the street, whom I dont know, to try to save my life. This is because I firmly believe that humans are naturally good. When caught unaware, we will do good when the time arises. This level of trust is only true for the person caught unaware.

2. The trust you have in another person to do a petty chore for you. For me, I have almost no trust in people. Such as my friends. Like if I give them an envelope to keep overnight and tell them not to look inside. I don't trust them to do that. Its only a small meaningless envelope, and there would be no consequences for them. Thus, I would believe that they would open the envelope. Another example would be if I gave a friend or stranger 50 cents. I wouldn't trust them to keep it, because it is only 50 cents and nothing bad will happen if they use it.

3. The level of trust to do the right thing. For me, I have this trust in only those that I know. For strangers, I wouldn't really trust them. Yes, I believe that people are inherently good, but because I don't know the person, I wouldn't be able to say with all seriousness that they will do the right thing. The "right thing" in this case is something that is only semi-important. (Going back to 1 trust. The reason I would trust another human being to save me is because I believe that any person can realise the seriousness of the situation, and act on it. The less seriousness there is, the less incentive there is to do right.) For the people I know, I believe that they would do right. For strangers, I think and hope they would do right, but I can't really and fully believe that.

Feel free to discuss. Again, I believe people are inherently good, but in today's world they sometimes fail to act on their potential.

Social conditioning does shape people (to both be rebels and to be "good") but I think what are more interesting are the situational factors that cause someone to act in a certain way at a certain time. I know that's similar to social conditioning, but I'm talking in the moment as opposed to over a long period of time.

Milgram got people to apply increasingly powerful electric shocks (up to fatal @ 450 volts) to fellow test volunteers next door - with the person next door screaming to let them out, and then finally going silent to imply they were dead (the shocks weren't real, the stress was recorded, but the people applying the shocks were not to know). Some of the people applying fatal shocks continued and continued but were so under stress they had seizures. Milgram pressured them to continue, the lab situation at Yale University made them scared to stop, and the contract they had signed was one they were reluctant to break. So 65% of people became so irrational they went all the way up to 450 volts, a fatal "dosage", while everyone went to 350 volts.

They were all informed of the effect of the shocks. But they all became pretty damn evil. But it was entirely because of the situation. Social influences have far more effect than anything else.
