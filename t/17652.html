#327985 6 2007-06-06T11:33:05Z shoftee
Actually, you haven't included the sphere cut-out shapes in your graphic. The one you show here are a lot more complex, as you already confirmed.

Anyways, moving along to your problems.

The first thing you must understand is that you can "subtract" dimensions when doing these checks, in order to simplify them.
A sphere-plane check is a one-dimensional check (i.e 3D minus 2D) - you need a single distance value, a sphere-edge check is a two-dimensional check (3D minus 1D) - you need two distance values, and a sphere-point check is a three-dimensional check (3D minus 0D) - you need three distance values. Accordingly, a sphere-plane check is equivalent to a edge-point check, a sphere-edge check is equivalent to a plane-point check, and so on.

The box-cube check is pretty obvious, IMO, and you seem to have noticed that too... Now for the sphere-cube check. Skipping the sphere-plane and sphere-edge checks (which are pretty straight-forward and equivalents are already explained in the Metanet tutorials), we go right to the vertex VR case.
First of all, you need to know which the vertex closest to the sphere is... which is pretty simple, really. You take the delta between both centers (the sphere and the cube), and that'll result in three values. Then you take the sign of each component (represented with either +1 or -1) and multiply it by the cube's half-side. After that, you use the 3D Pythagoras theorem (d = x + y + z) to check the distance between the resulting point and the sphere's center. If you can wrap your mind around three dimensions (which I assume you can as you've undertaken such a challenge in the first place) things should go smoothly from there.

For the curved-ramp-box and curved-ramp-sphere, things are a bit more messy. Explaining exactly how to calculate the vectors would be a bit too time-consuming, but I could tell you the VRs you need to check. You have two full sides in the ramp (which makes 2 plane regions, 7 edge regions and 6 point regions) two 1/4 circle cut-outs (which are still easy to deal with, they're point-circle distance checks), two VRs for the curved edges (which are point-sphere distance checks), and the cylinder cut out - edge-sphere (which can be simplified to point-circle).
IMO, if you've managed to get the cube-sphere checks, the hardest part here are the curved edges.
The curved-ramp-box checks are easier - You don't have to worry about the curved edges as much. The hardest part here is the box-cylinder check (equivalent to a rectangle-circle check, which is already explained in the Metanet tutorials), so you should have relatively little problems with it.

That was, of course, the case for the curved ramps. The 45 degree ramps are a lot similar to those:
Again, two full sides (2 plane VRs, 7 edge VRs, 6 point VRs), two triangles (2 plane VRs), the edges on each side of the ramp (2 edge VRs) and the ramp itself (another plane VR)

The biggest problems come when you do the ramp-edge checks. The idea with them is like this: you have to check if the sphere is hitting an edge. That's a two-dimensional projection - one sphere-plane check for the ramp plane and one sphere-plane check for the plane with the triangle.

I think that wraps it up.

If you run into problems, I suggest you use pen and paper to clear things up. If that doesn't help, tell me and I'll try my best to make some diagrams for you.

Hope that helped,

I figured out the projection to a single axis, its accually alot easier that it seems. There is no need to project to planes first. I simply need to project each point to the axis, and take the smallest and largest values, and I then have the single axis interval. With an ellipsoid, all I need to do is project the center point, then find the raduis along the projection axis, and have the interval be plus and minus the radius from the center. The cylinder was trickier, but it turns out to be similar to the ellipsoid, I simply project the center of the circle at the top, and add the plus and minus points of that point, and do the same with the bottom. Then its simply the interval between the smallest and largest one dimentional point. This way I can simply apply a rotation matrix to the points to get the axis alligned orientations I need.

#341237 12 2007-07-10T05:07:15Z Raigan
If you're trying to find the shortest/longest vector, note that you can just use length-squared instead of length (avoiding the square root), so that you only need to do the √ on the shortest/longest vector. but personally i wouldn't worry about using √s all over the place until everything's working -- these days you're likely to run into cache-related speed problems before your bottleneck becomes expensive instructions.

For determining VR containment, note that each region is bounded by planes -- and those same planes share normals with some of the axes you'll be testing. For instance, on a cube, each face's external VR is bounded by the planes containing the 4 adjacent cube faces -- so if your point isn't behind all 4 of those planes, it can't be in the face's VR region. This is the same idea behind the 2d case you mentioned -- if you know (from an axis test) that a point is in front of a given plane, you can use this knowledge to eliminate any region behind the plane. I'm not sure exactly how you'd do that in a simple way though..
