The Paradox

The philosopher David Hume enjoyed playing billiards. He was well aware of the fact that when a moving ball strikes a stationary one (event A), the stationary one then starts to move (event B), according to Newton's Law of the Conservation of Energy. We would normally describe this occurrence by saying 'event A causes event B'.

Causality is a concept derived from the principle of induction, according to which we believe that something will happen in the future because it has occurred many times in the past. So we believe that the sun will rise tomorrow, why? Because it has risen many times before. Hume showed that however many times the sun has risen in the past, this does not prove that the sun will rise tomorrow, our belief is just that, a belief.

Going back to our statement 'event A causes event B', the problem is that however many times event B follows event A, it is not possible to say that there is any necessary connection between the two. So it is not valid to say that event A 'causes' event B, that would imply the forbidden necessary connection. All we can say is that in all known instances in the past, event A has been followed by event B. And we can say nothing about the future.

The paradox arises because the whole of science and engineering is based on the principle of causality. Empirically, causality works. Philosophically, it is meaningless.

What is the solution?

When Hume grasped just how paradoxical the concept of causality was, he started to doubt his reason. His reason told him that causality must be true, but his reason also dictated the opposite. To avoid having a nervous breakdown, he dropped the subject, and played billiards all day for nearly two years! This is dangerous territory.

Philosophers from Kant to Popper have tried and failed. Can we succeed?



Reference
David Hume: An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding - Section Vll
