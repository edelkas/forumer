The rate of radioactive decay can be likened to your standard exothermal reaction in chemistry. In such a reaction, the two reactants lose energy, becoming more stable. However, they often need a certain 'activation energy' to get started. Budding chemists quickly learn that to make a reaction happen you often have to heat it up: this gives the reactants the energy to react, so stuff happens.
For radioactive decay, heating the element will have no effect. Instead, the 'activation energy' required is received from quantum vacuum fluctuations (in laymans terms, spacetime spontaniously 'frothing' on a really tiny scale). This is in turn is a direct consequence of (or, more precisely, is described very well by) the Heisenburg uncertanty principle, which states that the uncertainty in the combined measurement of momentum (read: energy) and position of an object can never be less than (h/4[pi]). h is the Planck constant, which has been proven to be the same since pretty much the start of the universe.

So. Plancks constant is time-independant (it is the same regardless of when it is measured). This means that the Uncertainty Principle is equally time-independant (as all it includes is position, momentum and h). This means that the decay rate of a particular element is time-independant, as is the half-life. It all works.

#357064 16 2007-08-19T12:46:23Z iangb
Age of the earth - this is, indeed, due to rocks. Zircon crystals are amazingly resiliant rocks - they are formed in lava, pushed up into granite mountatains via tectonic plate movements, and end up back in the lava again as the mountain erodes and is slowly washed out to sea. Each time (in this cycle) that the crystal is in the lava it aquires a new outer shell containing (amongst other things) particles of uranium, but is otherwise unchanged. The uranium then decays in its closed environment, so the time at which each layer was laid down can be found using radiometric dating. This means that zircon crystals today have shells which are younger on the outside and older on the inside. Some of the inside shells are very old indeed.
Also, see The Age of the Earth (wiki).
