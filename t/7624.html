Ummm. You can't copyright colors. That's silly.

You can copyright information (like code), text, stories, images, icons, complex symbols, etc. Copyrights restrict the copying of an expression of an idea; not the idea itself.

Quote: blue_tetris, 2006-02-21T21:04:11Z
You cannot copyright names or phrases (only expressed concepts). You can copyright names as used in combination with certain ideas. You cannot copyright images (only the use of images). You cannot copyright color schemes! You can trademark them as a component of a mark or symbol. You can patent the specific production or formation of an image or game component.

You are correct insofar as you cannot obtain a trademark on the color orange for instance. However, you may obtain protection under trademark if the color obtains a "secondary meaning" which the applicant must prove. This is accomplished when "in the minds of the public, the primary significance of the mark is to identify the source of the product rather than the product itself." Inwood Laboratories v. Ives Laboratories, 456 U.S. 844, 851, n11 (1982)

This would mean that in the minds of the public the color scheme would have to identify Metanet software and not the game N. Of course this is difficult to prove because "the primary significance" to the public must indicate source. This is a formidable hurdle and one that is not easily overcome. Some factors include; extent of sales and advertising leading to buyer association, length of use, exclusivity of use, customer surveys, customer testimony, the size of the company, the number of sales, and the number of customers.

At this point, I would agree with you. I do not believe Metanet would succeed in this are for protection. However, Product Design might be applicable. Like a color, product design may be protectable if it acquires a "secondary meaning". The obstacles are the same as set forth above. This could be considered the products "Trade Dress". I believe it has been agreed that the simplicity of N is one of the major attributes which makes the game great. This would heavily weigh in favor of protectable content.

All of this being said, It is unclear if Metanet has an interest in protecting the color scheme of the product or if the original intention was to protect the Symbol or Mark of Metanet.

Educational factors are one of the best examples of fair use. The first step is to consider whether the use at issue falls into one of the specified FAVORED USES: "criticism, comment, news, reporting, teaching, scholarship, or research" If it does, then fair use is more likely. However, the above mentioned factors must be taken into accord with the following. The purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of commercial nature or if it is for nonprofit educational purposes. (I believe this school project would fall into this category and weighs in favor of fair use) The nature of the copyrighted work. (a simple color scheme and tutorial code provided by Metanet to use in educating yourself on collision detecting) The amount or substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; (I believe this would fall short of a substantial amount of copyrighted work as viewed in relation to the whole program of N) and the effect of the use upon the potential market or value of the copyrighted work. (N is free and Metanet will not lose or gain anything by the use stated in the first post of this thread). In addition, if the latter project takes on a transformative use, meaning that the potential infringer added "independent creative material" to the work, then it is more than likely Fair Use.
