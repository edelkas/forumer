Quote: Atilla, 2007-11-15T04:23:56Z
Again, it doesn't matter what people want to do. The fact is that over the medium and long term, it is more economically sound to buy a hybrid car.

No! It's not.

http://www.edmunds.com/help/about/press/105827/article.html

I link back to this-here article. Most drivers can't expect to reach the break-even point until five to ten years after the purchase. Even then, if you factor for the time-value of money, there were many more economical decisions that could have been made which would supplant an equivalent cost.

A perfectly sound purchasing decision would involve a gas car, and a savings account (with other assets already in it) to carry the remainder that was saved by not buying a hybrid. In the long run, this would be more economic.

Additionally, return must equal risk. The risks of investing in a hybrid on a purely financial basis are very high: Will we drive it enough to get its worth back? Will gas prices increase beyond the expected 3%, justifying this over a gas car? Will shops exist to help maintain the vehicle, and what will extra maintenance costs likely become? In ten years, the money that you will start saving may take a looooong time to equal out with other things you could have used that money to do, otherwise. In this case, the deferred costs, like those of a gas car, will be more financially sound than the large lump sum cost of a hybrid.

Quote: Atilla, 2007-11-15T04:23:56Z
Also, the middle-class frequently fork over a lot money for long-term investments. Buying a house, for instance. Many people also invest in shares or term deposits, or make voluntary contributions to their superannuation.

Right. But the most economical decision is the one which pays the most for the given investment period. As time goes on, the returns should be higher. And, dollar for dollar, a person with the ability to invest could save money on a gas car and other investments than on a hybrid car and no other investments.

Which is the soundest investment: I invest $500 today and get $600 back, a year from now. I invest $1000 today and get $1300 back, two years from now. I invest $2000 and get $3000 back, five years from now.

It'd be foolish to say the later ones are better just because they're more. In truth, the first has a significantly better rate. More money paid later isn't always better than less money paid now, because money is capital and can be invested alternatively. A lot of people feel smart or safe when they pay money into an investment to reap eventual reward, even if they don't analyze how much they'll be getting. You need to evaluate the actual costs and returns to find out which is the most economical.

These financial analyses have been performed, and they show that the break-even point is very far down the road. It's highly likely that I could invest the several thousand dollar difference (on bonds) and have interest payments equal to the difference in gas charges after ten years of driving.
