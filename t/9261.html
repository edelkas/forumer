#169265 15 2006-04-28T18:59:49Z blue_tetris
I think the new name is more marketable. The only people who were bent on using the name "Revolution" were the people who wanted to buy one anyway. Let's be honest. If you didn't want a "Revolution", that project name carried no meaning to you. The fact is, anyone who wanted to buy it when it was called Revolution will still want to buy it now.

The new name "Wii" is likely as bizarre to us as the controller and console is to everyone else. Wii will be a system unlike those others. It's not your typically next generation console. I've said it before and I'll say it again: people will eventually get tired of buying the same system over and over again. They'll want a new experience, not just better games and graphics. The new name "Wii" symbolizes the shift in paradigm that the company has made. It also allocates Nintendo to a different marketing niche.

Previously, the Revolution occupied much the same mental space as the Xbox 360. It had a dark, dispassionate sound. "Revolution" was a name fitting of a monolith of black and chrome, with a dim violet glow emanating from its casing. The new name fits the look of the console. It is cleverly terse, looks as much like a picture as it does a word, and seems more universal. It doesn't really appeal to the kind of gamer who would prefer games entitled "BloodSpawn" and "Typical Racing Game #27". The name "Wii" is revolutionary. It's what the old name wanted to be.

Also, Wii looks better written on white plastic. It almost looks like the name of the next Apple product. Incidentally, both companies would probably prefer we "thinki different" before we rush into making our next console decision.

Anyway, if you figure that Wii is to Nintendo as New Coke was to Coca-Cola, I think you're wrong. Something tells me that this name will fly for a number of reasons--the chief reason being that it doesn't cater to the people that already like Nintendo. It has placed itself in a different marketing tier from PlayStation and Xbox. I think that'll serve Wii well.

I reiterate: Wii is to intended market a different kind of console, not just a different console.

#169594 38 2006-04-29T18:51:24Z blue_tetris
I still don't know who the hell decided "Play Station" was a good console name. Let alone "Game Boy" and "X Box". If you really think about it, X Box is an atrocious name. It is downright retarded. You're playing a box. And if that weren't awesome enough, it's an X box.

I think Nintendo has done nothing but defy naming logic. Revolution sounded hardcore. Not friendly. Not highly fun. And certainly not unique. It sounded like a rehashing of other names. The word revolution was simply and inherently cool word like X or 360. Not only did "Revolution" not enhance the quality of the system, it didn't enhance the marketability either.

The name "Revolution" filled the same mental niche as the name X-Box 360. It was just a hunk of pretentious awesomeness. It wasn't going to sell the system. The naming logic that turns Nintendo into "Nintendo Revolution" is the same naming logic that turns Coke into "New Coke". It's just like slapping the word "NEW" on the side of everyone else's gaming devices and telling them to buy it over again. Why people who bought the X-Box are even considering buying a new X-Box 360 is beyond me. I think the time to make a new kind of gaming device is now.

And I think a new kind of gaming device needs a new kind of name.

So, I've said it before and I'll say it again: The name will grow on you. It diversifies the product from its industry. In time, the seemingly lame name will fit the system, in the same way that Game Boy (a very very retarded name... please, think about it for a moment) has found away into our language. Names that catch on quick have led to unmitigated failures in the gaming industry. Saturn, Dreamcast, Neo-Geo...

Someone said it best here: http://blog.wired.com/games/index.blog?entry_id=1467976

An important part of that article explains how normal individuals (who don't spend most of their time playing video games or browsing forums) have difficultly calling up the names of systems. The naming components are interchangeable. "GameStation 360", "PlayBox", and "X-Cube 3" are all applicable console names for the incumbent naming rules. Moreover, they lack brand identity. You and I know that X-Box games and PlayStation games are dissimilar. That's because we're gamers. And it's easy to sell games to gamers. However, to distinguish a product to a fanbase who doesn't ordinarily purchanse that product, you need to develop a competetive advantage and brand identity. Wii does that. Its competetive advantage is that it is novel. It certainly has more identity than "X-Station Revolutions 360". It meets all standards for marketability, from an analytical standpoint.

The truth is, gamers have been taught to look past a name. Gamers buy products based on the company that makes them, the tech specs, and the future games. Gamers have a more discerning eye than the rest of the market base. So, it's no feat to sell games to gamers if you've got the right technology. However, in order to expand your market base instead of retail games to the industry elites, you need to develop a marketing campaign--something that Sony and Microsoft haven't done for a long time. You need to attract new consumers and develop a strong customer relationship with them by offering an image.

Wii is going to do that. It'll attract all the gamers who appreciate the technology (and the gamers who don't appreciate the technology won't buy the system no matter what it's called); and it'll attract people looking for new consumer electronics. Not necessarily gamers. The populace.

Wii has a lot of marketability. Take my word for it.

Importantly, it doesn't fill the same marketing niche as X-Box and PlayStation. That means it will be in a marginally different industrial bracket with a marginally different classification of demand. A marginally different demand classification is good for a gaming company, because it means that it benefits cheifly from existing demand curves for video games, while isolating itself from brand competition.

*gasps*

In sum, Nintendo made the right decision.
