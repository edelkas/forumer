#353067 44 2007-08-09T15:47:30Z Tsukatu
Obby:

The problem with increasing the size of insects (*cough*ascoveredbyanymiddleschoolbiologyclass*cough*) is that expanding something by some linear dimension means that its volume will go up by the cube of that amount, and two-dimensional things like surface area only expand by the square of that amount. Surface area is very, very important to know if you're going to figure, say, whether or not the skin will be able to contain the bloody meats of the organism inside of it.
Imagine a cube that's 1 arbitrary unit on each side. Its volume is 1 au, and its surface area is 6 au - that ratio is 1:6, which is pretty safe.
Let's double its dimensions. Now it's 2 au's on each side. Its volume is now 8 au, and its surface area is 24 au - that ratio is also 1:3. The surface of the cube has to work harder to contain that volume.
Let's keep going.
4 au's on a side: volume is 64 au and surface area is 96 au; a 2:3 ratio.
8 au's on a side: volume is 512 au and surface area is 384; a 4:3 ratio.
...and if we get to 10 au's so we can apply this directly to the ant thing: volume is 1000 au and surface area is 600 au; a 5:3 ratio.
Basically, as size increases, the skin has to work much harder to contain the volume.
That's a factor of 10 different than the skin's used to - it now has to work 10 times as hard to contain the ant's bloody meats. And that doesn't apply to just the exoskeleton. The ant's lungs will tear as soon as it takes a breath, its stomach will rip open the first time it eats, and the heart will explode when the ant takes its first step. This doesn't even bring into consideration the fact that it would require dangerously low blood pressure so as not to tear its veins when (and if) its heart makes its first pulse, and we're of course assuming that this ant won't be trying to actually move anywhere.
So how do we help this thing live? Well, we can start by increasing the thickness of every surface area it has tenfold. This doesn't come close to solving the problem, however, since an increase in stomach lining, vein thickness, etc., is even more of an increase in volume, which ends up requiring the exoskeleton to be up to 100 times as thick (1000x thicker than its original size) just to contain the bare minimum required for the ant to continue a fragile, sedentary existence.
If we were to set this thing into motion right now, with its massively increased weight, it wouldn't be strong enough to expand its carapace enough to draw in a single breath. Let's fix that. To move, things need muscle, right? Aww, hell, that sounds like an awful lot more volume, and dense volume, at that. Already this ant's exoskeleton is 100 times thicker than it should be, meaning that it's basically something of an organic rock that, deep down inside, has some barely functioning organ system, and now we want to put more stuff in it?
