Hmm, let's see. About religion... I would say that it's generally a negative thing, because it supports intellectual dishonesty and general irrationality. Mysticism (an idea that most religions advocate) holds that there are other ways to learn about the world, apart from reason and observation. This philosophy is anti-reality and destructive. Most religions give people bad advice, too. Morality doesn't spring forth from religion as many would claim. Normative ethics can be determined through purely logical means. Although it may not have been their original intent, many religions have been modified in order to control people, take money from them, or both. I believe that these things are true because I have observed the world, and I've read the holy books, and this is what I got from them.

About god... "god" is defined a number of ways, and I have different thoughts about different god-type beings. I believe that many gods have been deliberately defined in ways that are not verifiable so that religious people can go on believing in them. The idea that "God works in mysterious ways" would be an example of this. If it's so mysterious, there's no way to know that it's god who's doing the work in the first place, and if something bad happens, we can just say that it's God's mystery, instead of actually thinking about the implications. So, yeah, most gods are generally unverifiable, which makes the whole idea essentially useless.

Many concepts of "god" are inherently contradictory. Defining god as "outside of time" would be an example. God, as I understand the concept, is the creator of the universe. A being that... does stuff... has to exist within time. God cannot be conscious if he doesn't exist inside some dimension of time, because consciousness is a process, a process is a series of events, and that series could only take place in some kind of time. Another sentiment that I hear quite often is that god is immune to the laws of reason. Statements like this are completely ridiculous, and are necessarily false. A statement that follows the form "[blank - noun] is [different blank]" assumes that the laws of reason apply to whatever it is that you're talking about. The axioms involved are so basic that I know of pretty much no human that would deny them.

So I'm an agnostic atheist toward some gods, but I think it's impossible for some versions to exist at all, making me an anti-theist with regard to those gods.

I believe that invoking the supernatural in general is not a good way to explain things. In fact, I would say that supernatural explanations aren't really explanations at all. A supernatural explanation does nothing to explain how or why something happened. Now, let me be clear about this. If a ball drops and someone says "it dropped because there's a natural force that makes stuff drop" that's not really much more of an explanation than "it dropped because Jesus rays hit it down." Where these two paths differ is in testing and learning more about the dropping ball. If there's a natural law that makes things drop, then we can test it and develop mathematical models and the like. We can investigate further. Saying that unverifiable, untestable things, like "Jesus rays" hit stuff down doesn't provide a direction for exploration. We cannot examine or improve explanations which we cannot test. This is why the supernatural is a useless idea.

I have more thoughts, but I don't really feel like typing them. If you want to know what I think about something, feel free to ask.

Oh, yeah. For irreducible complexity, I have an argument by analogy.

It's possible to build a stone arch with nothing sticking the stones together (example). If you remove any one of the stones, the whole arch collapses. The arch is therefore irreducibly complex. Removing one part makes the arch "die." However, it's possible to make a big pile of stones into an arch by removing the stones from the center, leaving only the loose arch on top. Similar arguments can be made for biological constructs. A nervous system might be irreducibly complex, but there might have been (at some point in evolutionary history) a "scaffold" of sorts that kept the nervous system functional while necessary parts were evolving. When the nervous system's new parts develop, they work so much more efficiently than the old "scaffold" parts that they completely replace the scaffold, and it is no longer necessary, and so it often goes away.

There's a problem, here. Theories, by definition, only explain a specific range of data (we don't have a theory of everything). It makes no sense to reject a theory because it doesn't explain data that it wasn't developed to explain. Evolutionary theory, for example, does *not* explain the origin of life - abiogenesis does. But if you think the origin of life is suspect, that does not give you any good reason to doubt evolution's truth, because evolution doesn't deal with life's origin, only what happens once life exists. Similarly, the fact that there are unanswered questions in cosmology doesn't mean that you can reject the big bang. The big bang theory was developed to explain phenomena like the spiral shapes of galaxies, or the expansion of the universe, or cosmic microwave background radiation (well, that was predicted, but still...). The Big Bang explains all of these, as it was developed to do. It may not explain everything about the origin of the universe, but that doesn't lessen its truth. What happened "before" the singularity (if there was a singularity - see below) may not really be defined, anyway.

Second, not every Big Bang model starts with a singularity. Some models start with an approximate singularity, just a hot, dense center.
