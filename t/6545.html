#128583 4 2005-12-30T23:26:01Z sweep
Switch!


Highlight the white text below for spoiler:
Initially there is a 1/3 chance that the contestant picked the correct door. So, in this 1/3 of cases, switching doors will cause a loss.

However, in the 2/3 of cases where the contestant picked the incorrect door initially, the correct door is one of the two on the other side. Since the host always removes an incorrect door, switching in these 2/3 of cases always results in a win.
So... the probably is 1/3 for a win if you stay with your original pick versus 2/3 for a win if you switch.


It's hard to understand at first, but I came across this riddle a while ago in the excellent book "The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-time". Think about it hard, it makes perfect sense when you understand it.

#129435 16 2006-01-03T11:28:16Z blue_tetris
Oldest trick in the book--a favorite of statistic professors, to be sure.

For those among you who still can't understand it:
http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Hall_problem

This works regardless of how many doors there are. In fact, the more doors you have to pick from initially, the more likely you'll pick correctly when there are only two doors left to choose from. It may be easier to view the problem with higher numbers:

There are 100 doors. Only one of them has a car behind it, the rest have nothing. You pick a door at random. Then, the host reveals what was behind 98 of the doors (all of which contain nothing). He leaves you with just your door and one other. One of those doors has a car behind it. You'd be a fool not to switch. The only time when it would be a bad idea to switch is when you picked the correct door initially--which is a one in a hundred chance. 99 out of 100 times, you'd pick the wrong door and would benefit from switching.

The same logic applies to the initial problem.

You get to pick one of three doors at random. Your initial chance of getting the correct door is 1 out of 3. Your chance of getting the wrong door is 2 out of 3. So, when given the chance to switch, 2 out of 3 times you'll be switching from bad door to good door and 1 out of 3 times you'll be switching from good door to bad door.

#162733 57 2006-04-07T03:12:29Z Tsukatu
What helped me get it:
When you pick one of the three doors, you're giving yourself a 1 in 3 chance. That means that there's a 2 in 3 chance that what you're looking for is in the group of doors we can call "the rest of the doors." The host's opening one incorrect door for you doesn't improve your chances as much as it redefines the objective to be "pick a group of doors, and you win the prize if the group of doors you selected contained the prize," and dividing the three into two groups: (1) the door you initially picked and (2) the two others. We've already seen that (1) has a 1 in 3 success rate, and that (2) has a 2 in 3 success rate. It is most logical to switch over.

You can extend this example to 100 doors, with the host eliminating all but two doors, just as he did when you only had 3 doors in total. This means that there are two groups that you can choose from: the group that is 1% likely to include your door, or the group that is 99% likely to include your door.

If you don't get this group business, let's try common sense:
There are four hundred and eight billiondy doors in front of you. One contains a piece of candy, and all of the others contain agonizing death. You pick one at random, since that's the best you can do. The host removes all of the choices except for two: going down the line of doors, he skips over the door you chose and one other particular door. You know that the candy is behind either the door you chose or the door remaining, and he only left your door closed because it's the one you picked. I should hope that you choose to switch.
