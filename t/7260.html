The key word here is "generally". FF is not generally. It is a series renouned for having very strong storylines. Furthermore, I disagree entirely with your belief that movies generally have stronger storylines than games. And the story IS (part of) the substance of a good RPG. You're trying to compare them to action games, which isn't really valid because the whole concept and goal is different.

As I said, the combat is definately the weak point of FF, since there is little skill involved (although there is some skill in choosing the right equipment and right party for any given situation). This is not true of all RPGs, however. Guild Wars is an MMORPG which requires considerable skill. Try also the Phantasy Star Online series although it's an ARGP. D&D-based games require correct tactics to make the most of your party members' skills (should you cast web first, or try to disintegrate the enemy mage? Should your rogue try to backstab someone, or lurk where it's safe and use her bow?), and of course you have to choose the right skills in the first place. Diablo and its sequels require you to know the strengths and weaknesses of various enemies, and of your own character (chain lightning or fireball? Perhaps lurking with a ranged weapon would work? Or should you just run in and hack it as quickly as possible?). Baten Kaitos, as I mentioned earlier, has a rather unique card-based battle system which requires quick eyes and very quick fingers.* Then, of course, there are strategy-RPGs like the Front Mission series. These RPGs require substantial skill on behalf of the player. FF, regretfully, less so.

*It's like this. All actions and items are represented by cards. So to attack, you might use the "sabre" card, while to defend you would use a Robe of Fire, chainmail etc. Each card has up to four numbers on it, associate with four directions on the second analogue stick. Getting a straight (eg. 8 7 6 5 4) or only using sets of cards with the same number (4444 or 443333, but not 4443 or 44432) will increase the damage/defensive power/healing of the cards you use. Furthermore, there are six elements arranged in pairs (water/fire, light/dark, wind/time) plus physical damage. Only the opposing element reduces damage from an elemental attack, only physical defense prevents physical damage. After you pick the first card, you have about half a second to pick each subsequent card. In the later parts of the game, you can use nine cards in sequence. It takes a bit of skill to regularly match the numbers on 9 cards without using any conflicting elements.
