Answer in white:
[answer]
Let's number the pirates 1 through 10, 1 being the youngest and 10 being the oldest.
If it was down to the two oldest pirates, 9 and 10, 9 would give himself all of the gold and leave nothing for 10. 9 would vote for, 10 would vote against, and so 9 is left with everything because more than half of the pirates have to vote against to override the decision.
If it was down to 8, 9, and 10, with 8 dividing the gold, 9 wants 8 dead regardless of how he divides it because with 8 dead he can take 99 gold for himself. 8 knows this, and so he needs to give some 10 some incentive to vote for his division. He knows that 10 would get no gold if it were down to 9 and 10, so 8 gives 10 a single gold coin. For 10, it comes down to getting 1 gold coin or 0 gold coins, so he votes for 8's plan. 8 give himself 99 and gives 10 a single coin, 8 and 10 vote for, 9 votes against. The division is accepted.
If it was down to four pirates, 7, 8, 9, and 10, 7 understands that 8 wants him dead regardless of how he divides it, because with 7 dead, 8 has his foolproof plan to get 99 gold for himself. With four pirates, 9 has no hope of getting any gold. He knows that if he votes against 7 that 8 will just take the gold anyway, and the result is the same: 9 has no gold. 9 doesn't have a reason to vote for or against. Seeing this, 7 gives 9 a single coin, and leaves nothing for 8, who wants to kill 9 regardless, and for 10, because he can afford to get the votes against as close to 50% as he can without going over. 7 gives himself 99 gold and gives 9 a single coin. 7 and 9 vote for, 8 and 10 vote against. The division is accepted.
With five pirates, 7 wants 6 dead regardless. 6 would need to give 8 and 10 incentive a single coin each, because they'd get nothing from 7 normally. 6, 8, and 10 vote for, 7 and 9 vote against. The division is accepted.
6 wants 5 dead regardless, and 5 knows that 6 won't give any to 7 or 9, so he gives them a single coin each. 5 gives himself 98 coins and gives 7 and 9 one coin each. 5, 7, and 9 vote for, 6, 8, and 10 vote against. The number of voters against wasn't high enough (needed to be more than half), so the division is accepted.

#245952 55 2006-10-26T03:37:30Z blue_tetris
Quote: Tsukatu, October 24, 2006 08:34 pm
As soon as there are more than three pirates, the second oldest has absolutely no chance of getting more than 1 gold. None. This is because the third oldest will not be killed when he offers the oldest pirate more than the nothing that the second oldest would give him. Seeing this, the second oldest pirate will vote for any resolution that gives him the most gold with which he can walk away from this situation - 1 gold.
In a real life situation, though, game theory says that a player's death would be highly undesirable. Likely equitable to -1000 gold, or some such other value, depending on the nature of the player. Therefore, the first pirate shouldn't measure the risk as being between 100 gold and 0 gold. The first pirate should measure the risk between 100 gold and death. Moreover, it's bad to think that the other pirates are going to be entirely logical and understand the game theory fully.

In most games, it's best to assume some of the other players are going to be acting without full knowledge. In fact, it's more wise to assume that some people are illogical and act according to those principles.

In the prisoner's dilemma game, two perfectly logical players will assume the other player will "remain silent" and will therefore also remain silent. However, in practice, the game degrades into accusations and manipulation, as a player has a greater game pay-off if they happen to "confess" when the other player "remains silent". Most often, even the most logical players will eventually play the game with everything but logic weighing their decisions. They'll succumb to emotions (like vengeance) and the gambler's fallacy (which will make them believe an event is more likely because it has occured more often in the past; or that because an event has occured a lot recently, it's most likely that a different event will occur because "it's about time").

All of these things manipulate and interfere with what could be called game logic, and some of these factors would render reason more valuable than logic.
