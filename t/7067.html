The same thing applies to "you'll die when the universe ends" and so on.

As chaos theory nicely states, very tiny changes can have massive outcomes, so those sub-atomic differences can eventually result in a macroscopic (ie real-world) change. So ultimately, the future is not predictable to any great certainty, and that certainty decreaces further with time, and size (of the system that is being predicted).

#151153 59 2006-03-03T17:00:54Z Tsukatu
iangb and Teh Dude:

The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle has to do with observation and our ability to predict present and future states. Using it to state that interaction of matter is chaotic, that events are unpredictable on a theoretical level, or that free will exists because of it is not a correct application, however.
The fact is that, even though we don't have a practical (or any, for that matter) means of finding out specific present and future states of things, does not mean that an exact, definitive outcome does not exist. In fact, an exact, definitive outcome is assured to exist unless there is something supernatural affecting the natural universe (i.e. God), and in all other cases, every bit and piece of the natural universe will follow a set, unchanging set of laws and will act in one way, and one way only, even if it's not possible to predict it.

The fact that you cannot predict present and future states of matter gives us the illusion of free will.

People also have a tendency to... "humanize" things. It's a perfectly normal thing to do, and it sure helps us in our conventional, everyday lives, but it becomes a terrible burden in discussions of science and philosophy. By "humanizing" things, I don't mean we anthropomorphize things. I mean that we look at ourselves as independent systems in a bigger system, instead of one massive, jumbled mess of matter, which is what we really are.
When I tried to explain what I meant to people in the past, the following example helped:
Imagine your skin. Zoom in until you can see individual skin cells. You're probably imagining looking top-down at your skin cells, so rotate your vision about 90 degrees so that you can see your skin cells in contact with the air. Zoom in even closer until you can see the individual lipids that make up the phospholipid bilayer on your skin cell membrane. Keep zooming in until you can see the atoms that make up the lipids and can see the atoms of the air next to it. Consider that the only thing keeping those lipids, that cell, and the cells that make up your skin, together is the fact that they have a slightly larger electromagnetic attraction to each other than they do to the air (especially N2, which is totally unattractive to just about anything).
Kind of wierd to think about, isn't it? On the atomic level, you're not a person, just a particular chunk of matter that, when looking purely at the atomic structure of one area of the universe, is very hard to distinguish from any other area with an equivalent density of matter (such as the ground, air, or another organism). If you look on that level, you'll pretty much see what's at the top of the attached picture.
Anyway, because of this tendency to think this way, we often take a more spiritual look at philosophy as opposed to a more sensical scientific look. You are not special. You are a clump of atoms, just like the ground, sky, and seas.
