#55813 55 2005-03-24T23:28:56Z Mare (guest)
Quote: brickman, 2005-03-24T16:48:03Z
The idea of shareware is to make people like your game and not want to have to stop playing it, not to show them a faint glimmer of hope that the game COULD become good.

That really isn't the definition of shareware -- that's maybe the idealistic definition.
Besides that though, I think it's most important to note that regardless of what Gish is, it is 100% NOT commercial.

Forgive the following rant, but 'commercial' vs. 'independent' is one of those issues we feel is not well understood.



The definition of what makes a developer independent is not very clear at the moment -- there are many different types of developers who classify themselves as independent, each with their own motivations. For example, some believe _financial_ independence is the parameter necessary to allow a developer to be classified as "independent".

Some developers see 'independent' as a label they can wear in order to help realize their goals of working at a "real" game company like EA or SCE. Like a way to develop their portfolio/resume.

For others, there's much more to it than that -- "independent" denotes a system of beliefs, and a motivation to succeed outside of an industry we love, but feel gives too much focus to creating a product for the sole purpose of making money. We feel the goal instead should be to make great games that are worth playing.

As insinuated, we (me and Raigan) fall into the latter category, and so does Chronic Logic (among others).

The commercial part of the game industry works much like that of the movie or music industry -- with financial backing from producers, artists (analog to game developers) parade their wares to publishers, through agents, who make deals with the studios.

Typically, publishing through this channel requires an artist to comprimise, or to sacrifice certain things in order to assure commercial success -- which means a loss of creative control on the part of the artist, and a gain of control on the part of the publisher or studio.

This may seem like a great idea -- put the game in the hands of people who KNOW what will sell to the public. They've made tons of money before, they can do it again. And sometimes this works well.

But often, publishers are far more willing to green light a project which presents non-innovative gameplay, style, substance etc with a metaphorical 'fresh coat of paint' than they are to represent a concept or graphical style demonstrating innovation, simply because the tried-and-true method is just that: it's proven to work in the past. Why bother fixing what's 'not broken'?

But we've seen this fail over and over in the music and film industry. The amount of crappy, derivative music and vapid, uninspired film produced in the course of a year far outweighs the really interesting or unique creations, at least in the commercial part of the industry. That's not an accident!

It's mostly due to the fact that there's so much risk involved on the part of all parties, especially financial risk. First off, it costs a hell of a lot to develop a commercial-style game these days, and no wonder. That money has to go to paying executives, managers, 3D animators (as the case may be), artists, programmers, team leaders, console programmers, biz dev, marketing, testers, etc etc etc ad nauseum.

profit generated is split a whole bunch, and little gets back to the artist, who by now has essentially only provided the basic concept for the end product. The rewards from doing that are so small, compared to the energy required.

(that's true at least typically, and in Western culture (with the exception of the "auteurs") -- I'll spare you a comparison to the ways other cultures operate)

The main point of that explanation is that games developed for commercial purposes are often more "safe" because of the risks of failure.


Independent artists and game developers have the freedom to really innovate -- to take an interesting concept and run with it, so to speak, because they're not risking on behalf of someone else. Whether a game is great or horrible, it all reflects back on the developer -- that's an important distinction. In the end, indie developers like us, and Chronic Logic, are doing it because we love making games, and there are games we want to play but which don't yet exist.

If it turns out a game isn't really so much fun (parallel in our case: Ultramagnetic), what would be the point of releasing it?

As it must be by now obvious, independent developers have a huge mountain to climb: commercial game dev companies have huge budgets and revenue, established brands and titles, and generally take up a lot of space in the game market. It's hard to produce something that will generate enough recognition, and beyond that, enough money to allow you to keep doing what you love. The solution thus far has been to resist trying to compete in an arena which is out of your league.

As an analogy, lightweight boxers compete in their own 'league', independent of the heavyweight league both because there's really no contest, and because it's far more important in the sport that the playing ground is level.


So indie developers necessarily must investigate new business models, like shareware, which will help generate revenue without requiring too large a shift in ideology (or selling out).

There's the standard shareware model, where you get a time or content limited demo and must register to unlock the whole thing.

There's also the Soldat (www.soldat.pl) model, in which you get the whole game for free, but registering unlocks bonus things like coloured smoke in your jet boots, and puts a star next to your name when you're playing online. This type of registration states more obviously what's really happening here:


when you register a shareware (or similar type) game, you're showing the developer you like what they're doing and you want to offer your support so they can continue to make games.


For us, that's sort of what the Audience Award was too. Everyone who voted for us essentially showed that they appreciate what we did with N. It's partially what's motivating us to devote the upcoming year to making our games full time.

So I hope I've shown through contrast what commercial can mean -- operating within the industry under specific terms.


Now to bring it back to your suggestion that Gish is commercial:

Chronic Logic is an independent developer who self-publish their games, and they are doing it successfully enough that they're able to keep making their own games. They're motivated by a love of good games, and a desire to retain their independence and control over their product -- and also, we met them and they're totally great guys. That's giving us a lot of hope: they make it clear that it's possible to survive in this industry when you're doing your own thing.

But the business model alone doesn't determine the type of game it is -- ie Gish isn't automatically commercial because it isn't freeware, or did what you consider a poor job of shareware.

Also note that, although an independent developer could make a commercially successful or a commercial-style game, commercial developers by definition can't make independent games, and I think the risk involved in making an independent-style game (whatever that may be) would be too great.

We too are investigating business models that will generate more money than freeware has, because we know what we love doing: making good games. We'll note your comments on the shareware model though, Brickman.


And regarding N and Gish being in the same league, we actually think that that specifically was pretty fair: small budgets, small teams, lots of style and good gameplay (oh, and physics )
