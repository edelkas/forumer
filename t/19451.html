On May 12, 1998, massive riots sparked by shootings at a student demonstration overtook the Indonesian capital city of Jakarta on the island of Java, and became a pogrom directed at people of Chinese descent. Much speculation exists about the causes of the riots, and whether the Indonesian army and government supported the riots-what is known is that during the riots, much of Jakarta descended into anarchy. Massive gangs began roaming the city, breaking, entering, and looting buildings, mainly those owned by or occupied by people of Chinese descent, often referred to as the ethnic Chinese, who were targeted because many are well-off, which is resented by the Indonesian ethnic majority, a situation similar to pre-World War II Germany. Many ethnic Chinese were killed, and a substantial number of Chinese women and girls were violently raped. After the riots, independent investigations repeatedly indicated that sections of the Indonesian military were actively involved in planning and executing the riots. This was denied by the Indonesian army; however, evidence indicates that the military was indeed involved in the riots.
The riots were carried out in protest against the government of President Suharto of Indonesia. Suharto was responsible for the pseudo-fascist New Order in Indonesia which was responsible for numerous human-rights violations. Suharto also presided over high unemployment and inflation, food shortages, and rampant corruption in the government. The riots were part of the Indonesian Revolution of 1998, in which Suharto was removed from office, and ended with the installation of President Habibe, a little-known man with so many academic qualifications that BBC News remarked that he has a fetish for academic titles. At the time he took power, BBC compared him to Napoleon, Francisco Franco, and Haile Selassie, but his rule, while corrupt, was not as draconian as that of the aforementioned leaders. In this sense, the riots may be considered a success in that they finally deposed a corrupt official; however, the death toll and massive injustices incurred implies that the ends most certainly did not justify the means.
Minor rioting had been a problem in Jakarta in 1997 and early 1998. Indeed, in early 1998, fear was common among ethnic Chinese in Jakarta that a repeat of the ethnic violence that claimed thousands of lives in the 1960s was immanent. Animosity toward ethnic Chinese was caused by the view that they are more loyal to China than they are to Indonesia; these views were reinforced by Chinas policy until the late 1980s of awarding citizenship to ethnic Chinese born in Indonesia and other areas of Southeast Asia. The most prominent evidence that the preceding minor riots had a racial element was some shopkeepers practice of putting up signs on the store window reading Belonging to an Indonesian. Most such shops were left untouched by rioters.
In the month before the riots, there were dozens of student protests and demonstrations against the corrupt regime of then-Indonesian President Suharto occurring throughout Indonesia. Since the late 1980s, pressure had been building on Suharto, who had ruled Indonesia since 1966, to resign. Supporters cited reports of corruption. His re-appointment in March of 1998 strengthened these views. The final spark occurred during a student demonstration at Triskati University in Jakarta when police opened fire on student demonstrators. Rioting broke out and spread like an oil spill; by May 13th, almost all of Jakarta had descended into anarchy.
Although the riots were carried out in protest of the government, racial tension and military intervention quickly turned the riots against the ethnic Chinese (descendants of Chinese immigrants to Indonesia), similar to Kristallnacht in Germanyshops owned by ethnic Chinese had their windows smashed in and were looted, homes of ethnic Chinese were raided, and many ethnic Chinese were killed. One major divergence from the similarity to Kristallnacht were the mass rapes-many of which were not reported until several years after the riots, and which the government refused to acknowledge had occurred. One anecdote later made public told of how rioters gathered around an apartment building which housed many Chinese, entered, and raped at least 50 of the occupants.
The non-human causes of the Jakarta riots included food shortages, unemployment, and human rights violations. According to BBC News, Indonesia before 1998 was ...a society which never shows its feelings and it did so with a vengeance, in what was literally an orgy of violence. The riots were reportedly sparked during a student protest at Triskati University in Jakarta when Indonesian military officers opened fire. Though the government has not acknowledged it, it is possible that the shooting was intended to start a riot; the Volunteer Team for Humanity notes that much of the destruction was carried out in a similar, almost carefully planned matter, and asks how can coincidence explain the similarity of the weird fact pattern, that the destroy/burning action invitor-leader didnt take a part in the plunder act. Even in many cases, the invitor-leader immediately left the mass who start to move themselves to destroy and plunder. (sic). This implies that a smaller, more informed group of people were actively influencing the riots, and causing them to occur in the manner that they did; rampant corruption in the military is a very likely cause.
One of the most notable causes-alternatively, one of the most obvious-of the poor economic situation in Indonesia was the 1997 East Asian financial crisis. Beginning with the collapse of the Thai baht in July 1997, many East Asian countries, including Thailand, South Korea, and Indonesia entered a severe economic depression that lasted over a year, and spread to other countries in Asia, including Russia, as well as to Latin America. As a result of the crisis, the currency of many Southeast Asian countries fell, and the rapid development prior to the crisis stalled. The crisis drove already severe unemployment higher, and fueled rising discontent in Indonesia.
The 1997 East Asian financial crisis was similar to a 19th-century era bank run; investors quickly attempted to remove their money from what they suddenly saw as an unstable East Asian market. These feelings of instability may have been compounded by the 1997 British surrender of Hong Kong to China, since the fate of that powerful economy was as of yet unknown. This insecurity, plus the recent raise in US interest rates following the end of the economic recession of the early 1990s, began to draw investors away from East Asian markets. This came to a climax in Thailand in 1997, when the Thai baht was attacked by speculators and soon collapsed. A domino effect extended to the rest of the East Asian market, including Malaysia and Indonesia.
While the principal causes were economic woes, discontent with the Suharto regime had been festering since the 1960s; however, with the collapse of the Soviet Union, Western powers were no longer afraid of Indonesia becoming a communist state and therefore did not worry as much about angering the Indonesian government. Since the West no longer looked favorably on Suhartos culling of his opponents, many of Indonesias youth grew more vocal about their dissatisfaction with the Suharto regime. Discontent with Suharto became especially prevalent following the 1997 East Asian financial crisis. Historically, many Indonesians had tolerated the Suharto regime, despite corruption and human-rights abuses, because Suhartos regime had brought prosperity to many Indonesians and good economic development to the country as a whole. However, when this prosperity was shattered in the financial crisis, Suhartos public support quickly declined. This was boosted when Suharto was reappointed again in March 1998, and protests spread against the Suharto regime, eventually leading up to the fateful Triskati protest.
So what made the riots spread so quickly? One theory is that Major-General Prabowo Subianto, Suhartos son-in-law, ordered his troops to encourage the riots, which he could then blame on General Wiranto, the Indonesian Chief of Staff. An official investigation into the riots indicated that the Indonesian army had attempted something akin to a coup detat-whether it was for or against the Suharto regime was not revealed, and possibly not understood. In any case, most of the people of Jakarta had been severely discontent for the past several months, and most were quick to take any action against anyone in the hope of changing something. At times, bystanders joined marching student demonstrators, and the demonstrations quickly disintegrated into mob action, with looting, burning, and attacks on innocent people. In one instance, a witness reported people being offered money to join in the riots-since money was scarce; many of those people had very little choice in the matter.
Some important evidence that the military was involved in planning and executing the riots was that the riots were carried out against ethnic Chinese. Presumably, a group of rioters dissatisfied with the government would attack government buildings, unless they were externally influenced otherwiseplus, someone influencing them to not attack government buildings and officials most likely has a stake in the security of the government. Indeed, in the beginning of the rioting, it was carried out almost exclusively against military and police cars and offices. For example, rioters attacked a car dealership displaying Bimantara sedans, which (Bimantara) was partially owned by Suhartos son-in-law. However, the ethnic Chinese were quickly made scapegoats of and the riots turned against them, partially because they, too, like Suhartos family, were well-off.
The police, intentionally or not, exacerbated the riots; in one instance, the police ...clubbed students and journalists as they fled the scene, clogging rush-hour traffic. Bystanders joined the student protesters who hurled rocks at the police. It is likely that high-ranking officials discontent with Suharto ordered this to instigate a mass riot, or even a revolt. Two main officials implicated for this are Major General Prabowo and Major General Sjafrie Samsuddin. Major-General Prabowo, in particular, is considered to have played a key role in causing the riots. One theory is that one of the two generals instigated the riots in an attempt to discredit the other; if this is true, it could well have been Samsuddin and Prabowo is innocent.
Despite powerful evidence for military involvement, it is known that the military was not responsible for all of the rioting; in one area of the city, rioting began when high school students, marching behind a banner reading Down With Suharto asked passers-by to join them-and abruptly, they deteriorated into a mob, throwing stones at anything breakable[8]. When police arrived on the scene, the area was littered with glass [8] and police fired shots into the air, causing the members of the mob to split up and flee into side streets. In other areas, police attempts to breakup rioting mobs were not as successful, and in at least one instance, students and bystanders threw rocks at riot police. In some documented cases, disgruntled riot police joined the rioters.
The government-led investigations into the riots as well as Indonesian statements about the riots in the first few days after the riots were patently false. Initially, Indonesian government officials repeatedly denied that any rapes occurred during the riots; according to the Human Rights Watch, On June 29th, armed forces commander General Wiranto gave a press conference and stated that the military had gone to twenty hospitals that had treated victims of the May riots. Of 630 victims, twelve had died, eighty-seven had been kept in for treatment, and the rest were able to go home, but there was not a single case of rape. However, on July 3, the Indonesian minister for womens affairs, Tutty Alawiyah, acknowledged that rapes had indeed taken place, contradicting earlier statements, and implying that the government almost certainly knew about the rapes before then, and definitely knew about the issue by June 29th. On July 7, a spokesman for the Indonesian National Commission on Human Rights stated that We can say without any doubt whatsoever that on those dates [May 13-15] mass rape of ethnic Chinese women occurred that was widespread, systematic, and sadistic. (Human Rights Watch).
Despite admission by several branches of government that rapes had occurred during the riots, the government continued to deny that any raping of ethnic Chinese women had occurred even through August 1998. The Human Rights Watch reports that By August 17, the national police commander, General Roesmanhadi, was threatening to prosecute the organizations that had been most vocal in publicizing the rape issue on charges of spreading false rumors. Government claims that there were no rapes were strengthened by revelations that some of the photographs of rape that were posted on the Internet had actually been copied photographs from earlier violence in East Timor-others had even been stolen from pornographic websites. It is possible that such photographs were fabricated by people who wished to conceal the rapes or hide them; nonetheless, the body of evidence that implies a campaign of mass rape is far larger than simply those photographs. However, it was later generally agreed that mass rape actually did occur.
After the riots, at first, many ethnic Chinese rape victims were reluctant to come forward, and one of the main places that many came forward was in churches. Many ethnic Chinese are Christian, and they overcame their shame at church. One young girl told of how a gang of rioters invaded the apartment building where she lived, which was populated with mostly ethnic Chinese. Despite trying to evade the rioters, and offering money for the rioters to leave her and her family alone, she, her sister, and her aunt were gang raped by the rioters; her sister and her uncle were stabbed to death. A hospital worker said that she knew of at least 400 cases of rape-indicating that far more ethnic Chinese were raped than those that came forward.
The government cover-up for the rapes is one of the single most powerful pieces of evidence of government and military involvement in the riots-after all, theres no reason to hide something that somebody else did. However, government officials repeatedly denied that any rapes had occurred until July 3, when the Indonesian government essentially did an about-face-the Indonesian minister for Womens Affairs acknowledged that rapes had taken place, and said that making the victims feel more secure was more important than raising evidence-which, despite being true, is still a weighted statement in that she tries to avoid making a case for an international investigation of the riots. Even after this, the government still tried to paint the picture that no rapes had occurred. On August 17, General Roesmanhadi, the Indonesian national police commander, threatened to prosecute the organizations which had been the most instrumental in publicizing the rape issue on charges of spreading false rumors. Other Indonesian government associations quickly followed suit, and before long the official government position was that no rapes occurred in the riots.
While many well-documented and supported cases indicate that police and military actions may have angered some of the rioters, and possibly did so intentionally, evidence that the government and military actively supported the riots is anecdotal at best, but reports are common enough that it is doubtful they are all false. One Tim Richardson reports that his fiances father was traveling in a small bus when traffic ground to a halt due to the outbreak of the riots. He and his fellow passengers decided to complete their trip on foot. An elderly man, he rested frequently during his two kilometres walk. In that time, he saw empty trucks travelling along the road, recruiting young men with the promise of 20,000 Rupiah to join the riots (the minimum monthly wage is about 150,000 Rupiah). While not confirmed, stories like this are not likely to be fabricated-after all, there is little gain for the storyteller by telling the story, and thus are good evidence that such events actually occurred. Plus, since most such retellings share similar elements, and generally agree with each other, it is likely that the events described happened-it is doubtful, for example, that several people will all fabricate what is essentially the same event.
Although the riots occurred nine years ago, most of the leaders and rioters are still unknown; the only accused riot leader was President Suhartos son-in-law, Major-General Prabowo Subianto. This strongly implies a planned riot; impromptu riot leaders would not have had loyal subsidiaries and would have been easily captured after the riots had ended. Also, it implies that the government, even that of President Habibe, was somewhat sympathetic to the rioters-a more focused investigation almost assuredly would have turned up more information than the Indonesian government released.
Investigation into the riots was mostly led by the newly-formed Indonesian National Human Rights Commission, though the Indonesian government has been slow to react to its findings. The NHRC operates more or less independently; despite having no true power, it exercises power in the form of press releases that may put pressure on the government to respond. When the NHRC investigated the May riots, the finding that many ethnic Chinese were raped and the calls for a national investigative commission were at first accepted by President Habibe, and in July, the government set up an investigative commission, presumably a nod to the NHRC. However, in August, many public officials had begun questioning whether any rapes actually occurred, in blind ignorance of the NHRCs findings. The NHRC was the only organization permitted to investigate the Jakarta riots; according to the US Department of State, The [Indonesian] Government generally considers outside investigations or foreign-based criticism of alleged human rights violations to be interference in its internal affairs. It emphasizes its belief that the linkage of foreign assistance, or other sanctions, to its human rights observance constitutes interference in its internal affairs and is therefore unacceptable.
All investigations, news reports, and anecdotes considered, the evidence strongly points to a riot made possible by a severe economic downturn, aided and abetted by the military of Indonesia. The riots, sparked by the shooting of student protesters at Triskati University in Jakarta, quickly consumed much of the unfortunate city, and became an outlet for pent-up resentment towards the Indonesian government and ethnic Chinese living in Jakarta. The riots, while planned by the military, were soon out of the militarys control, and mobs of angry rioters began roaming the streets, breaking into shops, looting things which they did not need, and breaking and entering the homes of ethnic Chinese, destroying much of what was in the homes, and raping the female occupants. Ethnic Chinese were a target for the riots because similar to the Jews in Weimar Germany, they were a prosperous minority in an economic depression. They lived generally together, and were seen as being more loyal to their native China than to Indonesia.
The rioters rioted because their lives at the time were miserable. A severe economic recession and the recent reappointment of who was essentially a dictator, then-President Suharto, had filled the Jakartan barrel with plenty of gunpowder. They rioted when they did and where they did because of the men holding the strings, the men with the guns. Disagreements in the Indonesian military had made it desirable for any one of the high ranking generals to try and oust the others, so as to get more of their allies in the military into high-ranking positions. In the end, the riots succeeded in doing what both the rioters, and the puppet-masters, had intended: disposal of the aging dictator Suharto, but at a cost far higher than anyone had imagined.
