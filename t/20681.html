There was a whole lot of unnecessary argument in that thread relating to the nature of God. The original case that needed to be proven was "Is free will compatible with perfect foreknowledge?". This doesn't require any statement about God.

Event A is a single event.

For free will to exist, a person must be able to subjectively determine the condition of event A. This determination must be dynamic and not limited to a single choice.

For perfect foreknowledge of event A to exist, there must be a single, unchangeable state for event A; such that the single event A can be known certainly, and will not deviate from that known.

Free will would allow a range of options which would prevent any certainty or perfection in foreknowledge. That's why they're incompatible.

You don't understand my point. I'm not saying that God or belief in God is bad because He/it is illogical. I'm saying that if God is illogical, nothing meaningful can be said about His nature. For example, "God is X" and "God is not X" can both be true simultaneously, even though this is a logical impossibility. Try substituting "omnibenevolent" for "X" - we can make completely contradictory statements about God and believe them both. If you accept that God is above logic, you must accept that "God is omnibenevolent" and "God is not omnibenevolent" are equally valid, because without logic you have no basis to distinguish between them.

So, no, I'm not saying there's something wrong with God being illogical. I'm saying that this results in certain consequences, namely that anything said about God, by definition, has no logical basis, and therefore arbitrary. If you are not willing to accept these consequences, then you may find the idea of an illogical God to be wrong.
