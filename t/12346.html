It's fusion, not "fussion". And no, fusion is a totally different process to hydrogen burning. In the latter, you simply split water into it's component atoms, using some energy to do so, then recombine the hydrogen with some oxygen, getting less energy back because the process is not 100% efficient. The advantage is, of course, that you can actually burn hydrogen, unlike water. In our current plans for a fusion reactor, deuterium and/or tritium* would undergo nuclear fusion to become helium, yielding extra energy that was previously stored within the atoms themselves, rather than in the bonds between atoms.

Hydrogen with one or two extra neutrons, they are present in lesser amounts than normal hydrogen. Deuterium is also called "heavy hydrogen" Both occur naturally, though trituim is somewhat scarce due to a relatively short decay time. They can be produced from "heavy water", which is water with deuterium instead of normal hydrogen.

#223297 21 2006-09-03T17:52:44Z stepself
Electrolysis isn't the only way to produce hydrogen of course. In fact, hydrogen via electrolysis is trivial, and not particularily useful on a large scale. Steam reforming is more common in industry, and a major source of hydrogen gas worldwide. The reaction is:

natural gas + water --> carbon monoxide + 3 * hydrogen

There are logistic problems associated with this reaction. It is catalytic, usually requiring very expensive metals like platinum or palladium. The reaction must occur at high pressure and temperature. And carbon monoxide is a cat. poison, so it must be removed as well or efficiency will drop very rapidly.
