Well, one of my most important (not the most, as I can't think of what my most pressing concern is right now) suggestions is that letter grades should be removeda 69.5% is not the same as a 79.4%. One of my friends (we're talking high school here) always tries to do his best throughout the entire year solely so he can sleep and not care during the finals in the last weeks of school. This is pretty idiotic in my opinionin universities, the last test tends to count for over 50% of the end grade, so my friend and others like him aren't getting final-exam practice, and letter grades also make people lower their goals. For example, in my AP Physics C class, I had a 71%, a strong C (more lenient curve; the lowest possible B- in specifically that class alone is a 74.5%), and my final exam was 200/200 possible pts.; even if I got everything perfect I could not possibly have picked up to a 74.5%, so I simply aimed to fail the test, quite literally (get around 50-60, which is what I successfully did), only to prevent myself from falling into a D. If letter grades were removed, I would instead strive to do my best on everything no matter what (although I honestly don't think I could've gotten much farther than what I -did- receive on that test... flippin' hard, man, that!).

So, As, Bs, Cs, Ds, Fs... all should be removed, and everything should be %-based. The system is dumb as it is with letter-grades; those who get a 79.5% will be placed with people whose average is ~85%, and thus be even more unprepared for the ensuing class for being lucky, and vice-versa for those who score an 89.4% but barely fail to get an A.

What problems are there in the college/university system? I'm not really too sure of their structure since I've yet to be exposed to any as a student.

#333683 10 2007-06-20T22:50:49Z blue_tetris
Less standardization in lesson plans; more standardization in testing. Learning takes place in the classroom and no governmental guidelines can assist a specific teacher working in a specific student base to do any better at their jobs, because the career itself is so situation-oriented--different situations require different methodology. However, educators know that a national standard of performance must be met by their students, and must take measures so that their students meet a set of standardized levels of achievement.

So, in sum, don't restrict a teacher's way of teaching; but certainly provide an expected base of knowledge to be attained by graduation.

One of the biggest issues I had with my former high school's curriculum is the amount of required classes that students are forced to take. I understand the necissity of requring a certain amount of credits to graduate, and requiring students to have so many of said credits in the four core subjects, math, literature, science, and social studies. This helps to create a well-rounded student who has the oppurtunity to be well-versed in the liberal arts.

The problem isn't forcing people to take classes in those core areas, it's forcing certain classes on students. Let me explain. As a freshman, everyone at my high school had to take economics and civics, as well as biology, intro to american literature and gym. Where is there room for a student to explore the possiblities of academia? Suppose a student as no desire to learn about economics or biology, instead, the want to learn about sociology and physics. Well, they can't because they have to take classes which may or not benifit them in the real world. I'm sure every class that a student takes has the potential of benifiting him or her, but if a student does not apply him or herself into that specific field as a career, then many classes are pointless. For example, if a student wants to be a journalist or an accountant later in life, biology serves very little purpose.

Out of the 23.5 credits I needed to graduate, exactly 8 were elective courses. Take away 4 of those credits if you assume that a student has to have 2 years of a foreign language, something that many competitve university's are requiring these days, and now there is only 4 credits of electives. Where is there room for the student to explore and discover interesting classes that may lead to potential careers?

Argh!

While the language may or may not be useful, the exposure to world cultures and learning skills developed through the acquistion of a foreign tongue have been shown to help students become better, well, students.
