#230847 15 2006-09-19T21:42:22Z Tsukatu
Boom!, as a Linux user, I still despise Macs.

Mac OS X uses a UNIX framework, right? So does Linux. That makes the framework identical. The only difference then is between what Macs offer vs what Linux offers, and at what price and convenience.
And that's where it turns into an argument for GPL/OSS software, and that's where the basis of my hatred of Macs lies.

In fact, have a look at an earlier post of mine:
Quote: Tsukatu, 2005-12-30T23:26:59Z
We can look at different operating systems as cars.
A Windows car is a pile of many, many pieces that all build and work off of each other. The result is very shaky, but it somehow manages to work. However, when there is a problem, it's very easy to locate the one problem part and replace it with a very small (if non-existent) fee.
A Mac car is a hermitically-sealed bubble. It's difficult to go on more "freestyle" roads with it, because it was built for efficient work. If something does go wrong with the car, because it's one big piece, the whole sonofabitch has to be replaced.
A Linux car is a tank. I don't mean one of those crappy soviet tanks, I mean something along the lines of a perfectly built (Swiss or German?) hovertank. It's extremely difficult to get it to break down. But what happens when it does break down? Eat three more metaphors:
Getting help with Windows isn't usually that big of a problem. The official Microsoft auto parts dealer is very nice about providing any replacement parts you need, usually at no charge. Sometimes you need to talk to a specialist, who are usually well-trained.
Getting help with a Mac isn't really getting help fixing as much as it is buying a new one.
Now, imagine a world where everybody loves tanks. The guy who invented a tank shared his design openly with the world, and since then an enormous community of tank-lovers and experts emerged, many of them even building their own tanks by fundamentally changing the design of the tank. There are millions of people in the world who know pretty much everything there is to know about a tank. Getting help with Linux is simply going online and finding one of the countless tech help forums that have a "Linux" subforum and asking. Usually anyone there will be able to tell you how to fix it and how to avoid having it happen again, and it's absolutely no cost to you!
We've compared design and repair. Now we'll cover economics.
100 people walk into a massive lot that contains Windows, Mac, and Linux vehicles. 90 of these people are attracted to the shininess and sleek facade of Windows, so they immediately get the impression that Windows is the best designed system. They pay the hefty price of a Windows vehicle, and buy many more Windows applications. 5 of these people are more attracted to Macs, for whatever retarded reason, and they decide to buy a Mac. But only 5 of the initial 100 people decide to walk over to the lot with the massive sign that says "FREE TANKS! GET YOUR TANK HERE! ANY DESIGN YOU WANT, WITH 100% CUSTOMIZABILITY! FREE TECH SUPPORT UNTIL THE END OF TIME! EFFECTIVELY AN UNLIMITED TIME WARRANTY!"

Fuck Macs.
Go Linux.

Mac OS X is an entirely proprietary operating system that is extremely prone to such nastiness as vendor lock-in, which made Microsoft as hated as it is today. You have to pay hundreds of dollars for the Mac, pay hundreds more for the proprietary programs you use, and pay hundreds more for technical support and service.
Linux is completely free, and these days many distributions (such as the legendary Dapper Drake version of Ubuntu) are more straightforward and powerful than Macs. So you're basically getting slightly increased performance out of the machine for free. The most popular graphic design programs, 3D modeling programs, office suite programs, all that useful but expensive stuff, have Open-Source alternatives that are typically just as good or better than the programs you have to pay money for. In addition, those programs are being constantly developed by many more people than Apple can devote to the task. There are greater numbers of more motivated and more skilled programmers working on some obscure Python library than Apple can place on its newest and hottest piece of software or OS X version. And to top it off, help with using Open Source software is seventy fold more accessible, and free as well. Beyond that, if you know what you're doing, you can even customize Open Source programs to your liking and recompile them - I'd love to see a proprietary piece of software try to accomodate that same freedom.
And don't go with that old "but the software on the Mac will be faster" nonsense. They're both built on UNIX, aye? They'll run the same, if not faster on a Linux box.
Mac and Linux also have that "viruses aren't applicable to me" hotness. The issues of concern with Macs and Linux machines are purely security-related, and even then they both do that leagues better than Windows.

What we're reduced to is this: you're given a choice of a sturdy Volvo that you will end up spending several hundred dollars per year on, or you can get a hovertank for free with a comprehensive lifetime warranty and shockingly easy customizability.
Why would any sane person go for the Volvo?

And hey, guess what!? You can do Windows shit on a Linux box as well! See, there's this wonderful pair of programs called WINE and Cedega. They can emulate most Windows applications and games without a hitch, and at no speed decrease. Many Linux gamers (such a thing exists - I'd love to see a solid Mac gamer) even say that playing games like Doom 3 and Halo with Cedega is just like running it natively in Windows.

In terms of my advocacy of operating systems, I'd recommend a Linux machine first because you'd be able to do most everything a person would expect a computer to do for free, then Windows for the serious gamers that play games that Cedega is still working to translate properly. I would never recommend a Mac. They are shitty versions of Linux that are more costly. I don't know why someone would get a Mac. It's beyond me.

Quote: elaxion, September 21, 2006 05:54 pm
For usability reasons, isn't linux difficult? Like running different programs and the like.

Unless you're a very special case, it's just as easy as running a native Windows app in Windows. To run OpenOffice Writer (like Microsoft Word, but free) you go to Start -> OpenOffice -> OpenOffice Writer. To run OpenOffice Writer in Linux, you'd go to Applications (equivalent of Start bar and just as accessible) -> OpenOffice -> OpenOffice Writer.
Even using WINE to run a native Windows application in Linux is very straight-forward and simple. Sometimes there are kinks, but those are usually well explained at winehq.com.

And hey, if you're uneasy about trying Linux, you don't even have to install it to try it out. Linux is the only operating system that offers a LiveCD. You simply boot from the LiveCD instead of booting from your hard drive, and you're running Linux without touching your hard drive at all.
If you're interested in trying it out, go to http://www.ubuntu.com and either download and burn an .iso, or ask them the mail you some CD's (they pay all costs, including shipping).
