#425563 2 2008-04-06T07:44:58Z LittleViking
Video games do not develop your hand-eye coordination! Hand-eye coordination is used for things like catching a ball, punching a small target, hitting a ball with a paddle - basically seeing a target and moving your hand to that position. Hand-eye coordination does not mean seeing something on the screen and pressing a button, nor seeing something on screen and moving a mouse. The Wii nunchuck might help with developing these skills, and so might some arcade machines. But it bothers me when people use hand-eye coordination to support video games.
#425585 3 2008-04-06T09:07:12Z Atilla
Hmm? Studies have shown that playing action games does, in fact, improve your fine motor skills and some aspects of visual perception. Playing games can also help those who have lost movement or coordination as a result of injury to the CNS regain their ability. It's certainly not going to turn you into a professional sportsperson, but there is some effect.

That said, I don't think it's sensible to make a list "justifying" the use of video games. A lot of people who deride games as a waste of time are perfectly happy with TV, Uno, and other pursuits which aren't really the greatest source for essential life skills. The point is that games are enjoyable and, in moderation, they do no harm.

I read something in the newspaper a while back that said kids who play video-games are better at spatially numeric tasks, like looking at a bunch of shapes (or counter-terrorists) and telling you how many there are without counting. Not sure if there is a real world application for this, but it's interesting.

Also, to agree with LV, I don't think playing games willl have any effect on your hand/eye coordination. You are using completely different systems. Simply pressing buttons in relation to something that happens on-screen should not train yourself to do anything better in your life besides pressing buttons. Getting better at catching a ball (or even shooting a gun) happens when the muscles used to catch a ball or shoot a gun build a memory of what they had to do to succesfully complete their task. This wouldn't happen if you were catching a ball while playing Madden '08 or shooting a gun playing CS. The only thing playing these games will imporove is your game-playing abilities.
#425863 12 2008-04-07T01:47:42Z Atilla
Quote: Obby, April 07, 2008 06:07 am
Call of Duty 4 teaches me how to shoot Commies and Arabs.

There you have it, folks. Playing video games is the American Way.

@Hendor: Incorrect. I recall a study showing that surgeons who play Halo before an operation actually perform the surgery faster and with greater accuracy. Also, the military is known to use games for training purposes. Even if they don't make you an expert marksman, they can certainly teach you strategy.

I think people who claim that games don't teach you anything aside from pressing buttons have an excessively narrow view. That kind of reasoning ultimately results in the conclusion that reading teaches you nothing, except how to turn pages, and that push-ups don't make you stronger, only better at push-ups. Games stimulate the portion of your brain associated with fine motor skills and visual perception. I don't think it's entirely unreasonable to believe that these functions will improve with use, much like virtually every other brain function.

Really, I think people might be surprised about some of the relations which do exist. For example, despite the drop-out rocker stereotype, musical ability and mathematical ability are correlated. Now, pressing keys or blowing into a tube certainly doesn't teach you how to solve equations. However, harmony, rhythm, even melody all rely on mathematical patterns and relations. Thus, even though there is no direct connection between playing music and adding numbers, there is a correlation because they use some of the same parts of the brain. I think similar relations apply for video games and tasks which rely on similar skills.

#428304 20 2008-04-14T06:19:08Z TornadoTK
Not sure if this applies much to current games (unless you get into organized clans), but a valuable skill I've learned over my years of multiplayer gaming:

Teamwork.

Whether it's giving orders or taking them, so much more gets accomplished (and it gets accomplished more efficiently) when a few people in a group become a real team. You build skills based around communication, organization, decision-making, time and resource management, and most importantly, working in a structured system. Many of these skills are vital in the business world of today.

Games like Splinter Cell: Chaos Theory and the older Rainbow Six titles are excellent examples of team-based strategy, and a solid team makes you an unstoppable force. Many games today have you working as a team (Halo 3, CoD4, Team Fortress 2), but the feeling is a little less enjoyable unless you have friends there with you.
