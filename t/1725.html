yup - suki is right (again). Centrepetal force is basically the name given to any force, (be it gravity, tension in a string, or friction between the turning car's wheels and the road) that acts TOWARDS the point around which a body turns - it is this force which causes the body to turn. So technically, i guess, centrepetal force doesn't exist either - it is merely the name given to whatever force is involved in circular motion.

Centrefugal force is non-existant - the reason you feel a 'pull' outward when you are in a car going round a bend is that your mass 'wants' *wince* to go in a straight line, in accordance with the wonderful laws of Newton & co. It was orginally invented by the slow-of-thought who used it to explain why objects move in a straight line - they imagened the centrepetal force being there constantly, and centrefugal force counteracting it to make a straight line. Actually it is the opposite - there are no forces involved in a straight line - that's the point.

The site is stupid. It goes directly against excerpts from Newton's actual books, which I'm too tired to dig up right now.
It basically makes the mistake of calling "normal force" the "centrifugal force." The normal force is the force you get when you have an object being pushed into a surface, and the normal force is the surface pushing back. Where you're sitting right now, you have a gravitational force on your butt, and the chair gives back a normal force because of Newton's action-reactiong pair business.
So the normal force acts exactly as a centrifugal force would act if it existed. The main problem with calling it centrifugal is that it confuses people who think that the main force involved with simple harmonic motion (like the circular path) is an outward-pushing force, which is untrue. Calling it that would also be adding another force to the equation, since you will always have a normal force pushing back. If we're told there's a centrifugal force, then we think to ourselves, "here is the centripetal force, and the normal force counteracting it, and then the centrifugal force as well counteracting the centripetal force... so essentially we have any rotation immediately leaving its circular path because it has more of a pull to the center of the circle."

Let's say you have a bucket of water and you're swinging it around your head with your arm. The water is going to be pulled inward by a centripetal force, and so is the bucket. The problem is that the bucket is pushing against the water because it's also affected by the centripetal force, so the water pushes back and ends up going in a line tangent to the point on the circle it currently occupies. So what about the bucket then? Why doesn't it rocket toward the center of the circle, seeing as how we've only shown it has a centripetal force, and no surface to provide a normal force in opposition in the same way the bucket acted on the water? Well, your arm acts as a rope and provides a tension force that counteracts just that. So in the end, we have all of the forces cancel out, and the only thing that happens is that it gets a velocity on a path tangent to the point on the circle it occupies.

It's difficult to visualize, but that's one of the best known examples.
