#429716 3 2008-04-19T01:51:37Z Strife
What a coincidence - we just had two reps from the RIAA at my university for a question and answer session.

On the subject of piracy in general, not specifically PC games, I think trying to curb piracy is a respectable and necessary task, however messy. Despite the arguments about the questionable legality of making your own copy of a piece of software or media, the fact remains that piracy has a very significant detrimental effect on the market for these things. People are losing their jobs smply because there is that much less money coming in.

But I'm a little different in terms of music - I'll happily pay an artist for an original reproduction of their work, but I feel less satisfied about doing the same for a copy. I pay for food because each piece of food is an original craft. Otherwise, if I go out and purchase materials to construct for myself a copy of something I see, then I've already paid in materials and time for that product, and the fact that I can make it myself means I don't need you to sell it to me. But I can't copy experience - if you want to put on a concert, I'll pay for that, but I'm not paying you $10 to click "burn another copy" and pop another CD in the drive, because I can buy the raw materials and the tools and do it myself. Thanks for the creativity and all, but I can take it from here. If you want me to pay you at some point, though, then you have to be able to do a better job than I can, and enough to convince me that I need your version.

I can't think of a suitable segue...
Here are some of the questions asked to those RIAA reps:

A company in your employ, MediaSentry, was issued a cease and desist order on January 2nd by the MA state police, yet there is evidence that they have continued surveillance, directly in contradiction to the terms on the order. How can the RIAA lecture students on legal and moral issues when they violate personal rights?

It has come up that your partners do not wish to disclose the methods they use to find file sharers online. Do you believe it is right and fair for someone to be forced to accept that your methods are correct on faith, especially when they have been proven faulty before?

How can you continue to pursue file sharers when there has already been one person fully exonerated from your charges (Atlantic v. Andersen)?

After having your expert witness embarassed on the stand in UMG v. Lindor, and having other so-called "expert" witnesses disbarred in other courts (specifically in the Netherlands in 2005), how can you continue with these cases?
(Examples:
* expert witness kept no detailed record of his search
* expert witness made serious errors concerning how certain parts of a network function
* expert witness failed to document his forensics, which he stated is not necessary and any other professional would consider okay
* expert witness claimed to be an expert on MediaSentry but was unable to discuss its methods or potential flaws
* expert witness claimed that the screenshot evidence proved his case, admitted they could be easily manipulated, and proceeded with testimony anyway
* expert witness admits that forensic inspection of the defendant's hard drive revealed nothing suggestive of illicit filesharing)

On April 2nd, the University of Maine filed a Rule 11 Motion in the case Arista v. Does. part of their motion states that the RIAA's pursuit of student filesharers violates FERPA. How do you justify circumventing FERPA (privacy) regulations? Do you believe it would be fair for Student Health Services to turn over my records, in violation of HIPPA?

Another portion of the Rule 11 Motion in Arista v. Does alleges that the RIAA v. Does is designed to intimidate people into settling. why are you still railroading people into lucrative pre-litigation settlements when you state your initial intention is to sue and not settle?


There were a handful of other questions asked, and the RIAA reps didn't walk away very happy.

But still, kids... stealing is wrong.
