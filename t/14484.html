#270402 4 2006-12-15T03:18:30Z Tsukatu
How could it not be relative?

It is a custom in many indigenous tribes to honor their dead ancestors by eating them, in order to help their ancestors spirit live on inside them, and to gain their wisdom and guidance. We think that's horrifying and wrong, and they think it's one of the nicest things they can do for their ancestors, and not only think that it's right, but that there's also absolutely nothing wrong about it.

I like what Sigmund Freud says in Civilization and Its Dicontents.
I'll try to summarize it as best as I can:
The ego is our animalistic nature. If we were to act according to our ego, we'd be insensitive assholes, seeking only to satisfy our own natural needs and desires regardless of what that does to anyone else. We'd be stealing, killing, and raping. But what makes humans so different is that they have this superego, which serves basically as a conscience. It knows what is right and what is wrong, and then denies the ego.
How does the superego gain leverage over the ego? By taunting it with a human being's most desperate need: love. Every time the ego does something that would make it less loved (by an individual, by a community; anything that would make it disliked or lose approval), the superego is right there letting the ego know that, and the ego realizes that it wants acceptance more than it wants that candy bar. After all, it's much easier to associate with someone who doesn't take all of your stuff, right? Better to have a loyal friend than all of his stuff. And the fun part is, the superego can chastise the ego before it even acts - if the ego has the intention of doing something wrong, it is criticized just as severely as though it had actually performed the action.
So right is equated with what gets the person acceptance, and wrong is what makes people dislike him. But if the person has no sense of right and wrong to begin with, how should he be expected to learn it? The answer is the child's parents (or whoever raises the child). When the child is being raised, it needs constant love from its parents. Parents punish the child for doing something wrong by their standards, which conditions the child's superego to understand what would get him love from his parents, and what would lose it. So the parents' sense of right and wrong is more or less imprinted onto the child.
It does vary a little bit, because the child does go through that rebellious phase after the onslaught of hormones begins to mess with their thinking, and there are also minor adjustments made by peer interaction, but ultimately the right and wrong framework that the parents set will be passed to the child (especially because all of those peers the child is hanging out with likely have similar senses of right and wrong as they get from their parents anyway).
In this way, sense of right and wrong follows cultural development. Many cultures clash with regard to what they believe is right and wrong. That doesn't make particular cultures inherently evil.

One of the examples I like the most is geeky: a paladin fights for all that he believes is right; two paladins from two distinct cultures that have never met each other think that the other culture's practices are evil. The paladins Smite Evil each other - does it function?

#270480 8 2006-12-15T06:38:30Z Daddaluma
Here's my take.

All systems of morality must eventually boil down to some fundamental claim which is ultimately unprovable. So no system of morality can claim absolute truth.

However, this does not mean that there isn't one particular moral system which most or all could agree on which could be said to apply to all situations at all times. It just wouldn't be making "factual" claims.

If a certain fundamental axiom, or set of axioms could be agreed upon by everyone, then in effect, we have found our moral absolutes. Morality doesn't have to be a rigid set of one line, "thou shall not . . ." claims either. We can still claim moral absolutism, even with a system that allows for the same action in different situations to have different moral values.

Let me try to clarify just a bit.

If, for example, you made the claim that the bible is true, and therefore Morality is determined by god, you have made an unprovable claim. And since your entire morality system rests on an unprovable claim, it cannot be said to be fact.

This is true for every morality system you can think of though. For instance, if you said it's immoral to kill another person for no reason at all (something, I believe pretty much everyone would agree with), and I asked "why?" what would you say?

If you were a libertarian, you'd say that that person has a right not to have his body aggressed against and by killing him, you are violating his rights, which is wrong. But this claim, too, is an unprovable axiom. There's no inherent value in human life, and there's no rights which are intrinsic to the universe.

I suspect that was the part of what I said that wasn't all that confusing, so let me try to explain what the rest of what I said meant.

So, since all morality is based on a certain degree of subjectivity, it's value has to lie in how well it predicts our feelings toward events and actions. If everyone in the world agrees that it's immoral to torture a small child for fun, then any morality system which leads to any different conclusion has no value.

Now, we all know there are things which some people claim are immoral and others don't. Based solely on this fact, you might think that it's impossible for morality to be anything but relative. Take abortion for instance. McP thinks abortion is immoral, whereas, I do not. However, if we examine the underlying factors a bit, we might find that we are both working off the same fundamental moral claim. For instance, we both think it's wrong to murder innocent people, however, I don't think the fetus is a person. So it's not that we don't share the same morals, it's that we disagree on the facts of the situation.

So, what I'm saying now, and what I was trying to say before, is that even though on the surface we all make different moral evaluations and different situations result in different applications of our moral compass, we all might share the same fundamental axiom from which we derive our morality. And if this were the case, then we would have moral absolutism.
