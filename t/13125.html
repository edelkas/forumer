I'm not convinced that this question has a right answer.
Anyone can feel free to answer, but I'd especially like to hear the opinions of Kablizzy, Dave, KinGAleX, iangb, and Atilla.

A man walks into a restaurant. He has an odor about him that is offensive (no question about it - everyone will find the odor upsetting). This odor is caused by his own negligence, and could be easily fixed. In other words, do you fight more for the man's right to offend people's olfactory senses as he pleases, or do you fight for the people's right to live in an environment free of such terrible smells?

#239878 5 2006-10-12T01:56:56Z blue_tetris
Ahh. The old debate between positive liberty and negative liberty. I'll forgo the metaphor and say that I most prefer a balance between freedom of action and freedom from coercion. However, I think the reservation of freedom from coercion (negative liberty) is a slightly better cause than the reservation of freedom of action. In your example, you portray negative liberty in a poor light. This guy who could otherwise change his ways refuses to for the sake of pissing people off.

If the man were homosexual or practicing atheism, instead of "smelling foul", we'd probably view the argument in a different light. Should the town reserve the right to discriminate against people who have made different decisions than them? The decision to smell foul is almost universally depicted as a bad decision; in some areas of the world, the decision to be an atheist or be homosexual is also deemed to be equally offensive to others. Should institutions have the right to tell people that their decisions are "offensive" and disallow them access to certain areas? I don't think so.

Again, this is my opinion. My general liberalism guides me to believe that people need to have freedom from others before exercising freedom over others.
