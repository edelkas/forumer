LESSON ONE: CORRECT FORM OF ITS.
Righto. And here we go, emarking upon the seas of grammatical adventure.
This one irritates me a lot, because nowadays I tend to read the form of 'its' that's grammatically correct rather than the one that logic dictates should be placed there. It's confusing and irritating.

And so, here's the simple way it works.

it's : It Is. That's what it means. Simple. If you want to say "It is cold", you can use "It's". It's really quite simple.
its : Belonging to. A nice, clear meaning. For what one might say if one wanted to bring about the vibe of "The feet belonging to it(/he /she) were cold." Instead, you would write "It's feet were cold." The feet belonging to it were cold. This one is slightly odd, since it goes against the vibe from some rules involving apostrophes.

But, anyway, it's (it is) very easy to remember.
It's a cold day.
The planet was the earth, and its dying years were very cold indeed.

LESSON TWO: APOSTROPHES
Apostrophes. For plurals, ONE DOES NOT USE THEM. For saying something belongs to someone/ something, YOU USE THEM. For ommiting a section of a word, YOU DO NOT USE THEM. For a plural of a word ending in a vowel, YOU DO NOT USE THEM. Though you CAN use a tilde (~) if you're a part of that new revolutionary campaign.

But nevermind that last bit.

Bananas: More than one banana. There are several bananas.
Banana's: The banana's... something. Such as, "The banana's skin had been turned blue by some random kids playing paintball."

And then we have things like,
There's/ Don't/ It's: "There is", "Do not", "It is". The apostrophe is shoved in there to show that there are sections of the word missing, or that two larger words have been condensed. It's simple. There's not a lot to it. Please don't omit these apostrophes.

Also, contrary to popular belief, it is "Two Week's Notice"- the notice 'belongs to' the two weeks.
Stupid, stupid movie title.

The tilde: Some groups want to use this to seperate the 's' from the endings of words ending in a vowel. Like, "folio~s" or "banana~s". These groups amuse me almost as much as the groups that want to replace "hello" with "heaveno". But not quite as much.

LESSON THREE: WHERE AND OTHER THINGS LIKE IT
Let's talk about Where.

Where is my mother? At what location is my mother?
Wear the turban, it looks good on you. Put on the turban, it looks good on you.
We were young, happy, and eating ghoulash. We, at a point in the past, have been young, happy, and eating ghoulash.

These ARE NOT INTERCHANGABLE. It is rather irritating constantly reading things that seem to be saying, for instance,
"We (put on) young, happy, and eating ghoulash."
"(it was/ we have been) is my mother?"
"(at what location) the turban, it looks good on you."

Whenever I see things like this screwed up, I want to wrench.

Yes, I did mean wrench. Wrenching is very theraputic. You should try it sometime.

LESSON FOUR: COMMAS AND PERIODS (FULL STOPS): USE THEM
Commas are very good, as they seperate things. They do this much more effectively and easily than other punctuation marks; the semicolon has nothing on the comma.
It is easier to read things when progression of thoughts are seperated, as you can keep track of both your own mind and the mind of the writer more easily. Often, writers on the internet don't put them in, because they're idiots.

It is difficult, reading posts without commas. And wihout full stops. Meanings can get seriously skewed.

A few corrections... and a little background on linguisitcs:

Its always means "belonging to it". The word cannot mean belong to he or she. We already have the word his to mean "belonging to he" and her to mean "belonging to she". The reason its doesn't use an apostrophe is for the same reason that we don't say you's to mean "belong to you[/i].

We have what are called possessive pronouns, like your and my to prevent us from saying things like "Is this you's sofa I'm sitting on" and "That's me's wallet! Give it back!".

These pronouns don't follow normal apostrophe rules because they were never meant to use an apostrophe. They are their own grammatical entities and, as such, get their own set of rules--they don't break any other rules of grammar.

Also, your example "It's feet were cold" should read "Its feet were cold". This would only be useful if refering to an animal to which you could otherwise apply the pronoun it.

Apostrophes. For plurals, ONE DOES NOT USE THEM. For saying something belongs to someone/ something, YOU USE THEM. For ommiting a section of a word, YOU DO NOT USE THEM. The tilde is used to make the letter n sound like "ny-" in Spanish words. Like in jalapeno. The n (when it was originally a loanword) had a tilde above it--that's why it sounds the way it does. It still carries that accent mark, for people who want to pretend that they're pompous polyglots. And, if you're one of those folks, you can put funny accents over all sorts of loanwords--like navet and dj vu.

As for those of us who understand the concept of Anglicized words (and how not to be a dickface), we typically write things the way our keyboards were formatted to write them. And that means no tildes (or those unfortunately-named circumflex accents and grate accents)...
