Greetings, all! I've been talking a lot about writing this article, and I've finally got around to finishing it. Now, a few things before I begin - First and foremost, <b>this is not a mapmaking guide</b>. For those of you expecting it to be, look elsewhere. This is meant to be for folk who already know how to map, have already found their own style, and already understand how to utilize NED to their advantage. With that said, this can be used as a tool to further refine and expand your mapmaking techniques, and to better understand some very subtle things about game theory that most new (and some advanced) mapmakers tend to either overlook or ignore. Secondly, this article is meant to help mapmakers with making an episodic map pack. This isn't meant for NUMA maps, and this isn't meant (as much) for non-episodic map packs. Now, I think I should define what I mean by NUMA maps, episodic map-packs, and non-episodic map-packs.

<ul><li>NUMA Maps-</li> NUMA maps are - at their basest, most fundamental level - Casual maps. There is no set guide to making good casual maps - They can be funky, they can be innovative, they can be strange, they can be nigh-impossible. They can be completely unplayable, they can be art, they can be DDAs, they can be anything. There are few rules for making a good NUMA map, and none whatsoever for making a NUMA map at all, because it's more an art form than a science. NUMA maps, are - definitionally - freeform. They lack constraint, but they flow freely with innovation and thought. And not all of them, mind. Some are just a smattering of objects and tiles that one could hardly call a map, but that is the beauty of the NUMA map - it can be anything. Even the worst map that someone submits can be salvaged and tweaked and turned into something brilliant. NUMA Maps can be of any length, any difficulty, any style, and any form. They can even lack a style entirely. The doors are wide open with a NUMA map, as they are meant to be played on their own.

<li>Non-Episodic Map Packs-</li> I choose to list these prior to Episodic Map-Packs for an important reason. Non-Episodic Map-Packs have less constraints upon them than Episodic Map-Packs do. Non-Episodic Map-Packs (Hereafter referred to as NEMPs) are a gateway, of sorts, between NUMA maps and Episodic Map-Packs. NEMPs have the ability to be a collection of maps, perhaps with a theme, perhaps not. NEMP maps can include any number of styles easily - they can integrate well or poorly, they can be played in any order, they can function as a whole, or as a fun collection of maps from the same author. These maps can differ greatly in style, or they can all be tied together with a common tileset, enemy, or gold placement. It can have any number of maps, and the maps within can be of any length and difficulty. How this differs from Episodic Map Packs? That information follows. Now, some NEMP maps have been submitted to NUMA. Take <a href="http://numa.notdot.net/user/tktktk">tktktk</a>'s Simple Challenge Series. Functionally, the Simple Challenge Series fits the Non-Episodic theme perfectly, but it was submitted to NUMA. But the idea is the same - they are meant to be a pack, a series, and despite the submission method, the goal stays. Despite this, NEMPs can be as simple as a collection of NUMA maps, or as complex as a pack with a theme. But the distinguishing factor about an NEMP is the distinct lack of a guideline.

<li>Episodic Map-Packs-</li> Episodic Map-Packs, on the other hand, have more stringent guidelines to abide by. When you sit down to create an Episodic Map-Pack (Hereafter referred to as EMPs), you have to take a number of things into account that you don't necessarily have to take into account with NEMPs and NUMA maps. When you strive to make an EMP, your goal is to mimic the Metanet levels from the game, arrange them in an episode format, and further into a column format, and present the pack as a whole. The first thing that you have to take into account, obviously, is arrangement and length. Each map should compliment the others in an episode, and even in a column, and the maps should be neither too short nor too long. Each map should be played in order of relative difficulty, and as you play through the episode, the column, and the pack, the player should feel the theme of the pack well - That is to say that while a player is playing through your pack, the feeling that you want to convey through your maps should be felt fully. This is perhaps the most difficult part of crafting the episodic pack; It is vital to understand that the atmosphere and mood that you may want to get across to the player may not always be the perception of the pack that the player Now, purists like myself will also take into account "episode time" - Something that refers to the time it takes to complete an entire episode of five maps. Currently, the userlevels menu doesn't account for this time, but for a good balance of level difficulty and length, one must also take into consideration episode time. Episode time is a difficult thing to gauge, but each of the five levels within an episode should contain enough gold to finish the episode with an excess of time on the clock. This may come to be more important during the v1.5 release of N, but as it stands, the only reasons to take episode time into account are for principle, and to assist in measuring difficulty and length.

Whilst each map functions on its own, the purpose of the pack is to invoke both a theme and a mood, whilst also providing quality gameplay and aesthetic presentation. EMPs take far more thought and planning to execute well, as there are so many more things you have to take into account when putting together an EMP. NEMPs can take on this same level of stringency, but it is less a rule than it would be a luxury. With EMP maps, you should give thought to each object's placement within a map, each tile's effect upon the map, and fiddle with differing placements. Playtesting is also key when putting an EMP together - each map should be tested for length, difficulty, placement, and timing. Each Episodic Map's length should be shorter than the average level, so as to make the episode's length between the length of 2.5 to 5 NUMA maps. Length during the early levels should be nearer to 2.5; Whereas episode length during the later levels in a column should be nearer to 5. It's not that long maps don't have a place in map packs - They do. They really do. Long maps need to be mixed well with shorter, simpler maps, but the type of NUMA longmap that we've come to grow used to is strictly outside of a pack setting. And more complex maps absolutely ruin flavour and kill theme - The maps need to be unique and memorable, and when you come across the sixteenth map with eight switches, all hidden under bounce blocks, all guarded by 8 chaingun drones and seeker drones, smattered with mines, and on and on and on and on and on, it gets so repetitious. You have to strike a good balance between short and long maps, simplistic and complex ones, and so on. The challenges within each map and within each episode should also become progressively more difficult, but also progressively more innovative.

</ul>

This article is meant to explore, in-depth, what makes an EMP tick. As I said above, when you construct an EMP, you are mimicking what Metanet has set into motion. While there is room for innovation, there are some

Patterns - Metanet Utilizes a lot of patterns. Pattern recognition is absolutely huge in aesthetics, and is a big part of what is and is not appealing to the eye. Now, having one or two things that shoot off from this isn't a bad thing, but the only maps from this pack that I remember are the ones that included patterns.

Simplicity - Minimalism makes packs. Long maps don't belong in map packs. Packs, more often than not, are meant to be played as a whole, rather than just a conglomeration of maps. If it were just a smattering of different maps, you'd be better suited to submit them to NUMA as a series. Simple maps don't need to be short, and don't need to blow by. And good simple maps don't. Simplicity in a map doesn't dictate anything about the map's gameplay - It just takes skill as an author to utilize the simplicity in the right ways. Minimalism and unique execution are keys in what makes a good episodic map. If you make long maps, you're not necessarily a bad author, you're just a NUMA author. You don't do packs as well, because you can't grasp the idea of minimalism and simplicity. Look at every Mare and Raigan level - They do minimalism better than anyone. So, confusing difficulty and distance with aesthetic principles is where I think we're going wrong here. Simple maps have a perfect place in packs, particularly episodic packs, because the pack is meant to be played as a whole, rather than as 124 really long, grueling maps.

Physics - One of the lesser-thought themes in a Metanet map is the idea of Physics that they use. They use a ton of jumps, but not in the same way as NUMA authors do. Metanet uses downward jumps, making landings difficult to stick. They do this in a few other ways, too, but one of the most predominant things that their maps accomplish is, basically, to showcase their physics engine!

Space - I still haven't mastered space management, but Metanet does it beautifully. Their tilesets draw the eye to the action, and away from the empty space - In fact, in most of their maps, you don't notice empty space. This is perhaps the trickiest thing to get the hang of once you become an established mapper, but it's perhaps the most rewarding to get a good hold of, and most NUMA mappers (I'd say a vast majority, even) don't even have the concept at all.

The reason you don't notice it in good maps is because those maps don't blow by - Take Zag and Brambles and Shambles from the first Legacy pack. Both are very simplistic levels at heart, but neither "blow by," and both are lengthy and memorable. A strictly short map can be good, too, but short maps are more often simple than long maps, and simplistic maps can either be short or long, difficult or easy. It's the execution that dictates all that. But long maps can make a pack just as easily - so long as those maps are executed well. And from what I've seen from past packs, long maps are more often suited for NUMA, since most people map for packs like they would map normally - they don't change anything when making a map pack. And those are the types of long maps that kill a pack. So, long maps do have their place in a pack, but the type of long maps that most starting authors put in to packs are those that are suited for NUMA - I make a distinction between a NUMA map and a pack map - Because there is certainly a large difference between the two.

Oh, and you're absolutely right - That's not how you make good maps. I never said they were the best maps, but I did say that they were pack maps. And that's the point, map packs aren't a collection of the 50 best maps you can make, they're a collection of 50 maps that work well together and compliment each other. I don't care about individual maps. Individual maps are NUMA maps - Map packs rely on so much more. Making good maps is an entirely different thing than making a good map *pack*. But map-packs *are* MetaMaps. That's how you make packs. That's what mapmaking for map packs is, at its heart. You're mimicking the Metanet ideal. You certainly are able to make long, arduous maps, but those don't belong in a pack as well as more balanced maps do. You have to plan the entire experience, instead of the map-to-map traversing. They don't belong in an episodic format as well because they don't *fit* in that episodic format. Again, you can certainly make them, and you can be an unbelievable NUMA mapper, as most people here are. But those people are awful at packs for these very reasons - They make single maps, as opposed to MetaMaps. And that's the key.

So, you can be a great single mapmaker, and an awful levelpack maker all at the same time. And when you put together a pack, you're attempting to do put together a set of maps that work well together, and that's why Metanet maps are heralded by me in this fashion - I'm an episodic mapper, and an awful NUMA mapper. That's how it's always been. And I didn't figure that our until I started with Legacy. Once I started doing work with the Legacy boys, everything suddenly clicked - My maps had purpose, they had rhythm and flow and everything that they needed to have - Because they fit within a greater whole. Love in an Elevator was an Episodic map. But no one liked it - No one paid it any mind. Same with all of the other 100+ maps I've submitted to NUMA over the years - No one likes them. Why? They're certainly not terrible maps. They're just not NUMA maps. The people who map for NUMA map with one goal in mind - And that's the map itself. People who map for a pack have an entirely different goal in mind. And that's how I began my Map Theory and the "Analysis of a Metamap" project came about. My maps thrive on minimalism and simplicity, and that lends itself more readily to a column format than any single map ever could. So, in that vein, I think Mare and Raigan did a Killer job of outlining what makes a good column-map, because column maps and episodic maps are that formulaic type, it's more structured, more balanced, and it takes a whole lot of seeing the bigger picture than "Map, map, map, long map, map with mines, map with Thurgood Marshall, map, map..."

And that's why packs like these fail (I really don't mean that for this particular pack, I'm just making a point). They don't come together in the fashion that they need to in order to succeed. And that's entirely where a map-pack needs to fall - It has to feel cohesive and it has to feel different than just a smattering of NUMA maps. So, really, I don't think we have a disagreement at all - Good mapping is good mapping, but good mapping for a levelpack is a much, much different beast. And beyond that, the thing that I outlined in my original post was referring specifically to Metanet Maps, and the article that I was going to write on them, so I think that everything I said therein is quite accurate. And beyond *that*, even, Mare and Raigan's instructional that I linked to is for people to get strictly better at making Metanet Maps, those maps for SUBLiME that will be representing Metanet in these episodes - In the main games for N+.

They certainly don't have to be in the Metanet style, you're right. And if I said that above, then I misused the term 'style.' MetaMap packs are episodic packs, and the reason you put out a pack of levels is to mimic the episodic format, and not so much to mimic the Metanet Style. If that isn't your goal, then you've missed the idea of map packs altogether. Sendy's maps may have been amazing (In fact, all of her maps are amazing), but here's the thing - She didn't sacrifice an ounce of style to make her maps fit into an episodic setting. Her episodes worked unbelievably well in the episodic setting because she understood the principles behind why you put out an episodic pack (And Hell, if she didn't, she sure did a Hell of a job of bluffing it). She made her levels more minimalistic, simplistic, and worked with the themes of the game, the timer, the physics, and spacial differences. And this isn't a stylistic thing, it's something that has to change for your maps to fit into episodes. I'm not even entirely sure why, but episodes just need this cohesive feel to them in order to feel like an episodic pack.

Now, don't get me wrong, people release levelpacks every day that go against this very idea. Non-episodic packs are all over, and they're great packs, but they're not MetaMap packs. They're not meant to be Episodic packs, they're meant to be a collection of NUMA maps. And style has nothing to do with the thought that they're not episodic, it's the thought that each map was a separate entity from each other map - No thought is given to organization, no thought is given to where levels go in the pack, no thought is given to the timer, or to the aesthetics of the episode, or any of that. Again, it's just a bunch of NUMA maps. Sendy did a fantastic job of incorporating Minimalism into her pack, particularly in maps like Honeycomb Chambers and Gnarley Grotto - All of the flavour is still full there, but the maps are simplistic, and to what end? To make the pack Episodic. And because of that, they fall into the category of MetaMaps, and not due to her style, but due to the theme of the pack - The theme of episodes. If you look at Sendy's NUMA maps, and then at her columns, you'll see a drastic difference in execution, but almost zero difference in style. But even here, her style contrasts quite a bit with Metanet's style. Sendy puts bounce blocks in out-of-the-way locations, makes longer-type maps, and decorates tiles like none other. All of which go against Metanet's style, but don't necessarily go against the episodic ideal. Which is why her pack works. She didn't make single maps, she made episodic maps, and in her style. And that's the key point to what I was saying above - It's not at all about Metanet's mapping style, it's about the theme of what episodes are made for.

So, if your pack isn't a MetaMap collection, it's a NUMA-level collection. If the pack is the former, then the idea is to mimic the Episodic format, right? If the pack is the latter, then it's just a collection of NUMA-levels in a text file. Sendy went that extra step to make sure the levels were flavourful enough to play through as a whole, instead of just a few maps of increasing difficulty. Which is where the "rift" comes in. And, if you're just submitting a bunch of NUMA maps in a text file, why not just go the extra mile and submit them as a series on NUMA? Because that's all it really is. If you're releasing a pack, you have to have at least some thought to cohesion and conglomeration, or the pack ends up falling short.

In either case, as you said, I think it rather comes down to a difference in style and a difference of what we each like, rather than a problem with the mapping themes. Because they're two separate areas of thought. Episodic Maps vs. NUMA Maps is where I'm coming from, where I think you're thinking more along the lines of a difference of styles.

So, I'm good at logic. In A Practical Study of Argument, by Trudy Govier, she states:

QUOTE
A person who tries to persuade you by rational argument will claim that because the premises are true or acceptable, the conclusion should be accepted. The arguer is saying, in effect,
Premise 1
Premise 2
Premise 3
Premise N

Conclusion


She then goes on to explain how a cogent argument can be supported:

QUOTE
The basic elements of a cogent argument, referred to here as the ARG conditions, are as follows:

1. Its premises are all acceptable. That is, it is reasonable for those whom the argument is addressed to believe these premises. There is good reason to accept the premises...
2. Its premises are properly connected to the conclusion...The condition may be usefully subdivided into two parts:
a. The premises are genuinely relevant to the conclusion... that is, they give at least some evidence in favor of the conclusion's being true.
b. The premises provide sufficient or good grounds for the conclusion...


So, taking those into consideration, and I abbreviated a lot, since her book is leagues deeper than that, anyway, using those conditions, you can evaluate any argument.

So, let's take your argument in kind.

QUOTE
1.) Whatever exists, has a cause
2.) The universe had a begining
3.) Therefore, the Universe had a cause


Now, let's go first to the A condition, Acceptability of premises. In order to properly judge an argument, we have to determine whether statements one and two are acceptable, within a reasonable doubt. In order for a premise to be acceptable, it needs to meet one of the following:
1. It is supported by a subargument that is cogent.
2. It is cogently supported elsewhere, without dispute,by the arguer or another person, and the fact is noted.
3. It is known a priori to be true.
4. It is a matter of common knowledge.
5. It is supported by appropriate testimony.
6. It is supported by appropriate appeal to authority.
7. It is not known to be unacceptable, as such, and can serve provisionally as the basis for argument.
