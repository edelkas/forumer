I think that's the idea. First time I heard it, I figured they had to be using a scale on two different octaves, slowly fading from the high one to the low one over the course of the octave. For example, if the first note of their scale was a C, the next note would be 6/7th, or 86% of the D and 1/7th, or 14% of the D that is one octave lower. By the time you get to B in the scale, you are only playing 14% of the B and 86% of the one in the lower octave. It should sound perfectly natural to move from that to the original C. Because you go through the entire scale and end up where you started, you can reason that you couldn't have actually gone anywhere at all, and if you think about it, you didn't. If you think of your base C as 0 and each note representing one number, increasing and decreasing, each pair of notes "averages" back to 0 (ex. (6/7 * 1) + (1/7 *(-6)) / 2=0, or C) Of course, the combination of Ds doesn't sound like a C, music doesn't works that way.

Hope that made sense.
