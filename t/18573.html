#347761 13 2007-07-26T22:09:02Z blue_tetris
Quote: DemonzLunchBreak, July 26, 2007 01:18 pm
1. Rational happiness is the goal of every being.

2. It is morally good for beings to pursue this goal, while allowing other beings to do the same.

3. Beings have an inherent right to acquire property and to act upon their property according to their will. This is because these things are completely necessary to any pursuit of self-interest.

4. It is immoral for a being to initiate force or coercion - because these violate the property of other beings and property is an inherent right.

5. The state has a monopoly on protection, judicial services, the creation of money, etc. This monopoly is not natural - it is forced. To maintain its monopoly of protection, the state must initiate force or coercion.

5. The state is therefore immoral, and should be abolished.

These steps require a few leaps in judgment. Some of these questions seem to have "duh" answers at first, but I ask that you ruminate on them, as they are at the basis of macroeconomics.

The first can only be presumed as true. You'd be surprised how many people struggle endlessly not to be happy--however, from a societal standpoint, we may as well view the pursuit of happiness as universal.

The second serves no purpose, really. "Morally good" to pursue happiness? Who says? I'm sure anyone could agree that it is morally equitable to allow other people to pursue happiness. That's a fine statement to go forward on.

The third requires two definitions to stand logically. It's missing those definitions. The needed definitions are (a) the process of true acquisition of property and (b) what defines something as specifically your property. Is the fact that you underwent the process of acquisition enough to say that the property is yours and yours alone to act upon?

The end of the third statement is simply forcing the next leap in judgement. It describes ownership of possessions and the unhindered action upon one's own possessions as the core to pursuing one's self-interests and (presumably) happiness. Can one not be happy without property? Futhermore, can one not be happy without property which is exclusively one's own and cannot be affected by others? Presuming that one needs unlimited domain over her possessions to be truly happy is quite a leap in logic. I think the entire argument fails here. You have not proved that a person needs complete ownership and control over possessions to be happy; so I can presume that some people are just as happy with partial ownership over their possessions, or are happy with a limited amount of control over their possessions. (As a side note, I'm happy enough with the amount of control [considering my limits] that I have over most of my possessions.)



The fourth is, again, iffy. Having unlimited domain over one's property necessitates that you can do whatever you want with it. Wouldn't certain actions of property acquisition and usage limit these rights for other people? If I can do whatever I want with my things and I elect to do something which interferes with another's rights, that affected person has been coerced or unfairly driven. There are very few rights that I maintain--in any arena of society--which don't have the capability of infringing on your rights, if I try hard enough. So, should I be allowed to coerce you into accepting actions you wouldn't want to have done? Should someone coerce me into not doing these actions? Here it is, drawn out:

I want to perform A. 1 does not want A to be performed; existing in a system where A has occured is negative coercion to 1. Should I be forced not to perform A? Should 1 be forced to accept A?

No matter what, someone is being coerced by some other force. Society is a balance of power, which involves necessary amounts of coercion. If power is not vested formally, power will establish itself in the form of the person with the most charisma, the biggest guns, or some other arbitrary system of coercion. Because--and this is very very important--resources will always be limited--there will always be competition, and there will always be undue force upon certain people (by other people, by themselves, by government, etc).



The fifth is mostly false. The state does not have a monopoly on protection. The state assists in protection. I have the right to defend myself, my home, my family, my friends, and my possessions. Saying they have a monopoly would indicate that I have no power to protect myself; but I do and everyone else does too.

Who should have a monopoly of judicial services? You're using the phrase "judicial services" to refer to a specific institution created by the state to help it come to conclusions. If you're saying that the government has a monopoly of "judgments", you're wrong. In fact, I judge that statement to be very false. The fact is, the state only has judicial services to help itself appropriately provide protection without being arbitrary. Judicial services are set in place to help further protect you from possible faults in the state's protective actions (which, again, exist to defend you against others who would coerce you).

Now, the government does create the money. How, exactly, does this affect us? The citizens of society are the only ones who can provide that money with equity. Anyone within the society can disavow the worth of that green slip of paper and ignore its presence. Moreover, the government has absolutely no control over the equity of all other items. If anything, issuing cash equity hurts the government by driving inflation.

Most often, your "money" is just a number meant to represent your total equity. It's nothing more than a symbolic value in a book. When you mortgage your house to a bank, they don't give you "money". They give you a big number. This number is a contract between you, the bank, and any outside entity who chooses to believe that the number has credence. If this number was not suitable to you and the bank, it would not have been given as equity.

Money is a number. Society defines its value. The state provides cash equity for money. We accept the cash as equity for the money we have.



I don't believe I missed anything.
