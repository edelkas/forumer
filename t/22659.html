#438138 6 2008-05-21T23:14:09Z under_pressure
The critical flaw was in Johnson's policy of escalation. In a way, it signified that we weren't really entirely committed to our objectives in Vietnam - in past wars, such as World War One and World War Two, the US miraculously mobilized, whereas in Vietnam, it was -too- gradual.

Interestingly enough, we theoretically - theoretically - had a lot at stake; according to the Domino Theory, if South Vietnam fell to communism, we believed that all of southeast Asia would fall to communism as well. It wasn't until the Tet Offensive in 1968 when we realized that we were in fact -losing- the war in Vietnam. North Vietnam launched a massive attack when they were supposed to be licking their wounds, and although the US beat back the invasion with great success as far as numbers go, we finally realized that Vietnam was a political, economic, and military trap.

The war in Vietnam was quite a costly venture, yet during his terms, Nixon failed to raise taxes in order to pay for the massive defense spending required to fund the war, contributing to the stagnant and inflationary economy of the 1970's.

Sadly for Lyndon Johnson, he may well have gone down as one of the greatest presidents ever had it not been for his involvement in Vietnam. His civil rights legislature accomplishments were unlike that of any president since Abraham Lincoln, and his Great Society program could have made great strides in the health care industry.

As a nation, Americans became more self conscious after Vietnam; despite our self-assured military prowess following WWII and booming economy, we began to question our true greatness as a nation after we failed to win a war in a supposedly primitive nation.

Uh, well, to sum it up, yeah, in a lot of ways, Vietnam was a huge loss for America.

I agree with Dave.
Admittedly, the Domino Theory was rather silly, but fears seemed to be confirmed by the fact that South Eastern Asian nations were gradually falling to communism. Obviously, it wasn't actually cause and effect, but our greatest concerns looked as if they were being validated.

To answer your next point, the Cold War was dominated by the concept of a "zero-sum game" - i.e., should America lose in Vietnam, it would mean -total- loss, and therefore, in effect, total victory for the Soviets. If we had won, total victory for us.

Realistically, the threat of communism was negligible, but American paranoia was in part due to Kennan's "containment" philosophy, which American leaders and citizens critically misconstrued. Despite Kennan's protests that he was -not- in fact implying that Soviets were bent on world domination, he was basically ignored; most Americans just went "OH GOD COMMUNISTS". Kennan did actually argue that if America attempted to contain communism, the Soviet economy would fail and collapse within itself. Interestingly enough, he was right, and when the Soviet Union finally collapsed in 1991, he was called before congress and given a standing ovation.
