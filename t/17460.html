To add to what I said above in a reply to Atilla in general - is there, then, a difference between logic and reason? I agree that many Christians try to rationalise their worldview, as do most people, but I still think it is more accurate to say that God is illogical, but can exist within reason.
If God is truly omnipotent, He could just declare Himself logical and nobody could argue.

Anyway, what you have to remember here is that the issue is not whether or not God is logical, or even whether Christians are logical, but whether Christians are, of necessity, opposed to logic and reason. I am stating that this is not the case.

Belief in an entity which is illogical may not itself be illogical. If something is beneficial to you it is logical to do it, right? What if belief in something illogical is beneficial? Furthermore, being illogical, having a logical blind spot, or applying flawed logic does not necessarily mean opposition to logic and reason. To take the most extreme stance, you believe that you stance is the most logically correct, yes? This means that everyone with a difference stance must be ignoring a logical flaw in their own stance, and is therefore illogical. Thus, everyone who disagrees with you must be opposed to logic itself. I think we can agree that this is a rather silly stance to take. Most people take illogical stances because they do not know better (or they are not convinced that their stance is actually illogical), not because they actually reject logic.

We may also consider that classical logic is not the be-all and end-all. For example, the statement "This statement is not true" cannot be assigned a truth value under classical logic. It could be argued that, in a similar manner, it is unreasonable to attempt to apply classical logic to god because that is a situation where logic cannot apply. This is not opposition to logic, but rather placing a constraint on the bounds of logic; and clearly there must be some restraint, because we can easily come up with paradoxes where classical logic fails. Why "classical"? Because it may be possible to resolve these conflicts using paraconsistent logic (1) (2). I'm not terribly well versed in paraconsistent logic, but it is apparently possible to use it as a logical framework even when there are contradictions under classical logic. This may resolve issues with the omnipotence in a logical manner, thus undermining the proposition that god is illogical.

Is there a difference between logic and reason? Sort of. Reason could perhaps be considered somewhat similar to fuzzy logic, in that there is room for things like "maybe", "somewhat", "both" and "neither" instead of just "true" or "false" (er, obviously I'm comparing with classical logic here). The differences are usually most noticeable in places where logic fails, such as the liar paradox or (as you suggested) the existence of god, where we can point out that something is apparently illogical but nevertheless reasonable. I'd say that the primary difference is that reason doesn't quite have the same rigid, rigorous approach as logic. If we compare to physics, we could say that reason is a macroscopic approach, while logic is a microscopic approach.
