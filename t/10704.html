#192372 4 2006-06-21T04:34:00Z Kablizzy
I still make horrible maps. Every single day, I pump out twice as many piles of crap as I do good maps. Your best bet is to go into Ned with a blank screen, like, right now, and just put a handful of E tiles somewhere. Do a cluster here and a cluster there, maybe connect them together. Perhaps you want the map to look symmetrical, perhaps you don't. Then, imagine what kind of enemies might go in there. Try some drones, maybe a rocket launcher. Then look to see if there would be a good place for an exit, and the exit switch, and if not, make a place for it. From there, give it a quick run-through to see how the ninja interacts with the environment that you just created. If something seems to flow well, leave it. If not, then knock out a part of a tile.

From there, add some innate objects. Put in a couple bounceblocks, add a mine or two or three. Put in some gold. Now test it again, see how it works. If you think it works well, then go with it. If not, look at it, and ask yourself, what is wrong with this map?

Through this, I've been making this map that I've described. I'll attach it here for ya.

What I did, is I created the center islands first. I then smoothed them out a bit, and added drones. I didn't have a goal for the map, so I created the outer islands, and ran through. The islands themselves didn't seem to be too playable, so I threw in three bounce blocks. I then added the gold, and the rocket launcher, and added mines to garnish. Put the ninja on the right, exit switch on the left, doors underneath. In fact, I think I'ma submit this badboy to Numa.

And, don't worry, we're working on something that should be done soon, it'll definitely help with things like this.

Anyway, from there you can start fiddling with designs and messing with different clusters and areas to work with. Look at metanet maps and maps from the top authors, and see what kinds of maps they do, don't try to mimic them, but take a part, and make it yourself, in Ned, and then mess with it. mangle it up, and see how you can affect it. Keep doing this until you have something entirely unique and your own.

#192379 5 2006-06-21T05:16:19Z Echarin
I have a few pointers for people just starting to make maps:


Don't feel like you need to use the entire area given to you. Sometimes all you need for a good map is just half the screen, sometimes less.

Simplicity is usually better, so don't overload your maps with lots of different enemies. It might add to the difficulty, but if you don't know what you're doing it'll just become annoying. (you can ignore this for the most part later when know what you're doing)

There are two ways to go about shaping a map: You either add tiles, or you remove tiles. Most beginners only use the former. Try making a map by first loading the entire map with filled tiles and then cutting away tiles to.

Object placement, in my case, was down to how effective the enemies would be at doing their job (i.e. killing N). In the thwump part, I made sure that you could get the door switch, but then I tested people's greed by placing gold down there, so it would be more of a close call getting out of there. #192802 21 2006-06-22T03:17:13Z Kablizzy
That's a pretty awesome map for a first try, TK. Just a couple notes:

* I think the map could have been done better in a smaller space.

* It seemed as though you had too many pent-up ideas working at once.

* The aesthethics were a bit off.

But all of those are normal for mapmakers in your position, so don't sweat it.

However, a couple things to keep in mind. First of all is the idea of standardization. When you have a plethora of objects (In this case, I think you used one of every object), it's harder to grab the player's attention.

The second idea that you may want to look into is minimalization. When you include less objects, and less object types, and try to accomplish similar things with less objects (For instance, if you have fifteen one-way platforms, and you realize that the player can bypass four of them entirely, then you should remove those four), your player can identify more easily with the map.

The third thing that you may want to focus on is theme. Try themes. Try making maps with just laser drones and mines, or just thwumps and floor guards. This helps the player better understand that you were trying to grab their attention with something fresh, and unique, and planned.

So, I took the liberty to edit your map a bit, and add and subtract some things. I'm not sure how it turned out, but it touches on a couple of things.

First, theme. I standardized the layout of the mines and the gold. This provides a consistent theme throughout the map. I also lessened the space that the map took up, and moved a couple of the enemies around.

Finally, I rounded out some of the corners with 1-tiles, just for aesthetics.

So, anyway, not to say that this is better, per se, but it addresses a couple of the most-commonly overlooked things in maps.

Edit: And yes, I do realize that the level is unbeatable now. I was simply illustrating a point.

#193201 41 2006-06-22T21:12:05Z Hellheart
Long post

The advice given here is quite good, really. It's the advice I followed at first, and followed for quite awhile after, and I follow a variation of it now.

The problem is that, somewhere in the middle, I realized that the one thing my maps really lacked was a natural flow. I'm talking about how some maps, even non-race maps, just seem to *fit*, where the ninja flies in the direction he should and the movement is fluid and dynamic. There's been many variations on this, including a "rhythm" system I've used where the movement isn't so fluid, but the movements have a timing that kind of pulses...

This kind of thing doesn't happen easily. In fact, if you've been placing tilesets and then making levels, it's likely not to happen at all.

I stopped doing that for a while and focused on nothing other than flow and fluidity. I didn't care how my levels looked, or how they rated, I started experimenting and seeing how I could make the ninja's movements natural. I started with race maps with lots of slopes and loops...easy stuff. I started adding in other objects, jumppads, having N change directions and do other things. This is where I began to understand how strong the subtle influences of slopes really affect how a map *feels*.

I then worked to carry the same fluid feeling into a more open space with more choice, using more objects and less tiles. I'm still working with this - some makers have it down really well. But now that I understand how correct tile placement will significantly increase the quality of a map (and, consequently, your ratings), I scrap a ton of my maps and keep the interesting objects for combination and juxtaposition, or make tilesets with flow in mind.

If I want to make a map that truly flows well, I use the following format:

1) Decide what my level is going to be. Action or Puzzle? How fast do I want it to move? What enemies will it have or not have? Will it be enclosed or not?

2) Choose a spot for the Ninja. This is optional, but it totally changes the way I shape a map. I usually place him on the bottom if I'm doing a more enclosed map, and the middle or top for a more open map.

3) Create an initial set of tiles and objects in a small area around the Ninja. The idea here is to make something nice to build the rest of your level around. I playtest this while I make it, and if it's not good enough I start over.

4) Once I'm happy with the start, I make another small set of tiles and objects in a direction I want the Ninja to go. I focus on fluidity here - if the initial set had N launching off a slope, I want him landing in a spot where that momentum is going to be useful. I'm not trying to build a map around a tileset; I'm trying to build a tileset around a map
a) I playtest the entire map while I do this, to gauge difficulty and cohesion. There are exceptions, such as sequences late in a map, but I don't post a map anymore unless I have what is virtually a completion demo.

5) I continue until my playthroughs reach a point of difficulty I prefer. This is one of the biggest advantages - I will know exactly how large my map should be.

6) I clean things up and make the map look as pretty as possible, playtest it more, and compare it to the better maps I've made to see if it's worth posting. I tend to play these maps a lot even if I don't post them

This takes longer. Often it takes a *lot* longer. But you'd be surprised at how much better your map will play when you use this method, unless you're a natural at flow. It's an acquired skill, and after I made like 10 of these, I found I could make pretty tilesets into maps like these. But you have to understand the dynamics before you can do that.
