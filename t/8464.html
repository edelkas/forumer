- This thread has to do with something a little more specific - the existence of free will and simultaneous existence of am omnipotent being. That's quite different from the existence of free will (which I believe cannot be determined at the moment).

For those interested, this will also be posted on my religious discussion forum. I may bring up things people said on that forum.

A brief introduction:

All of reality is nothing more than one gigantic chemical reaction that follows the natural laws of the universe throughout its duration. Because any state of the universe can be predicted theoretically by observing the energy states, positions, etc., of the contents of the universe, this means that in order for there to be an unpredictable event (and therefore a non-binding event; an event that is not forced into happening because that is the outcome of the chemical reactions involved), then there must exist a random event (a random event is defined as one that is not predictable) to steer it off of that predictable course.

So, without further ado:

Free will is entirely contingent upon the existence of random events.

Now, either (A) random events exist, or (B) random events don't exist. (A) is a positive assertion, and (B) is the only other option available in a boolean phrase (true or false) such as this one, which means that (A) and (B) are all-inclusive; it's either (A) or (B). If it's not (A) then it has to be (B), and if it's not (B) then it has to be (A). (C ) is right out. (A) and (B) shall be the letters to be considered, and none else. They are the only two options because they are the only two existing options.

(A) The existence of random events means that there exist events which cannot be predicted. It follows, then, that if there exist events which cannot be predicted, then free will has a chance of existing.
However, (B) the non-existence of random events means that there is no such thing as an unpredictable event. This means that the universe follows along one, theoretically predictable path. This would mean, if you haven't guessed yet, that free will does not exist.

Taking an atheistic stance on this, it is up to you to look into the argument scientists are having over which is true, (A) or (B).

Taking a stance that believes in an omnipotent deity, on the other hand, eliminates one of (A) and (B) from consideration, as you will soon see.
An omnipotent deity is an omniscient deity. An omniscient deity knows everything. This means that the omnipotent deity knows the future! It knows everything that will happen to you and knows every decision and thought that you make. In other words, the omnipotent deity can predict all events (let that soak in for a little bit).
Hey, wait a second! If something can predict all events, then all events are predictable (because something can predict them), which debunks (A) the idea that random (unpredictable) events exist. (A) is debunked because (A) says unpredictable events exist, but we have (hopefully) seen that something (an omnipotent deity) can predict all events, meaning that those unpredictable events can't exist. Someone who believes (A) that unpredictable events exist cannot believe that an omnipotent deity exists, as that omnipotent deity would be able to predict those events.
That means that if you believe in an omnipotent being that you automatically side with (B). If you do not side with (B), then you are arguing against your god's omnipotence.
(B) states that random events don't exist, and therefore neither does free will (because all events are predictable - we've gone over this, remember?).
Quod erat demonstrandum, it is irrational for someone to believe in both an omnipotent being and free will.

If you do this a thousand times, it still doesn't mean there was no possibilities other then the predicted one. On a different tack, consider quantum particles. You can measure the position of the particle. You may even be able to explain why it should have such a position. We can consider a choice in the same way - there is a chance an individual may make many different choices, and once the choice has been made, you can say that it happened because <InsertReasonHere>, but this does not prove that there wasn't really a range of possible outcomes in the first place.
