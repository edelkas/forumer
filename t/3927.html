Black holes weren't something we observed, or guessed from observations. They were predicted as possible by general relativity. The theory suggests that it's possible to have an area where spacetime is infinitely curved, and so the gravity is inifinite. Now, we have strong evidence for them existing, like x-rays from the galactic core.

AtomicShadow: There's no "stuff" in there to bump into, the gravity is stronger than the force that holds particles apart, so all of the matter is destroyed. You get ripped apart because of the gravity gradient, with stronger gravity nearer to the black hole. There's enough of a difference between, say, your head and your feet that your feet would get pulled off.

The event horizon is the radius at which the speed you'd need to escape is the same as the speed of light. Beyond that point, nothing can escape. The reason something falling into a black hole appears to slow down is that light takes longer to reach the observer. If it takes ten more minutes for light to get out, it looks like it took the person ten minutes to fall that far.

Quote: blue_tetris, Jul 5 2005, 10:51 PM
Increasing the density of something does not provide it with more gravity. Its mass will remain constant, and therefore, its gravitation will remain constant.
While this is true, you've overlooked something. The strength of a gravitational field is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the object which creates it (for all those who aren't good at maths, that means gravity gets MUCH weaker over distance). The radius of Earth is about 6000 km. The radius of a pea is about 1 mm, which is 6000 000 000 times less. This means at the surface of our pea-sized planet the pull of gravity is 3.6 * 10^19 times stronger (that's 36 000 000 000 000 000 000 times). Trust me, it's a black hole.

So why isn't there a black hole at the centre of Earth? Because when you are inside an object, the pull of the matter on one side (partially, unless you're at the centre of gravity) cancels the pull of the other side.

And to all those who say the flow of time is absolute, you're wrong. The relativity of time has been experimentally verified, and if we didn't take it into acount many modern devices wouldn't work. A GPS, for example, requires precise syncronisation between an object on Earth and a satellite with a relative velocity of several km/s. The clock on the satellite must therefore be constantly adjusted to account for the relativistic effects.

So there.

In addition (on the nature of time and our perception of it):

Time is merely the interval between events, in the same way that distance is the interval between objects. This interval may be different for every frame of reference, just as the distance between Australia and the USA is different to that between Austalia and Europe. Events still occur in a logical order (i.e. no causes before effects - at least, in our universe, and as far as we know), but events may occur at a different rate for each observer, and events that are simultaneous to one observer may be asyncronous to others. Time is just another dimesion, although possibly twisted at a weird angle to the rest, so to speak (for those who don't know, space and time are both distorted in various ways, including by gravity and relativistic effects caused by a difference in velocity between observers. In fact, they are usually unified and referred to as 'spacetime' by physicists).

And moving on to psychology... Psychological studies have shown that young people experience time as though it were flowing more slowly than our elders, because each second is longer compared to our accumulated experiences. For example, when you're five, ten years is twice as long as you've lived for, and seems like ages. For a forty-year-old, it's just one-quarter of your life, and qualifies as the near future. This doesn't make creatures that have lived longer more sluggish - it is merely their perception that changes, not any physical property. You could say the world seems to move faster for them, and as part of that world, they naturally keep pace. It's just that the earlier parts of their life now seem to have gone so very slow by comparison.
