Now tell me, why the heck would you want to subtract the box's position from the box's halfwidth???

The vector you're looking for is a unit vector, that means its length is 1.
For an AABB(Axis Aligned Bounding Box) the facing vector is (1, 0).

#73255 5 2005-06-08T16:47:22Z Guest
about triangle collision, it looks like you're doing box collision, since you're looking at penetration along x and y.

if you read the tutorials, you'll see that the penetration directions you need to test are the directions perpendicular to the surface of the shapes -- in the case of triangle-vs-axis-aligned-box, you have to test x and y (which are the directions perp. to the box), and THEN test three other axes -- the directions perpendicular to the edges of the triangle.

i'm not sure i totally understand your code -- you ned to represent your triangle using 3 points (well, there are a lot of ways to go about representing a triangle, but that's an easy one).

if you look in the source code for either tutorial, you'll see box-vs-triangle code, although it's not the general case -- the triangles in N always have two axis-aligned edges, so we only need to test three axes in total (x, y, and the "diagonal" edge of the triangle) instead of the 5 for the general case (x and y for the box, plus three other directions for the triangle edges)

Now, firstly, you should use Math.pow() only if necessary. Instead you could just do:
Math.sqrt(ax * ax + ay * ay);
Even more, you don't even need to calculate a square root. Since you know that the vector (axis_x, axis_y) has to have a length 1, then your square root is already calculated for you:

axis_x = Math.SQRT2 / 2;
axis_y = Math.SQRT2 / 2;

since a 45 degree line with a length 1 has x and y of 0.7071.... and the square root of 2 is 1.414.... then you just have to divide by two and there you have it: no function calls! And no function calls means more speed.
Nevermind, with that little optimisation, we can carry on...

#87479 11 2005-08-01T14:41:24Z Guest
ah.. in this case, i think i know what might be happening.

the simplest thing is: you have to project the delta vector onto the axis. right now you're just considering the distance between the two centers, but you need to consider the distance between the two centers when measured along the axis you're testing.

secondly, you're correctly measuring the projected size of each object, but you might be _incorrectly_ measuring the delta vector, because you're not using a point in the middle of each object.

for objA, you're fine -- the center of the box is the center no matter whatever direction you project it onto. but, in the case of the triangle, are you _sure_ the point you're using as the center is in the middle of the projection onto the triangle's axis?

i'm not sure if this is a real mathematical rule, but as far as i've tried it's not possible to pick a single point inside a triangle which is in the middle of that triangle's projetion onto all three of its potentially seperating axes.

the simplest example is the right-triangle tiles used in N: if you put the "center" point (used to calculate the delta) in the middle of the tile along x and y, it's much too close to the hypotenuse and is thus not in the middle of the triangle when measured along the third (non x or y) axis.

in N (and in the tutorial source) we didn't actually follow the tutorials word for word, because they're meant to explain the idea. it's actually simpler/faster to simply use a point ON the hypotenuse as the "center", and change the math a bit (the triangle's projected halfwidth becomes 0).

then you just get the size of the other shape projected onto the hypotenuse's normal, and see if it's larger than the projected size ofthe delta vector form the other shape's center to the hypotenuse.

either of the corner points of the hypotenuse work well as "points on the hypotenuse" so it's pretty simple..

if you read through the source for the 45deg triangles, it should be even easier to understand what i'm trying to say here
