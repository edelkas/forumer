Quote: Twistkill, 2007-09-05T22:47:08Z
Part of the reason for extreme heat is because of all the smog covering up the atmosphere and created the greenhouse effect.
While there are areas where the current temperature increase (about 0.7 C) makes a difference (South Africa, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet, the edges of Andean and Alpine glaciers), the Sacramento River valley has been plain old hot for centuries. Now, in a city, the excess heat generated by power consumption comes into play, but not so much in a small town.
However, with a recent near-doubling of population in the valley, power consumption has increased dramatically. Air conditioners use a massive amount of energy, and Carnot's theorem implies that they always will. "Solutions" might include better insulation-aerogel seems promising, but it's not manufactured in large quantities yet, and solar panels, which can't power a house, but can offset some of the energy consumption.
The word "solution" is in quotation marks because it's not a true solution-it's not more energy, it's a way to use what's available.

Quote: 321southpaw, September 06, 2007 09:19 pm

I read something in the back of The Sun magazine yesterday in the Sunbeams section (and I'm also paraphrasing):

Solar power will never be considered by power companies because nobody owns the sun.


You're aware that The Sun is a tabloid, right?
Anyway, solar power can't be considered by power companies, because with a 20% efficient (and that's considered good) solar panel, it would take approximately 100 square miles of solar panels to power a small town. Now, there's plenty of room in America to get far, far more than we need, but definitely not in densely populated areas like the New York-Philadelphia region or the Sacramento river valley. Solar panels can be considered a supplement to the power of a single-family home, but unless massive efficiency gains are realized (not so much in the panels as in the home appliances the panels run), solar panels on the roof can't power a house alone.
So they're a supplement. And no, it's not run by a power company, because it is free and requires very little maintenance. However, solar panels *are* available for purchase to the general public.
Using a generous limit from Wikipedia, let's say we obtain 40 W/m. If your house is 100 feet by 100 feet, that translates to 30mx30m, or 900 m. Our total electric wattage produced is therefore 36kW for eight hours a day (again, a generous number), so 288 kWh/day.
Now, an average home consumes somewhere around 4MJ/m-day (actually, it's somewhat higher in the US. Energy consumption in Sweden, however, averages less than half this number), or 1.11kWh/m-day, so our 900 square meter house consumes about 1000 kWh/day (isn't that a convenient number). Even in Sweden, the average consumption per day is around 400 kWh/day for a house of this size, and that assumes it's one story. (More importantly, in Sweden, the sunlight is at a very indirect angle meaning that even less energy is available to the unfortunate homeowner, not to mention the 4 hour days in the winter).
In short, no, you can't power a house like that, unless we, say, outdo Sweden. Hey, it's just Sweden, right? We can do better than that.

California does use hydroelectric power. A big chunk of Southern California's energy comes from the Hoover Dam on the Colorado River, and there are smaller hydroelectric plants all over the northern half of the state.

California also uses solar power. When I drive up to Central and Northern California, I pass by a field of solar panels so big, it looks like a lake out in the desert. The Mojave Desert is getting a few more big solar plants soon, and after they're finished, we'll be getting more power from them than from the Hoover Dam.

California also uses wind power already, though probably not nearly as much as hydroelectric or solar. You can see a map of the state's power plants here. It doesn't show how big any given station is, so you won't see any difference between a 100 kW station and a 2000 MW one, but it still gives an idea of the energy breakdown.
