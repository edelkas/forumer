I've been pondering this for a while.
This book, apart from simply being a good book, brings up a similar topic.


What is identity? What makes us who we are? How can we be distinguished from any other person, or even any other object? What makes an object what it is?

Surely one might think, as I did after listening to the above series, that what makes an individual being what they are is really their memory. If one loses their memory, all internal sense of identity is gone.

However, if a perfect clone of a person were made, and with a different memory and thus a different internal sense of identity, we might argue that it is not the "same" person, though others might perceive them as being "the same". Similarly, if a completely "different" person was given the exact same memory, we would also say that it is not the "same" person, though that person would internally have the same sense of identity.

Organic objects (and other things as well) are constantly changing. Humans, for instance, are constantly growing new skin cells and sloughing off old ones. Now imagine some sort of object which gradually had all of its comprising particles replaced with similar ones. In our minds, it would still be the "same" object. But, in fact, it would have none of the exact same atoms, molecules, or other types of particles. Where does our sense of "sameness" come from? What makes objects unique, and how do they maintain an identity?

Or is all "identity" and "uniqueness" just something humans have fabricated to explain the universe as we see it?


Discuss.

#393025 11 2007-12-24T20:03:37Z blue_tetris
I think JT is talking about ontological identity. In an absolute sense, there is no single thing which constitutes "tea cup". The particles of the tea cup don't have anything special about them which might cause them to be paired in such a fashion that they constitute one definite unit. Of course, the paint on the tea cup has identity, also. And the individual atoms in the tea cup each possess identity.

Identity is rendered by human labeling. I label a certian object to be "my keyboard", which endows an abstract subset of reality with being and wholeness.

Identity is a function of the human mind, and it allows for grouping and easy management of real data. The particles that make up a human and all its organs (including its brain) possess the identity of personhood through subjective analysis only.

In short, identity does not exist without the mind.

#393188 14 2007-12-25T06:30:38Z epigone
For more information about change and identity, see the Ship of Theseus paradox. For those of you who are too lazy to read the page, I'll summarize.

So, a guy builds a boat. He calls his boat the Theseus. It's a fine boat; such a fine boat that he wants to keep it in tip-top shape. As the years progress, he replaces the boards of the ship, one by one, and stores the old boards in a shed by the sea. Eventually he replaces every board. Some other chap comes along and notices what a fine ship this man has, and he decides to take the old boards and reconstruct the ship from them. Assume that there is no wear and tear and what-not, and the re-built ship is essentially identical to the ship with the new wood. Which ship can rightly be called the original Theseus? The one whose parts have been replaced over time, or the one which is comprised of the original parts?



As a corrolary, consider that the human body replaces millions of cells over the course of its life. Are we the same person that we were 10 years ago? We certainly look different, and we have millions of new cells that comprise us. But we are the same, or at least we'd like to believe. Then are we more than our bodies? Are we our minds? Then of course you have to look into the mind-body problem. Lots of dense philosophical stuff here, none of which has ever been satisfactorily answered.

@Epigone: The ship which has had the parts replaced is the Theseus. It's a matter of continuity. When two or three boards are replaced, the ship is certainly still the Theseus. The new boards become part of the Theseus; the ship has been repaired rather than replaces.

Then we take the new Theseus, and replace a couple more boards. The same reasoning applies; the new boards become part of the Theseus. And so on, for each replacement over the years. At all times, the ship is still the Theseus.

Quote: blue_tetris, December 25, 2007 06:18 pm
It's not insane to think that I've stolen that guy's body.
Isn't the regeneration of the brain too slow and precise to have an effect on identity in this way?

What if we consider our brain as a crossword puzzle?
The black areas represent unexplored or unreachable areas of our brain and the white squares the current potential of our cognitive system. When an experience of a certain quality occurs, the possibility exists for the white squares to bridge the black areas and unlock further potential.

Now if we consider the words laid out in the crossword the current focus of our cognitive system, as the potential grows and we discover more words, previous words can be either grayed or blanked letting the focus of our mind shift to the newly discovered areas. If we consider a blank white square an atom or brain cell (or whatever fits), if one of these die and effectively black out a letter, as long as enough of the word remains to be decipherable, as the brain regenerates the dead area the word will remain.

If this healthy regeneration remains constant, then while a complete shift (in that none of the words occupy any of the original white spaces) could be conceived externally as a new mind in a stolen body, the aggregate nature of the base puzzle would confirm the links to your former self (bar the application of physical damage) as unbreakable.
