#269968 12 2006-12-14T11:02:56Z iangb
I know the idea - 'tis a flawed paradox. Highlight my post for the flaw, if you don't want to try working it out on your own.

Imagine the path of the fly, in terms of it's speed relative to the ground.

It starts off flying to the left, then splats onto the train and starts moving to the right. Obviously it doesn't start to slow down until it is in contact with the train. So (goes the argument), at some point, the fly that is touching the train is (for an instantanious length of time) stationary relative to the ground. But since it is touching the train at that time this must mean that the train is stationary relative to the fly, and therefore also stationary relative to the ground. For that instant then, the train is stationary.

Answer:
It only works because it treats a fly as an incompressable object.
If, however, you treat the fly as a compressable object which is compressing (read: splatting) all the while that it is de/reaccelerating to the speed of the train, then there isn't a problem. The train isn't stationary relative to the fly until the fly has stopped it's rapid change in velocity, at which point it is going at the speed of the train anyway.

Ok, here's a problem with alot of your answers: The fly never "touches" the train-in quantum mechanics, I don't recall any mention of anything "touching".
Rather, the electrons in the fly repel the electrons on the front of the train. Even if the fly were incompressible, it wouldn't matter. He's not in an internal frame of reference, so the idea that his velocity is the same as that of the ground doesn't work-the train is still moving towards him, and is not stopped.
However, the fly doesn't have to stop the train-it already is. Since each inertial frame of reference is as good as any other, the train is already "stopped" in the 100 mph ground velocity frame of reference. The only time something can't be considered "stopped" is while it's accelerating. So, yes, the fly "stops" the already "stopped" train.
