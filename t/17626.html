It just kinda stayed the same. And the gold patterns staying similar through the pack kinda turned me off a bit.

Secondly, you guys had some AMAZING ideas here. Some of the tilesets absolutely freakin' blew me away, and some of the map names were unbelievably creative. I loved the idea behind this, and if it were executed better, this might be among my favourite packs.

But here's the bad part, guys. The organization was atrocious. It was really, really terrible. The pack itself comes in a migraine-inducing clump that screws up the entire pack. When you load the pack into userlevels, it bugs half of the maps, and the pack name doesn't even appear on all of the maps. Some of the maps have NaN errors, some include the ninja being automatically killed, and some have the ninja outside of the map area. The mapper's names are scattered about, some of the maps are labeled as action, some Action, some as the author name, and some maps don't appear at all. Now, when I say organization, I don't just mean the formatting. I mean playtesting. I think that in order to put a quality pack out, you've got to do some form of going through the maps and saying, "hey, I didn't quite like this part, maybe you should do this with the map," and getting everyone's feedback from there. I mean, the reason it takes Legacy 9 months to pump a pack out isn't because we're mapping the whole time - We're tweaking the whole time. All of this bad stuff that I mentioned would've been caught with a little extra time spent on the after-thought.

But more importantly, on the organization front, most of the maps are... not so much boring... as they are just kinda unimpressive. Quite a few of them come across as forgettable. As I said above, the ideas are absolutely amazing, the themes are fantastic, but there seems to be something to each map that didn't feel finished. They all felt like they started out with the right idea in mind, and then veered off when the sixteen chaser drones and 48 mines were put in. It's one of those things where I can see the tileset, and I know where the mapper wanted to take the map, but then I look at the objects, and turn my head to the side and go, "Why did he do that with the map?" You guys had the right idea, for sure, but it didn't quite seem to make it to the finish line.

I'm going to do an article soon called "Analysing the MetaMap", because there are a handful of things predominant in most of them;

Patterns - Metanet Utilizes a lot of patterns. Pattern recognition is absolutely huge in aesthetics, and is a big part of what is and is not appealing to the eye. Now, having one or two things that shoot off from this isn't a bad thing, but the only maps from this pack that I remember are the ones that included patterns.

Simplicity - Minimalism makes packs. Long maps don't belong in map packs. Packs, more often than not, are meant to be played as a whole, rather than just a conglomeration of maps. If it were just a smattering of different maps, you'd be better suited to submit them to NUMA as a series.

Physics - One of the lesser-thought themes in a Metanet map is the idea of Physics that they use. They use a ton of jumps, but not in the same way as NUMA authors do. Metanet uses downward jumps, making landings difficult to stick. They do this in a few other ways, too, but one of the most predominant things that their maps accomplish is, basically, to showcase their physics engine!

Space - I still haven't mastered space management, but Metanet does it beautifully. Their tilesets draw the eye to the action, and away from the empty space - In fact, in most of their maps, you don't notice empty space. This is perhaps the trickiest thing to get the hang of once you become an established mapper, but it's perhaps the most rewarding to get a good hold of, and most NUMA mappers (I'd say a vast majority, even) don't even have the concept at all.

I do want to retract that point l_d. It's not that long maps don't have a place in map packs - They do. They really do. What I was getting at was that long maps need to be mixed well with shorter, simpler maps, but the type of NUMA longmap that we've come to grow used to is strictly outside of a pack setting. Which is entirely what this pack here was. And more complex maps absolutely ruin flavour and kill theme - The maps need to be unique and memorable, and when you come across the sixteenth map with eight switches, all hidden under bounceblocks, all guarded by 8 chainguns and seeker drones, smattered with mines, and on and on and on and on and on, it gets so repetitious. You have to strike a good balance between short and long maps, simplistic and complex ones, and so on.

In either case, take Instructables (Which everyone needs to read) that Mare and Raigan put up this morning. I'll quote a couple parts thereof here in a bit. But the point is that simple maps don't need to be short, and don't need to blow by. And good simple maps don't. Simplicity in a map doesn't dictate anything about the map's gameplay - It just takes skill as an author to utilize the simplicity in the right ways. Minimalism and unique execution are keys in what makes a good episodic map. If you make long maps, you're not necessarily a bad author, you're just a NUMA author. You don't do packs as well, because you can't grasp the idea of minimalism and simplicity. Look at every Mare and Raigan level - They do minimalism better than anyone. So, confusing difficulty and distance with aesthetic principles is where I think we're going wrong here. Simple maps have a perfect place in packs, particularly episodic packs, because the pack is meant to be played as a whole, rather than as 124 really long, grueling maps. That's why Alexi's portion stood head and shoulders above the rest in this pack, the stuff he did was unique, and simplistic. It embraced minimalism, and it took simplicity to an exciting level. However, somewhere in the middle, too many objects and too much of the same idea got involved, and that ruined most of the maps. That's what I mean by minimalism - Each of Alexi's maps tried to do too much, and that ruined it. So anyway, on to the quotes.

Quote
Remember to focus on minimalism: too many entities or too many types of entity gets distracting fast. Limit yourself, as this will give your level the most punch. You can't go wrong with more punch.

Quote
A good N level should be easy on the eyes -- embrace minimalism. Good N levels incorporate balance in terms of positive and negative space (as created by the tiles and lack thereof), position and number of objects and tiles, and symmetry.

Quote
Because the design of the tiles is so strong, the objects have been placed in an orderly and minimal fashion, so as to keep the focus on the tiles, and not distract the eye.

Quote
Repetition of pattern and shape throughout a level will give it a clean and consistent look.

Quote
Although there are several subtle changes to the pattern, the repetition of strongly contrasting shapes in this map disguises differences, giving the level a clean and overall consistent look.

Quote
Note the symmetry in spacing of these columns, the consistency in doorway placement, and the general balance of the design.

Quote
Juxtaposition of several styles can quickly clutter up a design.

Quote
Note how the thwumps were positioned according to a pattern.

Quote
This map contains more object and enemy types than we've seen so far, and yet it manages to remain visually uncluttered due to its use of vast untiled space and simple, concise tiles.

Quote
Once you've got the basic level down, try imagining the gameplay flow. In this case, the player is pretty free to move around. Now unless you already have done, you'll need to decide whether you want to restrict/direct the player's movement, and if so, whether to do that with tiles, objects, or both.

Quote
Start with the ninja and the exit, and then add some enemies and objects. Every object should be added with a purpose in mind. Never add objects just to fill up space -- this leads to maps that look and feel thrown together.

Quote
The second level suffers from too many objects disease. Each bounceblock and one-way platform is pretty much entirely superfluous, and so they can be removed without loss of gameplay quality. Though the placement of objects is fairly consistent here, they still detract from the clarity of the level's design.

Anyway, I'll let you guys read the rest before I just quote the whole thing. In either case, are those not exactly the things I spoke about just yesterday? They certainly are. Consistently, patterns, flow, themes, utilizing space, physics, and simplicity. That's how you make a MetaMap. And I don't mean to detract from what you're saying l_d, but I think it's a pretty standard bottom-line that Minimalism and simplicity are cornerstones of design for packs. The reason you don't notice it in good maps is because those maps don't blow by - Take Zag and Brambles and Shambles from the first Legacy pack. Both are very simplistic levels at heart, but neither "blow by," and both are lengthy and memorable. A strictly short map can be good, too, but short maps are more often simple than long maps, and simplistic maps can either be short or long, difficult or easy. It's the execution that dictates all that. But long maps can make a pack just as easily - so long as those maps are executed well. And from what I've seen from past packs, long maps are more often suited for NUMA, since most people map for packs like they would map normally - they don't change anything when making a map pack. And those are the types of long maps that kill a pack. So, long maps do have their place in a pack, but the type of long maps that most starting authors put in to packs are those that are suited for NUMA - I make a distinction between a NUMA map and a pack map - Because there is certainly a large difference between the two.

They certainly don't have to be in the Metanet style, you're right. And if I said that above, then I misused the term 'style.' MetaMap packs are episodic packs, and the reason you put out a pack of levels is to mimic the episodic format, and not so much to mimic the Metanet Style. If that isn't your goal, then you've missed the idea of map packs altogether. Sendy's maps may have been amazing (In fact, all of her maps are amazing), but here's the thing - She didn't sacrifice an ounce of style to make her maps fit into an episodic setting. Her episodes worked unbelievably well in the episodic setting because she understood the principles behind why you put out an episodic pack (And Hell, if she didn't, she sure did a Hell of a job of bluffing it). She made her levels more minimalistic, simplistic, and worked with the themes of the game, the timer, the physics, and spacial differences. And this isn't a stylistic thing, it's something that has to change for your maps to fit into episodes. I'm not even entirely sure why, but episodes just need this cohesive feel to them in order to feel like an episodic pack.

Now, don't get me wrong, people release levelpacks every day that go against this very idea. Non-episodic packs are all over, and they're great packs, but they're not MetaMap packs. They're not meant to be Episodic packs, they're meant to be a collection of NUMA maps. And style has nothing to do with the thought that they're not episodic, it's the thought that each map was a separate entity from each other map - No thought is given to organization, no thought is given to where levels go in the pack, no thought is given to the timer, or to the aesthetics of the episode, or any of that. Again, it's just a bunch of NUMA maps. Sendy did a fantastic job of incorporating Minimalism into her pack, particularly in maps like Honeycomb Chambers and Gnarley Grotto - All of the flavour is still full there, but the maps are simplistic, and to what end? To make the pack Episodic. And because of that, they fall into the category of MetaMaps, and not due to her style, but due to the theme of the pack - The theme of episodes. If you look at Sendy's NUMA maps, and then at her columns, you'll see a drastic difference in execution, but almost zero difference in style. But even here, her style contrasts quite a bit with Metanet's style. Sendy puts bounce blocks in out-of-the-way locations, makes longer-type maps, and decorates tiles like none other. All of which go against Metanet's style, but don't necessarily go against the episodic ideal. Which is why her pack works. She didn't make single maps, she made episodic maps, and in her style. And that's the key point to what I was saying above - It's not at all about Metanet's mapping style, it's about the theme of what episodes are made for.

So, if your pack isn't a MetaMap collection, it's a NUMA-level collection. If the pack is the former, then the idea is to mimic the Episodic format, right? If the pack is the latter, then it's just a collection of NUMA-levels in a text file. Sendy went that extra step to make sure the levels were flavourful enough to play through as a whole, instead of just a few maps of increasing difficulty. Which is where the "rift" comes in. And, if you're just submitting a bunch of NUMA maps in a text file, why not just go the extra mile and submit them as a series on NUMA? Because that's all it really is. If you're releasing a pack, you have to have at least some thought to cohesion and conglomeration, or the pack ends up falling short.

In either case, as you said, I think it rather comes down to a difference in style and a difference of what we each like, rather than a problem with the mapping themes. Because they're two separate areas of thought. Episodic Maps vs. NUMA Maps is where I'm coming from, where I think you're thinking more along the lines of a difference of styles.
