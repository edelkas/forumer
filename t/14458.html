@spiker: Your dad is largely incorrect.

Quote: Spiker, 2006-12-14T03:00:39Z
-When two cars are traveling at each other at 40 mph, my dad said it looks like they are moving apart at about 80 mph. (From the viewpoint of an outside observer)

That's... quite poorly phrased.

Say that I am standing on the median strip of a freeway. Two cars pass me, travelling in opposite directions. Both are doing 100 km/h relative to me. Relative to one of the cars, the other car is moving at 200 km/h (just add the two speeds together: 100+100=200), and I am moving at 100 km/h (also add the two speeds: 0+100=100). Are you with me so far?

Quote: Spiker, 2006-12-14T03:00:39Z
-When two spacecraft travel past each other at 60% the speed of light, the appear to move away at 60% the speed of light.

However, the situation with the cars only works for speeds considerably less than the speed of light. If two spaceships travel in opposite directions travel at 60% of the speed of light relative to me, one will not be travelling at 120% of the speed of light relative to the other; instead, you have use a complicated mathematical formula to work out the relative speeds, which will end up being a little below the speed of light (but still greater than 60% of the speed of light). It's counter-intuitive, yes, but that's the way the world works.

Quote: Spiker, 2006-12-14T03:00:39Z
He did tell me that if you travel at the speed of light with a mirror in front of you, you will not see your reflection. This was much easier to grasp.

It's also totally incorrect. Ignoring for the moment that you can't travel at the speed of light, the speed of light is the same for all observers, regardless of their motion relative to one another. No matter how fast you are going, light will still appear to move at a set speed relative to you. Also, in your own frame of reference, you are always stationary (you can't really be moving relative to yourself, can you?), and since you are carrying the mirror it is also stationary relative to you. You would therefore be able to see your own image in the mirror.

Another thing to note is that for a static observer (using your spot of ground on the Earth as a frame of reference), looking at a car moving at 100 km/h passing a car moving at 80 km/h, you see that the car in front is moving at 100 km/h. But from the perspective of the car in the back, the car in front of it is moving at 20 km/h, and you're whizzing behind it at 80 km/h.

What happens when we remove the Earth as a reference point?
A spaceship cannot determine its own speed. If you're on a spaceship and you're looking out of a window, and another spaceship shoots by you, you have no way of telling whether the ship is whizzing by you while you're motionless, whether you're shooting by a motionless spaceship, or whether the both of you are shooting past each other.

The stomach is an excellent accelerometer, but a very poor speedometer.
#269997 10 2006-12-14T14:33:30Z blue_tetris
The concept of rate (and motion) isn't immediately numerically explicable. The easiest way to explain it is:

As an object approaches the speed of light (even through relativistic relationships), the speed of light itself becomes "further away". For instance, if you were traveling at 270,000 km/s, you could exert the energy it would take to move 10 km/s faster, however this much energy would only allow you to move 1 km/s faster.

The closer you get to the speed of light, the more unattainable the speed of light becomes. The speed of light, itself, essentially repositions its value to be infinite, that is--no matter how much energy you apply to an object, you'd have to apply "just a little bit more" to reach the speed of light. And that "just a little bit more" (whether it's energy/distance/time/whatever) gets larger (and approaches infinity) as the rate of an object approaches its ultimate limit: the speed of light.
